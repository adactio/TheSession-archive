The Hobnail Boots That My Father Wore

I learned this tune from my friend Barry McDonald, who himself learned it from Charlie Batchelor, an old bush fiddler from rural Bingara, New South Wales, Australia, who was born in 1897. Barry was one of the Australian ethnomusicologists seeking these old musicians to record and preserve their music in the 1970s and 80s, along with collaborators like Chris Sullivan, Mark Rummery and others. Barry, Chris, Mark and others played with Charlie in his Horton River Band in the early 1980s.

(Some more info about Charlie Batchelor, written by the late Chris Sullivan, can be found at https://www.mustrad.org.uk/articles/charlie.htm)

Barry explained to me that this tune is like a polka version of the Irish Washerwoman, and that outside of the title, it bears no relation to the popular song of the same name (though I believe it was originally recorded as “The Hobnailed Boots That Farver Wore”) written by R.P. Weston and Fred J. Barnes, and made popular by the Billy Williams recording in 1907.

Some info and additional link about that lyrical version of the song are here: https://folksongandmusichall.com/index.php/hobnailed-boots-that-father-wore-the/

The tune the song is sung to is different to the one Barry learned from Charlie Batchelor. So it may be that the popularity of the recording, and the distinctiveness of the song title, were inspiration for rural musicians to use the title with other tunes.

Barry likes to follow this tune with “Joe’s Three-Parter,” also known as “Sofala,” by Joe Yates, another old Aussie fiddler.

Re: The Hobnail Boots That My Father Wore

The 1st part of this tune is an old chestnut. The 2nd part sounds both like a military march and is a bit lopsided.

Thanks for the link to the lyrics.
The 18 verses (!) show a bias towards quantity over quality.
A mildly entertaining tall tale, a series of non sequential, unconsequential visual gags.
The original title spelling betrays a London origin (a stage Cockney musical act).
It is making light of the ‘hard life’, a theme a lot of the audience in England or Australia would have been well aware of.

I reprint verse one here:

1.
Poor Farver’s feet took up half the street
So his boots were in proportion
And the kids he’d squash in a day, by gosh
It really was a caution.

Was the big boot theme an early C.20th meme?

Yes, I agree that the song lyrics at the Folk Song and Music Hall site are a bit outlandish. If you read the Mudcat discussion linked on that site, it turns out that the original lyrics were quite a bit different, and only had six verses. Someone along the line had fun adding a bunch more.

That video of Little Tich was fantastic! You may be onto something that it was inspiration for other big-boot imagery of the time. And Charlie Chapman kept it going with his iconic Little Tramp character starting in the 19-teens.
