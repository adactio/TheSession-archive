Mme Bolduc, a.k.a. La Bolduc, née Marie Travers, used this 1910 tune for her 1932 song:
“Les Policemen”
a song of praise about the Constabulary force of Canada (!)
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j4jpxrV0L5I
Year by year ref. catalogue:
https://disqu-o-quebec.com/Artistes/B/bolduc_la.html

She seemed to be positively impressed with them, although she begun with these rather sublunary lines:
"I have sung songs on all matters of topics
About girls and boys, carrots and turnips
About keeping jobs for local people*
So now I’ll push a croon about our great Constables"
…

*Alas, she did, the previous year.

The overall result is jocular yet never critical.
Her unmistakable lilting swells the mood of jollity.
Miss this one at your own loss!

Chicken, X:3

This is a cheeky version of the chicken reel;
The high a in the turn can be played in various ways:
- as ‘a flat’/‘g#’ (a slighly flatten A or a lydian G)
- as an overblown overtone A on the flute/whistle (d’ fingering, with all fingers down, slightly overblown)
- as a syncopated, graced note immitating a crooning chicken
- etc

Re: Chicken

My source for X:3 is a Jean Wiéner arrangement (for piano, percussion and recorder or ‘flageolet’) of Joseph Michael Daly’s 1910 composition.
It was commissioned by the O.R.T.F. for the French TV program ‘Histoires Sans Paroles’ in 1964.
The program (a short show of old fashioned B&W briefs) run up to the 1980’s.
Many practicing Catholics could not watch this family show because it was broadcast on Sunday morning when they might still be at mass or when the telly was on ‘off’!
But that’s how this tune became quite famous in France, anyhow, half a century after it was composed in the U.S..
*Jean Wiéner (1896-1982) scored much music for TV & cinema and was a concert organiser; he was a pianist and Gabriel Fauré encouraged him to compose. He took an interest in ‘Black-American’ music (as it was known then), this ragtime-style composition must have caught his attention.
**Joseph M(ichael) Daly (1883-1968) was a tin-pan alley composer with various rags and American style reels to his name. The Chicken Reel original score had the under-title ‘Two-step & Buck Dance’ on it. It was self-published in Boston.
You can visualise the original score (in C maj., with all its parts (4?) and its burlesque ‘shave-and-a-hair-cut’ ending) on the musescore site:
https://musescore.com/user/36482158/scores/6618449?from=youtube_share

Various words were added to it, including by Joseph Mittenthal (after 1910) and Sam Marley (c.1920), nothing too transcendental.
American bluegrass or old time fiddlers really helped put the ‘chicken’ in this reel, starting off with a ‘clucking’-like slide on the first note.
