Re: The Lounge Bar

Everybody seems to be playing this at the moment. A lot of people at my main session are convinced that this is a traditional Shetland tune, and they call it “Da Lounge Bar” in the style of much older tune names from Shetland. When I tell them that it is not a traditional Shetland tune but is a relatively new tune composed by somebody from Scandinavia, they seem to shut off their listening ears. The following week, when it is inevitably played again, it gets referred to, again, as a Shetland tune.

The session pub in Lerwick, Shetland, after which this tune is named is called ‘The Lounge Bar’ not ‘Da Lounge Bar’. Ok, so none of this really matters, we should simply judge the tune on its merits. I think it is pleasant enough but I don’t find anything exceptional about it. I have a feeling that if people simply referred to it as a modern Scandinavian tune, it would probably have slightly less currency

I’m tempted write a tune and call it The Bothy Band’ as an experiment

Re: The Lounge Bar

Interesting commentary, Edgar. First response - in respect of your prospective tune to be called ‘The Bothy Band’ - is that you might call to mind Oscar Wilde’s claim that he ‘could resist everything except temptation’. Yield to it, Edgar, yield to it and write that tune. Could be good.

But, a propros of ‘Da Lounge Bar’: I heard (no idea if true or what) that Annlaug Borsheim composed it when she was in Shetland with some hours to kill because of a delayed flight, and improved each shining hour by writing a new tune named after the venue where she was spending her time waiting. Thus, composed by a Norwegian person in Shetland - does that make it a Shetland tune, a Scandinavian tune, or a somewhere-in-the-middle-North-Sea sort of tune? Does it matter? (Answers on a postcard, please).

I recall that I, who am not Irish and was not raised in an Irish musico-cultural environment, once wrote a tune which I like to think was in the style of in Irish air while I was spending some time in the County Kerry and soaking up a lot of proper Irish music. Does that make my tune Irish? Or English? Or both, or neither?

This is a pretty inconsequential contribution to the thread but, since there is occasional discussion about ‘pure drop’ authenticity, it occurs to me to ask how such cross-border creations are to be regarded when highly localised and persisting traditions are more and more a thing of the past in this global village. A great many contributors to this site are situated in places all across the world, and I dare say are hearing ITM with cultural ears steeped in the sounds of their local communities, and interpreting it accordingly.

I’m reminded of learning about the origins of jazz in New Orleans, as being the meeting Blues, Spanish, French, Gospel, Creole, and marching band tunes (plus other things, of course) and melting into what we now refer to as ‘traditional jazz’. Which, of course, morphed into various other forms within a decade or two.

Maybe new highly localised ‘traditions’ are emerging all the time all over the place, but just spreading, expanding, and evolving more quickly than ever because of the availability of modern media. Before recordings they would presumably only be spread by specific muzos taking their tunes from place to place within travelling distance, and hence take longer to be picked up and subsequently repeated/enriched/altered/developed/contaminated/lost/ossified/turned into museum pieces, or whatever.

One for the ethno-musicological historians, perhaps.

Re: The Lounge Bar

Sorry to be pedantic, Trish, but they are speaking Shetland dialect. Not Norn. Norn has been considered extinct for over a century. Furthermore, Norn (before it was extinct) used suffixes for articles. This can be seen in most modern Scandinavian languages but especially Faroese, which was considered mutually intelligible with Norn. e.g. borð/table, borðið/the table.
“Da” as an article before the noun is the Scots and English contribution to the Shetland dialect, which is actually pretty notable because pretty much all the rest of the grammar for the Shetland dialect has strong Scandinavian roots.

Anyway, I agree with you that it’s a Scandi tune and not really a Shetland tune, but a lovely tribute to Shetland nonetheless.

Re: The Lounge Bar

Article only comes before the noun in Danish if there are other modifiers (adjectives, basically), otherwise they are suffixes.

Faroese (closest living language to Norn) has double definitives a lot of the time (so preceding article and a suffix on the noun especially if there’s also an adjective in the middle there), but you *need* the suffix. It’s the preceding article that drops off sometimes.

Using *only* the preceding article and dropping the suffixes is the Scots influence - not the existence of preceding articles entirely. Sorry, should’ve been clearer on that!

The Lounge Bar by Annlaug Børsheim

I was recently invited to join an ensemble for a nearby performance. Their list of tunes included The Lounge Bar, which I hadn’t heard before. Fortunately, I found a live video of the composer, Annlaug Børsheim, performing and learned it from that.

In rehearsals, though, the ensemble played the tune like a bit of a hurried Irish jig, and now I’ve discovered Irish jig renditions scattered throughout YouTube. I’ve understood the tune to be a Shetland tune. To be honest, my western ear might describe The Lounge Bar as a 6/8 waltz.

I’m looking for others’ thoughts on how to describe the style and form of the tune. Is there something in Shetland music or dance that is like a slightly quicker version of a Vienna waltz in 6/8, or ballroom-style music? Or am I just hearing what might be the composer’s personal flourishes in a jig?

↳ Posted by Barry Morse on September 13th, 2026.

Re: The Lounge Bar by Annlaug Børsheim

I really like the relaxed pace that Annlaug Børsheim uses when she plays it. And she wrote it, so that’s good enough for me. I don’t know a great deal about Shetland music, but the few tunes that I have learned are always interesting - they seem simple on the surface, but have a depth to them that I like. The music of Shetland certainly has a closer affinity to Scottish music than Irish, and there is a strong, historical Norwegian influence - and, of course, Annlaug Børsheim is Norwegian.

A lot of subtlety and regional differences in tunes can be lost when they are all played like other tunes simply because they are in the same metre. Not all tunes in 4/4 are reels, and not all tunes in 6/8 are jigs. Not all reels are played the same way. Not all IRISH reels are played the same way. (I really like The Fly Fishing Reel, by Jackie Daly, but I learned it in a session that played it rather slowly, loosely and lyrically. I finally listened to a recording of Daly playing it, and it’s just wrong to my ears. Way too bouncy and quick.)

Get your ensemble to listen to how the composer plays it. There may be no ‘wrong’ way to play it but we all have preferences.
