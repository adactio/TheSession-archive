Garfield’s Blackberry Blossom

Another from the fiddles of Eastern Kentucky.
I first learned of this tune at a session where “Blackberry Blossom” (,a different version,) had just been played.
One of the group asked if anyone knew “Garfield’s Blackberry Blossom”?
To me, the tune has a modal feel, (even though it doesn’t appear to be,) and played at a more moderate speed with double stops it’s a tune that I’ve trouble letting go of.

In your comment, you said: “To me, the tune has a modal feel, (even though it doesn’t appear to be)”

Interesting - I thought the same. I was puzzled at first, but on looking at it more closely, I see that it’s actually hexatonic. Although seemingly in bog-standard G-Maj, the sixth note of the scale (E) is missing throughout the tune.

That’s what gives it a “modal” feel.

Re: Garfield’s Blackberry Blossom

I’m reading the comments posted 10 years ago…
I think the heightened “modal” feel you felt with this tune has less to do with the fact it is hexatonic (since -as you have remarked as well- tons, if not most tunes on either side of the pond are hexatonic anyways.
It is not due to the fact the mode is hexatonic or pentatonic or what but, i believe, because it is written out / played out in an unusual mode.

Most trad American pentatonic tunes are in -or based on- the major pentatonic mode gapped on 4 and 7 and/or its relative minor pentatonic gapped on 6 and 2 (respectively) but here, in X:1, the *major* scale is used yet the missing notes correspond to gaps 2 and 6 of the *minor* scale.
NB; it is less the number of degrees used in a melody that makes it pentatonic or hexatonic; it is the roles certain degrees play.
You could be dealing for instance with a nonatonic melody where degrees 6 and 3 are both found as sharp and natural notes (a.k.a. movable degrees) and yet, your basic mode is pentatonic because if you remove these notes or replace them by the nearest degree, the general caracter of the tune is not affected.

In this here tune, for example, the 2nd degree is present but mostly as a passing note (non defining or non structural).

Another reason we feel some tunes are more modal is due to “the melodic syntax”, so to say;
like the difference between, say, the Spanish language and Quetchua; they tend to use the same 5 vowels but in a different order, so they sound quite different (although perhaps both equally exotic to the non-Spanish and Quetchua speakers).
Modality is in the mind, therefore!
It’s what we’re used to, I guess.

Compare; X:2 which veers towards the more orthodox 2nd-off, 6th-off pentatonic minor mode.

Specific modal signatures exist also in the way you play discreet degrees; you could slide the 3rd degree and play the 7th blue, etc… even though the notation may not show you all that.
