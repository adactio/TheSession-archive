Re: Leather Away The Wattle-O

Leather away the Wattle was the first polka in a set called “old sets” from the Ballinakill Traditional Players (or maybe by that time the Ballinakill Ceili Band). The second tune was the Grand Old Dame Britannia, which is also called (in Martin Mulvihill’s collection) the Half Door polka. The Ballinakill recording did not name it but simply called it ‘the Ladies’ Chain’ which was the dance to go with. These tunes were on 78s but also reissued a few years ago on a cd with a lovely painted photo of a young Aggie Whyte, who was in the 2nd generation of the Ballinakill Band. It’s a compilation cd of women traditional players. Anyways I think that’s possibly why these 2 tunes get paired up namewise.

Re: Leather Away The Wattle-O

Meaning of the name of the tune:

John O’Daly notes:

“This spirited air escaped the notice of our most eminent collector, [Edward] Bunting, and probably would have never been in print had it not fallen into our hands. The words are the production of a violent Jacobite. By leathering away with the wattle, he implies his determination to decide all political differences by an appeal to ‘physical force’. The wattle was a stout cudgel, or ‘Ailpin’, in frequent requisition at country fairs and faction fights early in the present century. (p. 193).”

Meaning of the phrase:

To Beat Vigorously with a Stick: “Leather away” means to beat or go at something with great energy, and a “wattle” refers to a flexible twig, stick, or switch. Translated literally, it means thrashing or beating with a stick.

Curing Leather with Bark: “Wattle” bark was historically a primary source of tannin used in Ireland and Britain for curing animal hides into leather. The phrase likely relates to the vigorous physical work of processing or beating hides during tanning.

Folklore/Slang: Among some traditional musicians, the phrase has also been passed around as a ribald or colloquial euphemism for male masturbation, though folklorists note this is likely a later, tongue-in-cheek folk etymology rather than the original literal meaning.

Conclusion: Almost certainly the phrase refers to the process of curing leather, as Wattle bark is a principle source of tannin in Ireland and Great Britain. The bark may have tannin content of 40–50 percent and is ground into a powder for the process, producing a solid, very firm, and faintly pink leather, which is especially used for soles.
