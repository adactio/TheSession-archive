Under The Tholsel

From the playing of Garry Walsh.

Transposed from Abmaj.

Traditional tune.

The Tholsel is a building in Drogheda, built around 1870. Apparently “ending up under the Tholsel” was once a derogatory expression.

↳ Posted by NfldWhistler on June 23rd, 2020.

Under The Tholsel, X:2

Tholsel, Tolsey house, Tolboot…
These buildings are often called today the Market House.
Some would have been used as a courthouse, hence the derogatory expression, i suppose.

Being non-Irish and living on the continent, I don’t know the tholsel, I even just know Drogheda as a name without being capable to locate the town on a map (45 km north of Dublin).
Thanks for this tune which made me looking for more information about the tholsel and Drogheda. According to what I read on the internet, the building was erected in 1770.
I also found a song (no music, just the lyrics) :
"“Under the Clock”

There’s a place down in Drogheda, the Tholsel it’s called
Where the boys all stand with their backs to the walls,
They come down from the Labour and they gather to talk
But you won’t get no news standing under the clock
Fol-the-diddle- ring – o- the day- the day [repeat]

I went walking down Shop St with the half of a crown
And I went into Daly’s the cheapest in town
But the pints had gone up and it gave me a shock and
You won’t get your pint standing under the clock
Fol…………

Now the colleens of Drogheda are fair just the same
But they won’t let you near them to ruin their good name
You can buy them a diamond by going into hock
But you won’t get your………… Standing under the clock.
Fol…………..

Now I have been ‘round the world and I’m going again
And when I find that rainbow with gold at the end
I will come back to Drogheda with gold in great stock
And I will end up my days sleeping under the clock."

Re: Under The Tholsel

Nice find Moxhe,

It seems to be a case of
Loitering vs. The Clock!

(As the tholsels, often ‘adorned’ with a clock, vied with and displaced the chapel’s bell as a locus and symbol of authority, the pace and quality of life, the nature of work (tied to the new industrial and capitalist power structures) drastically changed, leaving many to ponder how and why.
‘Moving with the times’ and deified ‘Progress’, left a lot of people behind. Up against walls. Powerless and pennyless. Dreaming of unattainable ease and wealth. )

A great proportion of Irish songs reflect/ed this problematic relationship. 250 years of it and counting!
