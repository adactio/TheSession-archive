Fifteen comments

Huish The Cat

Piper Sean Folsom and fiddler Cathie Whitesides recorded this on their group Sheila na Gig’s “Holy Well” LP in the 1980s. Sean obtained the tune from piper Denis Brooks, who apparently obtained it from either the Petrie or Joyce collections; there is a similar jig in Petrie, Foxy Mary, but the 2nd part is different; perhaps Denis composed the 2nd part on his own. Whatever the case it is a fine piping jig, very old fashioned in its one octave, and apparently has made the rounds a bit, as I have a mid 90s recording of famed Dublin piper Mick O’Brien playing it.

Other Cats being Hu(i)shed are in various books, the old jig usually called as such is more akin to Bímíd ag Ól.

http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WuCokVnVbBw

Huish The Cat, a single Jig in one Octave

Kevin is partially right, I did hear Irish Piper Dennis Brooks playing a different version of this Jig known as “Foxy Mary” which has a different “B” part and can be found in some other Irish Music collections, & is not written in whole, or in part, by Dennis Brooks. My version came from the Roche Collection, which at the time I started playing the tune in 1975, I found in a photocopy of the original Tune Books, that were in 3 volumes. The Roche Collection reprint, has been available for some time now, so have a look at it if You like this Tune. As for the Recording here, off of the Sheila na Gig album “Holy Well” (1985) this rendition on You Tube, has the Gain up to high, so it is distorted. I have asked my friend who put it up on my “Channel” to fix it, but to no avail. The Second Tune I segue into is “The Rakes of West Meath” in 9/8 time, which I found in O’Neill’s 1001 Tunes (1907).

Re: Hunt The Cat

The tune uses a gapped modal scale, D E G A B C. It can be interpreted by an accompanist as either D Mixolydian or D Dorian, since there are neither F#s nor Fnats in it at all.

I tend more towards hearing it as Mixolydian, since the conventional regulators layout has an F# instead of an Fnat and I would play it in that manner.

Of course, if you play just the chanter and drones with a guitarist or bouzouki player accompanying, he or she may do as she wishes with regard to the DDorian vs Dmix question, and it would sound good either way.

Harmonising ‘Huish the cat’ for choir
I sing tenor in a choir and am learning how to arrange and compose choral music. I am not an instrumentalist and not a traditional music singer either, so I hope the question is still on topic for this community.

For an amusing piece about a cat, I’d like to take “Huish the cat” version 1 (https://thesession.org/tunes/13145) and use it either as a melody or stretched out as a bassline for a three-part adult choir. Its last two bars nicely fit the words “Hoosh the cat out from under the table”. Probably half speed unless I feel up to something like the pace and tongue-twisting of Anúna’s ‘Dúlamáin’.
AFAICS from the discussions here, the piece is in D Mixolydian, with no F or F#. Harmonising this is discussed in https://thesession.org/discussions/14058
If the melody had included an F, mixolydian would imply it should be F natural. The sheet music has the G key signature, which seems to hint that any harmonic treatment should include F#. This does not seem to accord with traditional practice as discussed in ‘Harmonising in ITM’ (https://thesession.org/discussions/46929#comment939263).

So my questions are: Are there any songs which use this melody? What is regarded as successful practice in harmonising this kind of modal melody?
There are all kinds of groups doing adventurous things with the harmonies and rhythms of traditional tunes, and I have barely started looking at these. Maybe I should spend more time at cmc.ie, but I thought I’d ask the experienced people here first.
Thank you
Patrick

Re: Huish The Cat

Patrick, in the mixolydian scale it’s the seventh that is flat (i.e., minor) not the third.
So D mixolydian is D-E-F#-G-A-B-C-D.
