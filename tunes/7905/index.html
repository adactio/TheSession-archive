Thirty-two comments

Mmmmmmm!!! ~ pig’s feet!!! :-P

For you below D challenged folk out there, well, there is this other way with measure 2 of the A-part ~

~ | GAGE D2 DE | ~

For you mad purists out there who swear off or swear about anything not fully certified ‘Irish’, I learned this here tune, or actually, re-learned it, off uh some guy with a strong Irish accent, though he may have been fakin‘ it, but he was from the ’Emerald Isle", extremely short, dressed in green, smoked a clay pipe, was a damned smart dancer, wouldn’t let me touch his pot of gold or his fiddle, and swore he’d learned this here tune offa the fairy folk, which he also claimed were personal friends. Hell, I wouldn’t have ever been able to play his fiddle anyway, as it was only a few inches long. Cute little guy, but I wasn’t going to say that to his face. The last person who did didn’t fare well at all, plagued with warts in places I can’t say…

Anyway, it’s obvious that the title is Irish, isn’t it? Who else would barbecue pigs feet when they are so much better pickled? :-/

Push Up the Hog Foot Under the Bed

The title has been a subject of much debate. John Hartford used to perform a tune with the title “Shove That Hog’s Foot Further In The Bed”

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=95tEtxLG0Oc&feature=youtu.be&t=315

It’s not the same tune, but the folk process has given us many instances of the same title being used for two or more melodies. Pushing a hog’s foot under a bed seems to be the earliest form of the title. There is a legend that relates it to a slave hiding food pilfered from the master, and the title “Push the Hog’s Feet under the Bed” appears in the index of Thomas Talley’s 1922 book “Negro Folk Rhymes.” (There is no page number and no such rhyme appears in the book, suggesting Talley had heard of the title and had itended to include it but failed to.)

I’ve found an even earlier reference - Dec. 7. 1900 the Semi-Weekly Interior Journal of Stanford, Kentucky ran this social note on page 1:

“The bachelors of Cedar Creek neighborhood are preparing to give a big dance during the Christmas holidays. Nobody but old maids and widows will be invited. James T. Pleasants will be employed as fiddler and he can only play one tune and that Is ‘Push Up the Hog Foot Under the Bed.’”

It’s my conjecture that folks who did not understand the older title thought “fire” might make more sense than “bed” and made the change.
