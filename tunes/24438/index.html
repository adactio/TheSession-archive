The Scottish Settler’s Lament

This is the Tannahill Weavers’ setting (as I remember it) of the Scottish-Canadian song The Scarborough Settler’s Lament, written by Sandy Glenndenning in the 19th century.
To set his fine and touching words he used the first part of an air called “Of a’ the Airts the Wind can Blaw”.

The Scarborough Settler’s Lament, X:2

This is a G major transposition of the E major version printed in The Penguin Book of Canadian Folk Songs -No29, p76-
ed. Edith Fowke, 1973

Catalogue ref; Fowke and Mills, 94.

Her notes p.200 remind us that, as well as the maritimes, Scottish people settled in the Ontario townships of Glengarry, Perth, Dundas and Scarborough.
In 1840, long before Scarborough was absorbed by Toronto, Sandy Glenndenning wrote these poignant verses.
(Outside Ontario this song was known simply as The Scottish Settler’s Lament. Other tunes were used as well: Sheila Bucher, regular editor of the Old Favourites page in the Family Herald said her grandmother sang the words to The Irish Emigrant’s Lament)

1.
Away wi Canada’s muddy creeks and Canada’s fields o pine
your land of wheat is a goodly land but ah! it isna mine
the heathy hill the grassy dale the daisy-spangled lea
the purling burn and craggy linn auld Scotia’s glens gie me
2.
Oh i wad like to hear again the lark on Tinny’s hill
and see the wee bit gowany that bloom beside the rill
like banished Swiss who views afar his Alps wi longing e’e
i gaze upon the morning star that shines on my countrie
3.
Nae mair i’ll win by Eskdale Pen or Pentland’s craggy cone
the days can ne’er come back again of thirty years that’s gone
but fancy oft at midnight hour will steal across the sea
yestreen amid a pleasant dream i saw the auld countrie
4.
Each well known scene that met my view brought childhood’s joys to mind
the blackbird sang on Tushy linn the song he sang lang syne
but like a dream time flies away again the morning came
and i awoke in Canada three thousand miles frae hame.
