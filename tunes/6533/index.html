Usually played in F# minor

I’ve heard this tune many times played by many different people, including my husband. The sheet music appears in several transcription tunebooks of artists such as Don Messer, Alfred Montmarquette and Phillippe Bruneau; and it’s on many Quebecois cds. I’ve always heard it (and seen it everywhere but here) with the little crooked pauses in the end of the “b” part of the tune before it winds up to a repeat of the “a” part. It’s usually played AABBAA, and I think those little extra holds add a lot to the charm of the original.

Disambiguation

The “Polka Chinoise” printed above is only one of many so-called Chinese Polkas composed and published circa 1850-1950 (roughly).
See for comparison :
La Polka Chinoise, a classic musette tune (here recorded in 2014 by Serge Desaunay with Kieran Fahy and Ray Barron)
https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=A8TordcqJ1k&pp=ygUWZGVsYXVuYXkgY2hpbmVzZSBwb2xrYQ%3D%3D

I love this piece of exotica (in the Parisian musette genre) which was composed by Georges Ghestem (1903-1978) and Joseph Colombo (1900-1973)
I couldn’t find an original Colombo recording but Prud’homme’s version will do just fine:
https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=nEmoB2z6yGY&pp=ygUUcG9sa2EgY2hpbm9pc2UgSG9uZXI%3D#searching

Several other “Chinese polkas” are known:
one, a so called “old age sin”, by Giacomo Rossini (1792-1868) can be heard here on the piano:
https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=FBL4WhJCwvk&pp=ygUWcm9zc2luaSBwb2xrYSBjaGlub2lzZQ%3D%3D
Hardly a polka as we understand it on the dance floor, I suppose.
Did Rossini start a trend, though?

Light composers from France produced several show polkas which were sold as piano sheet music and must have been popular in the bal room as well.
For instance Théodore Bonnay (d.1904) named his: “Tchinn-tchinn (polka chinoise)”, which seems to show he was yielding to an already established sub-genre.
…unless…
Unless he single-handedly started a craze?
…for it was him, accompanied by his son, who launched his new-fangled invention, the xylophone, in 1866!
(As everone knows xylophones sound quintessentially Chinese!) ;-)

How many Chinese Polkas have been written and recorded so far? It would be interesting to find out.
The only other two I found a reference for, searching only the internet, are both French publications (source: BnF);
one by P. Champion (c. 1880-1910??)
and the other by Charles Melant (ca.1880).
Without forgetting, of course, from the other side of the Atlantic and sounding very North-American, Montmarquette’s own Polka Chinoise of 1928.
(here by Yves Lambert:
https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=CIr-q9p3wOs&pp=ygUMcG9sY2EgY2luZXNl )

For my part, Montmarquette’s tune still sounds the most fun although it’s definitely not a polka as we know it LOL. As for pentatonic (and hexatonic) tunes, there’re surprisingly many of them in Irish music but funny thing is, unless you really, really want to stress their pentatonic nature with reserved variations and very specific backing, they rarely sound particularly “eastern” or “Chinese”! So Montmarquette really brought it up to 11 by focusing on limited scale and very particular rhythm.

Re: The Chinese

Thanks for your appreciation, Bregolas!
Yes, that’s right;
the tunes are only linked together by their name and their (somewhat) ‘quirkxotic’ nature.
The late 19th century, with the British and French colonial excursions into China and Indochina are to be ‘blamed’ for such trends. (Yet, the center of an empire will not resist the influence of its periphery, especially in the arts sector). At the time, couple dances were a by-word for modernity but in an area of dizzying change, it seems logical everything suddenly went polka! (itself a newfangled export). If several dozens have survived, hundreds were published at the time and competition was fierce so the role of the exotic titles was to hype up the newly commodified music (these entangled trends are exemplified in The Hippopotamus Polka, a long forgotten composition, mirroring both the fascination with the African menagerie as another provider of exoticism and commercial opportunity; the title is more colourful than the tune itself).
And yes,
some clichés die hard and I remember a music teacher in the 1980’s hammering on the piano, in parallel 4ths, a pentatonic tune she made up on the spot, telling her class it was ‘Chinese’! Having said that, for all its OTT ‘Chinese’ character and its ‘buzz’ title, I find the Ghestem/Colombo composition truly enjoyable, a really joyful thing, “but of course”, as the French put it, the culprits were outstanding composers of their generation!
In the 1920’s, the polka had lost its popularity (or edge) in the cities‘ dance halls (still remaining a strong favorite in the more popular and country joints); by then record shops opened up for business everywhere and everything marketed as ’blues’ sold like hot cross buns. Montmarquette recorded and published in such an environment. He too had to ensure the titles he performed etched themselves in the memory of his audience, both by virtue of the music and of the names.
