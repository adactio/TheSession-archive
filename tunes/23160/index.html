Caniad Y Gwyn Bibyd

Caniad y gwyn bibyd (The song of the fair piper / Amhrán an phíobaire bháin) has an interesting history to it.
It is one of the rare pieces of harp music from the bardic times we still posess although it had laid dormant for over 300 years in the Welsh Ap Huw manuscript before some courageous scholars and astute musicians decided to try and restitute its secrets to us.
I first heard it from a beautiful but much too rare CD the Co.Wicklow-based Irish harpist/harper Paul Dooley recorded 20 years ago.

Re: Caniad Y Gwyn Bibyd

So. The ABC I posted is only half the story for it only represents the top part of the harmonised piece. The harper played on the bass strings what must have been a simple ‘ground’ which to and froed between two notes (perhaps more) at a time.
The left hand, meanwhile, busied itself in the treble range, to play the melodic formulas and “divisions” visible in the ABC.

But that simple arrangement tells another story; for this piece, even if it was arranged according to the harmonic strictures of the times, was meant to lampoon the so-called art of the up-coming pipers: the new-fangled bqgpipe which was introduced in the late medieval period was much louder than the metal-strung clarsach but its range of notes and its harmonic potential offered far less possibilities to the player/composer.
The harpers looked with suspicion to the pipers for all were kept men and they vied not just for income but for status as well from the same aristocratic patrons.
This piece belonged to the comic repertoire then; it mocked the musical shortcomings of the new comers/intruding pipers.
This context can help situate the piece as belonging to a much older era than of the time when it was transcribed in the R.obert Ap Huw manuscript.
Of course, the harp and the pipes coexisted for a period, they each found their niche …until they were tragically displaced, destroyed and distorted -by force of writ, fire and ideology- by the desire to silence that which could subvert the advance of the all consuming English Empire. Conformity was to be imposed from a centralised model in the religious, economic social and cultural spheres.
Today the harp is still mostly silent; the great art, aloof -and at time mocking- is no longer commonly heard.
Check and seek out the chiming harmonies of the clarsach if you are not yet familiar with it: there is true enchantement in its pure, lingering overtones; more than could ever meet the eye on this score for sure (thus examplifying the superiority of orality over the litteral)!
