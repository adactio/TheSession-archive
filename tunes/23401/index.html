Dum A-dee A-die A-doe

My source for this dandling ditty is Hamish Henderson,
who can be heard singing it (28‘46"-29’11) at
https://youtu.be/7aV1UUmvgJo?t=1728 (recorded 1951 by Alan Lomax)

The words go something like;
1.
DUM a-dee a-die a-doe
DUM a-dee a daddy o
There’s a BABbie in the hoose
and I’m his mammy o
2.
DUM a-dee a-die a-doe
DUM a-dee a daddy o
There’s a BABbie in the hoose
and Ewan’s his daddy o

The melody has only one part but is a catchy necklace of notes nonetheless. It has been used to sing the song Molly na gCuach Ní Chuilleanáin which several Irish bands have covered.

Molly Na gCuach Ní Chuilleanáin, X:2

This version follows Altan’s version (Track 7 on Blackwater, 1996)
which presents two parts (used in the chorus ‘1’ and ‘2’; see:
https://songsinirish.com/molly-na-gcuach-ni-chuilleanain-atlan-lyrics/).
I have not included the fermata/pauses on notes which give their version a lot of breath and breadth but wrote it in a regular x4 number of beats/measures.

LYRICS:
Molly Na gCuach Ní Chuilleanáin (= The curly-haired Molly Ní Chuilleanáin)
1.
Ar meisce cha dtéim níos mó
braon leanna go deo ní bhlaisfidh mé
Ó chaill mé mo chailín beag óg
a chuireadh i mo phócaí an t-airgead
§/Curfá:
Is fada liom uaim í, uaim í
is fada liom uaim í ó d’imigh sí
is fada liom thíos agus thuas í
Molly na gcuach Ní Chuilleanáin
2.
Dhéanfaidh mé tigh ar an ard
is beidh ceithre ba bainne breaca agam
Is ní ligfidh mé ’n duine dá gcomhair
ach Molly deas bhán Ní Chuilleanáin
3.
Dá mbeinnse i ndeacair an bháis
is na daoine a rá nach dtiocfainn as
Ní dhéanfainn mo thiomna go brách
go dtiocfadh Moll bhán Ní Chuilleanáin
4.
Bhí mise lá ar an choill
is tharla dom soilse bhrádóige
Dhéanfadh sí marbhán beo
nó buachaill deas óg den tseanduine.

Re: Dum A-dee A-die A-doe

If I’m not mistaken, X:1 is the air to the song “The Day We Went to Rothesay-O.” The chorus goes, “Dirrum a doo a doo a day, dirrum a doo a daddy o, Dirrum a doo a doo a day, the day we went to Rothesay, O!” A (possibly) older song which uses the same tune is “The Tinker’s Waddin’” (i.e. the tinker’s wedding). It’s quite possibly taken from an old pipe tune, or an imitation thereof, but I can’t place it.

Dum A-dee A-die A-doe, X:3

Transcribed from a televised Ronnie Drew Group performance. (see url link above).
In this version, the rhythm is influenced by the prosody and the declamatory style of the performance.
Interestingly the tune part of the song is split in two:
instead of the sequence AA‘BB’ (usual in dance tunes) we have here ABBA‘ where A’ is the lilted chorus.

The Day We Went to Rothesay O
1.
Wan Hogmanay at the Glasgow Fair there was me masel and several mair
we aa went off tae hae a tear and spend the day in Rothesay o
we wandered doon the Broomielaw through wind and rain and sleet and snaw
And at forty meenits aifter twa we got the length o Rothesay o
§§§.
Durrum a-doo a-durrum a day durrum a-doo a daddy o
durrum a-doo a-durrum a day the day we went tae Rothesay o!
2.
A sodger lad named Ruglen Glenfield whose regiment were at Barrenhill
went off wi a tanner tae get a gill in a public hoose in Rothesay o
says‘e, I think I’d like tae sing Says I, ye’ll ne’er dae sic a thing
he said, clear the room and we mak a ring I’m quite the man in Rothesay o
3.
In search o boardings we did slide tae find a place where we could bide
there were eighty fowr o us inside in a single room in Rothesay o
Oh we aa lay doon tae tak oor ease when somebody happened for tae sneeze
and he wakened half a million fleas that et us alive in Rothesay o
4.
There were several kinds o bugs some had feet like dyers’ clogs
they sat on the bed they cockit their lugs they cried, Hurrah for Rothesay o
says I, I think it’s time for us to slow up but the police widna let us stop
So we went and joined the Band of Hope and bid farewell to Rothesay o.
§§§.
Durrum a-doo a-durrum a day durrum a-doo a daddy o
durrum a-doo a-durrum a day Oh the day we went tae Rothesay o!

The tinker’s waddin, X:4

Re: The Tinkler’s Waddin’

LYRIC:
This version can be used as a guide to Ian Manuel’s 1971 spirited version; he sings it in good company here; https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bbCFwxqBOL4

THE TINKLER‘S WADDIN’
(words by William Watt,1792-1859; see note below)
(NB: The *-ed verses are not sung by Manuel)
1.
In June, when broom in bloom was seen,
And bracken waved fu’ fresh and green,
And warm the sun, wi’ silver sheen,
The hills and glens did gladden, O
2.
Ae day, upon the Border bent,
The tinklers pitch’d their gypsy tent,
And auld and young, wi’ ae consent,
Resolved to haud a waddin’, O.
.§§§.
Durrum a-do a-dum a-day
Durrum a-do a dadee-o
Durrum a-do a-dum a-day
Hurrah for the Tinklers‘ waddin’ O!
3.
The bridegroom was wild Norman Scott,
Wha thrice had broke the nuptial knot,
Ance was sentenced to be shot
For breach o’ martial orders, O
4.
His gleesome joe was Madge MaKell,
A spaewife, match for Nick himsel’,
Wi’ glamour, cantrip, charm, and spell,
She frichted baith the Borders,
.§§§.
5.
Nae priest was there, wi’ solemn face,
Nae clerk to claim o’ crowns a brace;
The piper and fiddler played the grace
To set their gabs a-steerin’, O
6.*
((Mang beef and mutton, pork and veal,
Mang paunches, plucks, and fresh cow-heel,
Fat haggises, and cauler jeel,
They clawed awa‘ careerin’, O))
IAN MANUEL DIDDERS HER, WITH:
6.
Twa big scones they were baked
An‘ thick wi’ butter they were spread,
A cup o‘ tea an’ a hard-boiled egg,
Tae carry on the waddin’ O.
.§§§.
7.*
((Fresh salmon, newly taen in Tweed,
Saut ling and cod o’ Shetland breed,
They worried, till kytes were like to screed,
Mang flagons and flasks o’ gravy, O))
8.*
((There was raisin-kail and sweet-milk saps,
And ewe-milk cheese in whangs and flaps,
And they rookit, to gust their gabs and craps,
Richt mony a cadger’s cavie, O.))
.§§§.
9.
The drink flew round in wild galore,
And soon upraised a hideous roar
Blithe Comus ne’er a queerer core
Saw seated round his table, O
10.
They drank, they danced, they swore, they sang,
They quarrell’d and greed the hale day lang,
And the wranglin’ that rang amang the thrang
Wad match’d the tongues o’ Babel, O.
.§§§.
11.
The drink gaed dune before their drooth,
That vexed baith mony a maw and mooth,
It damp’d the fire o’ age and youth
And every breast did sadden, O
12.
Till three stout loons flew ower the fell,
At risk o’ life, their drouth to quell,
And robb’d a neebourin’ smuggler’s stell,
To carry on the waddin’, O.
.§§§.
13.
Wi‘ thunderin’ shouts they hail’d them back
To broach the barrels they werena slack,
While the fiddler’s plane-tree leg they brak’
For playin’ “Farewell to Whisky, O”.
14.
Delirium seized the roarous thrang,
The bagpipes in the fire they flang,
And sowtherin’ airns on riggin’s rang,
The drink play’d siccan a plisky, O.
.§§§.
15.
The sun fell laich owre Solway banks,
While on they plied their roughsome pranks,
And the stalwart shadows o’ their shanks,
Wide ower the muir were spreadin’, O.
16.
Till, heads and thraws, amang the whins,
They fell wi’ broken brows and shins,
And sair craist banes filled mony skins,
To close the tinklers‘ waddin’, O.
.§§§.
…………………………………………………………………………………………………………..
*spaewife: fortune-teller
*cantrip: spell, charm
*plucks: herrings damaged by the net
*cauler jeel: cool or fresh jelly
*kytes were like to screed: bellies were like to rip
*milksaps: food soaked in milk
*to gust their gabs and craps: to stuff their mouths and bellies
*cadger’s cavie: grumbler’s hen-coop
*plisky: trick
*craist: cracked

<<Who, I wonder, is he that was reared in any country district in Scotland and is old enough to have cut his wisdom teeth and never heard the rarely humorous, graphic, and rattling song of “The Tinkler’s Waddin‘,” which is quite a classic of its kind, though seldom met with in printed form? Even when printed the author’s name has not been attached. Yet we know that it was written by William Watt, who was born at West Linton, Peeblesshire, 1792, and was author, besides, of “Kate Dalrymple.” [https://thesession.org/tunes/8223] Watt, who was a weaver to trade, cultivated with success the three sister arts of poesy, painting and music. In his early career he removed to East Kilbride, where for a time he was Parish Kirk precentor. Two editions of his poems were published during his life - one in 1835 and another in 1844 - both of which sold rapidly. A third and last edition, issued in four monthly parts, one shilling each, and comprising in all 338 pages, appeared soon after his death. He died as late as 1859.’

Robert Ford, Vagabond Songs and Ballads of Scotland With Many Old and Familiar Melodies (single volume edition). Paisley: Alexander Gardner, 1904, 1-4.>>

The Tinkler’s Wedding , X:5

My source for this version -perhaps the most enticing of all the ones posted so far- is;
Track 5, Side B: The Tinkler’s Wedding
on the 1972 LP The Frosty Ploughshare (Bothy Songs & Ballads sung by Ian Manuel)
with Alistair Anderson, Bobby Campbell and Jim Eldon in the chorus (§§§).

NB: the snaps can occur anywhere depending on the words’ rhythm.
You can check the tempo at: https://youtu.be/bbCFwxqBOL4
which is rather more ponderous than for an Irish slide. But still very melodic and groovy.
