Cashman’s Polka

From the CD ‘An evening with Kevin Burke’ - track 2 tune 1
The Slíabh Luachra set

↳ Posted by Grtnwnhvn on April 29th, 2019.

Mistitled as “Cashman’s”, dubbed “DAF Truck polka” by John Walsh himself

And so I’m back with another tale of the Great Polka Confusion! It should be noted from the get-go that all of this is meant in good humour, and no one is being put on the spot – if there’s one thing I’ve learned about tune titles after studying hundreds of them, it’s that title mix-ups in traditional music is par for the course!

This polka was first titled “Cashman’s” in the sleeve notes of the latest album by The Monks of the Screw, which is a fantastic fiddle-flute recording full of lovely, rare tunes I highly recommend getting. The notes said John Walsh wrote this polka and that he named it after his neighbour Patrick Cashman.
https://thesession.org/recordings/1001

John Walsh would confirm he “made up” this tune (as he prefers to say), but he never called it “Cashman’s”. What’s more, Patrick Cashman would not have been mad about this polka at all, and here’s why.

Patrick Cashman was an accordion player from Derrygallon, Co. Cork who played with John Walsh and frequented the Scully’s session in Newmarket. He played a C/C# accordion. Yes, that’s right, he played a red Paolo Soprani tuned C/C# in sessions. For anyone not familiar with the button box trickery, if we take the D-melodeon or the C#/D accordion as the “normal” layout for concert pitch, he would’ve had all his tunes lower by a semitone were he to play C#/D style, of a full step lower if he played on the outer C row melodeon-style. That left him with the option to play tunes in D in the somewhat awkward “E major” fingering starting on the outer row (kinda like B/C style, but inside-out). Of course, depending on the tune’s key and the situation, Patrick would either play on the C row, or play solo, or borrow someone else’s machine, or not play at all. He was well able to play in the B/C system too, from what I hear, meaning he would have been fine at a session in Eb while playing cross-row style, if there was such a session. Fiddle players could also retune in theory, but other box players of course couldn’t!

I think you’ve already sensed where this is going. If anything, Patrick wasn’t necessarily a great fan of tunes in the key of D, which this polka is. However, he really enjoyed playing them in the key of G, which from the one-row perspective is like playing in A on the D-melodeon, a very pleasant fingering to play in. This leads us to the polka that was actually referred to as “Patrick Cashman’s Favourite” by John Walsh.

It’s this polka: https://thesession.org/tunes/10906

It, too, was originally in D, but John transposed it into G, which in the eyes of Patrick made it more “fiddle-like” and fun to play on his C/C# box. John didn’t write that other tune, but the new key stuck, so it’s named after him sometimes. The key of G also opened the opportunity to “play the bass,” or play in octaves, which the Monks of the Screw did a fine job on, with Paudie Scully playing the flute against two “low” fiddles! And so, finally, we got to the point where the confusion happened – the Monks recorded both of these polkas on their CD in 2000, but the names and the stories attached to them got mixed up.

So, if it’s not “Cashman’s”, what is it? For that, I’d have to first redirect you to other John’s tunes, none of which had any names whatsoever, until someone else put a name on them (links below). This polka hadn’t been any different and remained untitled for decades – until someone insisted that John put a name on it. This coincided with a certain near-incident on a Mallow road involving a huge DAF truck. Now check out what the first three notes of this polka are, sans the off-beat.

Yes, this polka was actually christened DAF Truck polka by John himself – and you can prove it by citing its very notes!
