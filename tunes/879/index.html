↳ Posted by crannog on July 14th, 2003.

A hint to newish accompanists: This tune has a G key signature, but the tonal centre is D, the fifth degree of the G major scale, making this a D mixolydian tune. DO NOT back it like a G major tune!

Think in terms of modes, it helps to more accurately identify the scale used in the composition of the melody and thus select appropriate the appropriate chords.

OK, I’m off my soapbox.

This tune first appeared in print in Maurice O’Farrell’s collection of tunes for the Irish bagpipes, published in 1804.

I LOVE the fourth part of this tune, it’s mostly crans and “bent” C naturals-two of the most characteristic sounds of the irish pipes.

Re: An Phis Fhliuch

An Phis Fhliuch
un FISH lee-OOHH
/əN fis´ L´ux/

“The Wet Pussy” (as listed in one of the Chieftains albums)

According to a Na Píobairí book of older uilleann pipe settings, the first bar of the B part should go to a D as in the first two setting listed above, and in the E part, the same note should be a C natural. That distinction has been gradually lost over time and people are muddling the two parts up.

Re: An Phis Fhliuch

I’ve wondered about that note, too. Playing a C there seems to make the E part redundant. Willie Clancy always played a D there. Chieftains do as well, on their 2nd LP. Seán Ó Riada is said to have picked the tune out of the Stanford/Petrie collection: https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Phis_fhliuch_(An) tunearch.org has a separate entry for O‘F’sWtL, for some reason: https://tunearch.org/wiki/O%27Farrell%27s_Welcome_to_Limerick

The blame for the C is on Tommy Peoples, who played it that way on the High Part of the Road. Irishtuneinfo says he was the 3rd to record it: https://www.irishtune.info/tune/316/ (Willie played it on the Breeze from Erin, which isn’t listed there)

Re: An Phis Fhliuch

This just in from the “there are no rules” department: there’s yet another arrangement of ‘An Pis Fhliuch’ on a new album, ‘Music for the Church of the Wailing Banshees’, by Chris Crilly. It’s joined with that slightly daintier slip jig, ‘The Butterfly’. There’s piano, bass, horns and drums. The track title, ‘The Butterfly and the Fish’, could have been improved, say: ‘A Treat for a Trout’. But I suppose gentle lepidopterists and Schubert’s progeny might have got their probosces out of joint!

The album is a mix of rollicking, dismembered and rejigged (sorry) well known Irish tunes, including ‘Cúnla’, ‘The Old Torn Petticoat’, the ‘Tarbolton’ and Tim Walsh’s 7/8 version of ‘Carolan’s Concerto’. There are delightfully blasphemous twists on liturgical themes (yup!), Turkish kanun, oud and percussion, even Ghanaian Highlife flavours. The eclectic album ends with a contemplative improvisation on ‘Seacht nDolás na Maighdine Muire’ for piano and kanun. Some here may be aghast, but hey….no rules. For all we know, there may have been wild Celtic sessions in the taverns of antediluvian Anatolia! In the immortal words of the late Fred Torak: “The more there is, the more there is.” Quite so.
