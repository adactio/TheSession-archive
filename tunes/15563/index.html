Re: La Polverita Fiera

THIS IS a fascinating illustration of the inevitable “folk process” with regard to tune transmission, both in musical interpretation and the title.

Talking here about two tunes often cited as “Tax Max Mazurka #1” and “Tax Max Mazurka #2”, or sometimes played as a medley called “Tax Max Mazurkas”.

Origins: they were composed in 1983 by me – L.E. McCullough – and titled “La Polverita Fiera” (The Savage Little Powderpuff) and “Buscando la Quinta Pata al Gato” (Looking for the Fifth Foot of the Cat).

They were first recorded by L.E. McCullough on the 1989 “Feadanísta” album (Bluezette Records, Austin, Texas) – check out the original here – https://lemccullough.bandcamp.com/track/la-polverita-fiera-buscando-la-quinta-pata-al-gato

Personnel: L.E. McCullough (tinwhistle, flute); Rich Brotherton (acoustic guitar); James Fenner (percussion); Pipo Hernandez (charango); Danny Levin (synthesizer).

Original keys: “La Polverita Fiera” in D major; “Buscando la Quinta Pata al Gato” in B minor.

Here’s where it gets fun.

The amazing John Carty recorded them on “At the Racket”, released in 1997 on Racket Records – https://racketrecords.bandcamp.com/track/the-tax-max-mazurkas

His keys (F major and D minor) accommodated the Eb alto sax of the phenomenal Seamus O’Donnell, which allows an alto saxist to play the tunes in the D major/B minor fingering. I did the same when I used an alto sax to record “Fair-Haired Lass” on my “Late Bloomer” LP (Kicking Mule Records, 1984) –
https://interrecords.net/l-e-mccullough-late-bloomer-kicking-mule-records-disc6060897n1.html

My recollection from conversing with John Carty is that he heard the tunes from an unnamed musician who had heard the Feadanísta album … but not recalled the exact titles or maybe never saw the liner notes … and then not illogically dubbing them “The Tex-Mex Mazurkas”. The 3/4 meter does resemble a mazurka though wasn’t composed with a mazurka feel in mind.

And somewhere along the way a musician with a basic Ulster /ɛ/ to /æ/ vowel drop pronunciation got involved, rendering Tex-Mex as Tax-Max. The circle completes!

It’s all pretty wonderful, and I’m thrilled anyone plays these tunes at all. In any key in any style, on any instrument using any name. I believe people even dance to them, which is maybe best of all.

This is how the folk process works in real-time, and I’m honored to have contributed to a small part of it.

On this page, written settings #3 by “coppers and brass” and #4 by “ceili” are the correct original key and notation – thanks!
