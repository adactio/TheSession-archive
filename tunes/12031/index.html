Pοlygοn Pοlka

This tune was composed by Chris Legge, a longstanding member of the Golden Guinea Tuesday session in Redcliffe, Bristol. Although this tune has been played many times in the session (and in others), over the years, it has never been committed to paper, as far as I am aware, and I believe this is the first time it has been transcribed from a live recording at a session.

Chris played it through three times, as is usual, on his D/G melodeon while I recorded it and played along on the fiddle.

When I transcribed the tune it became obvious that there were subtle differences each time it was played, which is what one would expect from music that has never before been written down – a refreshening change from the only too common, and often unavoidable, practice of transcribing from commercial recordings with their artificial perfection, or transcribing printed dots into ABC.

I therefore found I had to make decisions about which version of which bar to use, bearing in mind that the next time the tune is played in session there will be other subtle differences. A delightfully fluid situation indeed, which should encourage other players to use their own initiatives when playing this tune and not to slavishly copy what I have given here.

The sort of thing I have in mind is that I could equally well have transcribed the first 4 bars of the B-part as:
| cA BG | AF G/A/B | CA BG | AA G2 |

The recording was made Tuesday, June 19th, 2012, at the aforesaid Golden Guinea pub.

Enjoy!

Candid vs forthright

So what’s the big fuss? With its two down beats per bar and its coy recension of Charpentier’s Te Deum, did this petty polka really need all the lavish attention and careful socio-historical methodology you wrapped it in?
This sounds harsh, I know.
It’s just that I thought the real difference between ‘oral’ and ‘literate’ wasn’t between ‘played by ear’ and ‘planned on paper’ or ‘off the air’ vs ‘off the sheet’, but between a dynamic, flowing pulse and a square, imperious beat: This tune, no matter how many (polygonal) ways you vary it, has slavish and hokey written all over its genes: It’s trapped in its own bars. One can hear every each beat and bar of it without even looking at the score! (By the way, the 2nd part should probably be written 2/2 and played twice as fast, as a fling?)
If you’re interested by these issues: Ong on Orality: http://www.engl.niu.edu/wac/ong_rvw.html
