Jimmie’s

The first tune on the track “Jimmie’s” by Cassie and Maggie. I can’t find anywhere that lists the names of the individual tunes.

Some of the notes are a bit snappy, a hint of strathspey, but it’s played smooth and flowing, not choppy. I’ve indicated these with slurs as played on the original.

It reminds me of a workshop Sam Sweeney runs in which he explores the variations in rhythm that you can do with jigs (he can play a jig in 4/4 believe it or not and somehow still have it sound like a jig). Moving in-and-out of triplet feel and dotted feel makes this sort of tune a lot of fun.

Re: Jimmie’s

I think it’s too easy to view everything from an Irish music perspective. The terms for different tune types don’t mean the same in different musical cultures because the dances aren’t the same.

I suspect that this tune wasn’t written as a dance tune, but here we are, trying to pigeon hole it into a dance type of another culture. As I said above, “slide” means nothing musically in Nova Scotia. Hornpipes are played with straight eighths - if they are swung they are called “clogs”.

How one transcribes a tune is up to oneself - the transcription is just a guide as to how to play the tune, but there are often several ways of doing it. Is Hector the Hero in 3/4 or very slow 6/8, as Skinner had it?

Let’s just enjoy the tune for what it is and not worry too much about where it fits in our musical catalogue.

↳ Posted by DonaldK on March 9th, 2026.

Re: Jimmie’s

As a final takeaway, and in part response to Jeff, it’s important to realise that in Cape Breton jigs are something that you do jig steps to in a dance. It’s the jig rhythm rather than the jig itself or the length of the jig that’s important. Quite often a dance will finish a random way through a jig (the dancers don’t count the bars), depending on the number of dancers, and so to tell the band they’ve finished the dancers clap to tell the band they can stop.
They’ll use anything with a compound rhythm for jig steps: double jig, single jig, two step, slide, 6/8 march, etc. They’ll all be played at the same tempo with the same feel, for dancing to. So in Cape Breton, for dancing anyway, there is no distinction between all those tune types.

Listening again, it sounds more jig-like than strathspey-like to me. It could be the influence of the accompaniment as well. I’ve transcribed the tune in 6/8 for my own purposes and will probably keep it that way. That doesn’t mean I think it’s a jig, in the traditional sense, but it doesn’t sound anything like the marches you hear from Cape Breton (or Scottish) players, and for me, considered as a strathspey, it’s very soft.
