Forty-three comments

This tune can be played a number of different ways. It sounds quite sinister when it’s played at a flowing, steady speed. It also makes a great, driving session tune when it’s played hell for leather. Try playing it both ways, and decide for yourself which is more fun.

Cum yn Shenn Oanrey Cheh

The two versions I’ve added above are both played on the Isle of Man. The first one is called Cum yn Shenn Oanrey Cheh (Keep the old petticoats warm), which would fit in with the song lyrics posted above. The second version is just called ‘Jig’ in the Clague collection, and isn’t as widely played as the first.

Rakes

Barti Ddu - in this context, and most other tunes with ‘rake’ in the title, it refers to a young, handsome, stylish man. Usually with more looks than wisdom.

Re: The Rakes Of Kildare

This jig is in O’Farrell’s Pocket Companion (c.1810, Volume 4, page 106) as The Humours of Tallow. I found it when looking for some old Munster-related tunes, got attracted by the local name, and soon recognised the music. O’Farrell was an Irishman who appears to have been from around Waterford or east Cork, and his collections contain a number of Cork/Waterford places cited in tune titles. Tallow is a small town just southwest of Lismore, which tallies geographically.

More similar than I had originally thought. The A Part of Rakes of Kildare & B Part of April Storm were obviously quite related, turns out the whole tune is.

April Storm is set in a different key, the details are a bit different, the A Part of April Storm doesn’t loop back into the B Part in the last four bars, but overall, their structure and phrases have a lot of overlap.

“The Rakes of Kildare” is in A dorian mode, which is close to A minor, and you would use a D whistle to play it.. The first setting - Jeremy’s - is near to the way I’ve played it for 50 years. “Mug of Brown Ale” is also in A dorian, so stick with your D whistle.
