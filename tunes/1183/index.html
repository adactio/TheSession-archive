Sean Kelly, X:5

A B hexatonic setting (no 6th where at least one 6th can be heard in the other versions), thus bringing this tune in the high range of flutes and whistles (avoiding awkward high d rolls all the same).
The A and B settings work well, even if they are not usually heard.
“Highed up” settings can be useful in the open air (busking, marching, what not) or when you are not mic’d up and still want to be heard!

Re: The Cabin Hunter

No, “Cabin Hunter” is not at all a standard or recognized English idiom for someone who goes house to house searching for gossip. If you used “cabin hunter” in the US, UK, Canada, Australia, or anywhere else today, no one would understand you. They would assume you meant a hunter looking for a cabin in the woods or someone house-hunting.
BUT !!! When 19th-century Irish speakers translated the concept into English, they didn’t translate the word biadán literally (which would just be “gossip”). Instead, they described the behavior of a biadánaí: someone who “hunts” or drops from cabin to cabin (house to house) looking for news.
This is why traditional tune collectors listed the reel as:

An Biadánaí / The Cabin Hunter

In traditional music indexing (like the Ceol Rince na hÉireann volumes compiled by Breandán Breathnach), English titles for native Irish tunes were often the local Hiberno-English vernacular names used by the musicians who played them.

So while the title An Biadánaí literally means “The Gossip,” the traditional English name attached to that same tune in rural Irish English was “The Cabin Hunter.”
