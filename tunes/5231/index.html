↳ Posted by ceolachan on October 12th, 2007.

*Sigh*

I’ve been through this discussion a number of times…never mind.

I know that they are usually written in 3/4 time, but there are some Mazurkas which come with a really strong shuffle feel. These I write out as 9/8, so people can grasp the rhythm at least a wee bit better.

And I’m not alone with that! I’ve seen other transcriptions of Mazurkas done in 9/8, even in some books.

It’s just like with some Breton Larides 8 temps. Most of them are in straight 4/4, while others (those with a really strong swing) come in 12/8 flavour. The same goes for some Dans Fisel.

These dots can never fully capture the music, that’s understood. But I’ll alway try and get as much of the phrasing into a transcripton as I possibly can without cluttering the sheet.

So.

↳ Posted by Mario on October 12th, 2007.

I don’t give s**t about any conventions, as long as they are just that: conventions.

If I can make life a bit easier for someone who might want to play this (and has never before played a French Mazurka), then I’ll do. Even if it means ditching transcription conventions.

In my book, most conventions belong to some dark and remote place anway, where they can bother no one.

C’est la vie!

“Dark and remote places” eh? So, I see, you have the light and everyone else, or the vast majority of ‘others’, are wrong. It seems I’ve seen and heard it all before, repeating over and over again across history. As usual, those convinced of their own superior ways are generally not open to discussion, whatever proof is put before them. Who is in the light and who is in the dark? I’ve seen 4/4 barndances transcribed as 12/8 in print ~ that doesn’t make it right, anymore than transcribing modal tunes as Major is right, but it keep happening and will continue so… We all have our quirks, and some of us do daft things. It would be nice if someone was doing a search to find a ‘mazurka’ under mazurkas and a slip jig under slip jigs. ‘Conventions’ are guides, usually established over a long time and by many different minds, with experience and understanding. But, there will always be the quirky attempts to redefine things, c’est la vie…

Ahhh … tradition: what is it, what isn’t it? :-)

The magic of an internet wormhole brought me here (blame Daniela Heiderich), and it’s amusing to read so many declarations of what is and isn’t “right” and/or convention with the benefit of a lot of hindsight and a living very close to the source.

It strikes me that there was some talking at “cross purposes” going on, summed up by the comments posted by alancorsini and mario so many years ago:

== You can’t dance a Mazurka in 9/8 - Strip the Willow or any dance with a running step . . . yea, but mazurkas!
== Dancing…come to speak of it: I have to confess that I don’t know how to dance a Mazurka the Irish way - I only know how to dance it in French style. I take it there will be some differences?

In the last twenty years - so pre-dating the start of this discussion - the mazurka as danced in the “trad” community of France has slowed right down and *is* danced to a 9/8 beat. In fact, I’ve heard Irish slip-jigs played in a “mazurka” set, and it raises no eyebrows if the sheet music is written as such.

This rhythm of *this* mazurka, as featured on the Ecoliers album, is still played in the Grand Centre by groups with a harder traditional core and there’s a set of steps that works well with it (mazurka-mazurka-valse-mazurka in the Berrichon version, mazurka-mazurka-mazurka-valse in the Auvergnat tradition) that is much closer to what’s seen in other countries.

In twenty years of dancing bourrées, though, I have never, ever, ever seen or heard of this tune being played in or suggested as suitable for a bourrée (trois-temps). There’s no version of the bourrée that I know that fits or can even be made to fit the music ; if such a one exists, I’d like to see it!

Meanwhile, *this* is how La mazurka des écoliers de Saint-Genest is known (and danced) in France these days:
https://youtu.be/ixjHstaWRsY

The change (some might call it “rot” … ) is attributed to radical new ideas being spread through the Gennetines festival (Le Grand Bal d‘Europe) in the 90s with the finger being pointed in particular towards a “néo trad” movement centred on Grenoble. They’re also accused of turning the Gavotte de l’Aven into a slow and graceful dance rather than the somewhat hyperactive original as still seen in Brittany (some bands will announce it as a Gavotte de Grenoble, which can confuse the uninitated).

Anyhow, it seems like this might all be a bit of an academic argument, as my Dublin-based sister tells me that the local Comhaltas teachers are dead set against any of their students learning to read music, because it’s not “traditional” and if you can’t learn by ear, you shouldn’t be playing at all … !

Re: Mazurka des Écoliers de Saint Genest

Although the debate on the origins of this mazurka seems to have abated 10 years ago (at least on this site), some questions were left unanswered and some answers left unqualified.

So, to dispell the fuzzy mists around this popular tune (everywhere but in Ireland?) here is a quick refresher:
This tune first appeared in its current form in 1983, on the first track of an LP titled “Les Écoliers de Saint Genêt” (referring to the loosely connected band of friends who played on it and saw themselves as the up ‘n’ coming generation of trad. Berry musicians).
Our mazurka is the last tune on track one (from ~2’30“ on the link below) and is simply, perhaps humbly, called ”Mazurka".
It is credited, rightly, to Gilles Chabenat.
(G I double-L E S by the way!)
It only acquired its now stabilized name later, because it was handy and logical.

Although Gilles deserves credit for this beautiful, memorable piece, he built on already known formulas.
The first part for instance was already in the repertoire of “bal”musicians such as the Breton clarinetists Hyacinthe Guégan (b.1926) & Lucien Riou
https://thesession.org/tunes/14257
of the previous generation of musicians.
