Re: Lad O’Beirne’s

Bregolas, be careful what you wish for! ;-) The sleeve notes on At It Again, for track 11, read:

"Lad O‘Beirne’s/Fionn O’Donnel’s
Once again James [Carty, flute] and Francis [Gaffney, guitar] help me with a Johnny McGreevy composition; I heard it played by James ‘Lad’ O’Beirne. The second tune…."

So there’s half your answer—John Carty says it’s a McGreevy tune. Now the question is, what is Mr. Carty’s source for this as a Johnny McGreevy composition? John is careful in his study of tunes and their lineage, so he wouldn’t make this claim lightly.

I’ve learned a good handful of tunes from a fiddler who got them personally from Charlie Mulvihill, and they’re often common enough tunes but “distinct” (as you say) settings. So I wonder if O‘Beirne’s settings were also customized to suit the player. O’Beirne was one of the best fiddlers of his generation, and better players such as himself often can’t leave a tune along. They tinker with it, take it around new bends and over hillocks the melody hasn’t seen before. Yet in a way that finds the essential character of the tune. Bobby Casey, too, did this. In a way that makes me sit up and say, “Yes! *That’s* the tune what’s been hiding in there all along!”

Thank you for an entertaining and enlightening thread buried here in the tune archive!

Lad O’Beirne’s / Charlie Mulvihill’s / Garrett Barry’s in D (Update!)

So I’ve done a bit of detective work to see what more can be gleaned about this reel. I’ve contacted John Carty, Tommy Mulvihill (Charlie Mulvihill’s son) and Jesse Smith who’s a great admirer of Johnny McGreevy’s playing. They were all wonderfully responsive to my random tune history questions! To sum up: nothing is certain but the lack of evidence on either front heavily implies that the tune is “traditional” 😃

What we know:

– Charlie Mulvihill used to play this and the other reel (“Muckross Abbey”) a lot but it’s not clear whether he wrote any of them – Tommy believes it’s as likely they were attributed to Charlie precisely because he loved playing them so much!

– A tape exists of Lad O’Beirne and Louis E. Quinn playing the exact same set of tunes as Charlie’s but with slightly different variations. John Carty and Jesse Smith both learned it from that tape. Of course, Lad O’Beirne (who emigrated to the US in 1928) was best buddies with Charlie Mulvihill (who was born in New York in 1917) and they played at house parties together all the time, so either of them could’ve learned the tunes from the other.

– There’s no recording of Johnny McGreevy playing this reel (that Jesse knows of) or any additional evidence of him being the composer.

– It certainly is a version of Garrett Barry’s (or vice versa, or they had a common ancestor) – everyone agrees with that one!

– According to Andrew Kuntz of the Fiddler’s Companion, Garrett Barry’s is in turn a version of Miss McLeod’s / Mrs. MacLeod of Raasay, a Scottish tune that came from the Isle of Skye. I’m sceptical about this one since to me it sounds like a very distant relative at best – but even that could be confirmation bias due to the phrases of Miss McLeod’s having been so much clichéd in our minds. To be fair to Andrew, he cites Fr. John Quinn on this and other theory, saying that “The Grey Plover” (#759 in O’Neill’s 1001 or #1544 in O’Neill’s 1850) is an intermediate version halfway between Miss McLeod’s and Garrett Barry’s – that could well be true! Here’s The Grey Plover:

https://thesession.org/tunes/20170

In any case, whether you agree that “Lad O’Beirne’s” descended from Miss McLeod’s or that it’s a version of another family of tunes, I think it’s fair to remove the credit and consider it “traditional” – until more evidence of its authorship emerges! :-) Thanks for your attention, this concludes the public announcement, hahaha…
