Bonnie Kate

This tune is often played as a warm-up tune in one of my sessions, and follows on from Harper’s Frolic (recently submitted).
The tune is transcribed from a recent session tape, and the “variations” section is what was actually played by the lead concertina. The preceding section is the “bare bones” of the tune, in which I have stripped out the ornamentation.
Although this tune is called “Bonnie Kate” it is not the same as the Bonnie Kate elsewhere on this database.
Trevor

Bonnie Kate

This “Bonnie Kate” became popular with English musicians after it appeared on an LP by the New Victory Band in the early 1980s. Fidicen’s variations sound very close to the way I play it today and probably come from the same source. “Harper’s Frolic”, which also became very popular with English dance bands, was on the same LP and I’m fairly certain it was coupled with “Bonnie Kate” . Perhaps my favourite from the LP was the 48 bar schottische “The Mountain Belle” which I’ve never seen in print and would love to see posted, if only to find out how far I’ve altered it over the years.
Colin Turner

Re: Bonnie Kate/Harpers Frolick

These two tunes were a staple with our ceilidh band back in the day.

This what my mate ‘Craig Gemini’ dug up about its origins:

“Harper’s Frolick” is a very popular traditional tune often found in the repertoire of folk musicians across the UK and Ireland.
While it is frequently played in “Celtic” sessions (Irish or Scottish), it is actually of English origin. It is a staple of English Country Dancing and is a favorite among melodeon and fiddle players.
Key Details about the Tune
• Type: It is most commonly played as a polka or a hornpipe, depending on the regional style.
• Time Signature: Written in 2/4 or 4/4 time.
• Key: Usually played in D Major.
• Structure: It follows a standard AABB structure (two parts, each repeated).
The Famous Pairing
In the folk world, “Harper’s Frolick” is almost inseparable from another tune called “The Bonny Kate.” They are nearly always played together as a set, starting with Harper’s and transitioning into Bonny Kate.
Origins
The tune gained significant modern popularity through the playing of traditional English musicians in the mid-20th century, particularly within the Southern English fiddle tradition. It has a bright, bouncy, and rhythmic “frolicking” feel that makes it perfect for dancing.
