Up THe Stroods

As far as I know this is a traditional Shetland Tune. I know that there are different variations but this is the version as played on the album Sit On This by the Park Bench Social Club, and this is the version that I play in sessions.

I have heard it played with either extra bars or a bar missing at the end of each b part. I believe that it is played on the Transatlantic Sessions Volume 3 with this variation and also with a G sharp, as if it was in B Dorian.

Re: Up Da Stroods

Yes there’s a recording on Tobar an Dualchais from 1971 of Andrew Poleson playing “Up Da Stroods Da Sailor Goes” but, as you say, it’s a different melody. (I’d just like to say that, for me anyway, 1971 is not that “long ago”!)

The fact is though that, in the pre-internet days, the link between tunes and their titles (and tunes and their settings) was a lot looser than it is now. It can be quite a major undertaking to research the origins of a tune like this one. Even identifying when the setting with the sharpened fifth in the second part first appeared would an achievement.
If you’re really keen to find the origin of this tune you might be best contacting someone like Maurice Henderson or the Shetland Heritage Centre.

Re: Up Da Stroods

Hi DonaldK,
Yup there are different melodies with the same title and different titles with the same melody.
Since the tune originated in the Shetland Islands, I was hoping that there might be some recollection of when the tune started.
I did contact Shetland.org and asked them the question.
BTW, the 1978 recording was “FTX-368 - DA FARDEN BEN DA WYLKOMER SHETLAND FIDDLE MUSIC 50 fiddle tunes, introduced by Tom ANDERSON, including those used for bridal marches & weddings, for dancing & for listening & those influenced by the fishing & contact with the whaling ports of Norway & Greenland.”.
The particular tune I seek was track 9: “9. (a) UP DA STROODS DA SAILOR GOES & (b) SHIVER DE MIZZEN: Tom Robertson (with talk bef) - 1.45”.
Here is what was said about Tom Anderson:
"TOM ANDERSON (1910-1991). Born Aithness, N Mainland, Tom was the oldest of 6 children. His father played melodeon and his mother was the local church organist, so young Tammy learned the fiddle from his grandfather and uncle. In the early 30s he became interested in radio broadcasting and, when war came, he joined the newly developing radar section of the RAF. It was while serving in India that he resolved to devote more interest in his own native music.

After the war, like Peter Kennedy, he started using the first portable tape machine made by Scophony-Baird, and listening to Peter’s weekly “As I roved out” radio programmes. Encouraged by visits from Peter’s friend, Pat Shaw, in the early 50s, he exchanged message tapes with Peter and, while working as a travelling insurance agent started to unearth both tunes and players. In the evening he was playing for dances and performing in a local concert party.

After retirement in 1960 and the death of his wife, Babs, Tom devoted the rest of his life to researching and reviving local interest in Shetland fiddling and encouraging the making and playing of fiddles in schools and in 1977 he was awarded the MBE for his services to folk music."

Re: Up Da Stroods

Yes, Tom was instrumental in the research and revival of Shetland fiddling. He also taught many of the next generation, including Catriona Macdonald. But he’s been gone for more than thirty years.

What I might suggest is finding and analysing all the recordings you can, to see if you can create a developmental map of how the tune has changed over time. But I fear the origins may be lost forever.

On the other hand, we could just enjoy the tune for what it is (or what it has become in its various settings).
