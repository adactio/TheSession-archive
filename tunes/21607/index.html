Four comments

New Langolee

O’Neill included the New Langolee (248) right after the Old Langolee (247) https://thesession.org/tunes/21549 in his 1805 Tunes, because I guess he didn’t know how to upgrade from Langolee 1.0 to the new version. He was still in denial about what a langolee/langolee is.
Tunearch.org/Fiddler’s Companion says:

“It had explicitly sexual lyrics, and ‘langolee’ is supposed to mean ‘the stiff dick’ in Irish. The song (entitled ”Langolee"), from The Festival of Anacreon, seventh edition (1789), begins:

Ye Ladies attend to your juvenile poet,
Whose labours are always devoted to ye,
Whose ambition it is, and most of you know it,
To charm all your hearts, with his Langolee.
Langolee! what sweet vowels compose it,
It is the delight of each fair maid that knows it
And she that does not, may with rapture suppose it,
That Irish shillalee, call’d Langolee."

New Langolee, X:3

Tune of Thomas Moore’s “Dear Harp of My Country” as arranged by M. W. Balfe (Novello).

When I was very small, I read Gene Stratton Potter’s 1904 children’s novel “Freckles” about a disabled orphan of Irish parentage guarding an Indiana timber lease, and finding nature, friendship and love, etc. It exoticizes the Irish in a really maudlin way, but does have great nature descriptions. Anyhow, at one point the hero and his love interest sing “Dear Harp of My Country” together, and I guess I had wondered for well-nigh thirty years at the back of my head what the tune is, but didn’t wonder enough to Google it. The tune turns out to be “New Langolee”, which given the meaning of Langolee mentioned in the above comments is deliciously ironic.

Dear Harp of my Country!
Thomas Moore

Dear Harp of my Country! in darkness I found thee,
The cold chain of silence had hung o’er thee long;
When proudly, my own Island Harp! I unbound thee
And gave all thy chords to light, freedom and song!

The warm lay of love and the light tone of gladness
Have waken’d thy fondest, thy liveliest thrill;
But so oft hast thou echo’d the deep sigh of sadness,
That e’en in thy mirth it will steal from thee still.

Dear Harp of my Country! farewell to thy numbers
This sweet wreath of song is the last we shall twine,
Go, sleep with the sunshine of Fame on thy slumbers,
Till touch’d by some hand less unworthy than mine.

If the pulse of the patriot, soldier, or lover,
Have throbb’d at our lay ’tis thy glory alone;
I was but as the wind, passing heedlessly over,
And all the wild sweetness I waked was thy own!
