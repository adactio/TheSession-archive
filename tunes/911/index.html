Re: Lake House

shanachie,
I am sorry you should leave such a less-than-courteous comment about my perfectly acceptable activity on ‘your’ page (for this website); the posts and contributions on this website are after all, public, and for the enjoyment of all. We are meant to help each other out in our endeavours and struggles with music; it is, after all, a huge field to try to behold or grasp and it is little wonder sometimes, oft-times even, we only half-remember the tunes.
Luckily for me, and for whoever else cares to learn about the similarities between tunes, and like to indulge in some ontological discrimination work between them, I have been able to trace back the tune I mentioned 10 years ago.

Is it possible you might today be more interested in the question I raised 10 years ago simply because I have a clear answer now? If so, check these two links:
https://youtu.be/3bbg9txThhk?t=16
https://www.discogs.com/master/551752-Gwerz-Musique-Bretonne-De-Toujours/image/SW1hZ2U6MTk3ODE4MDQ=

They both reference the same tune, played (in D) by excellent Breton musicians.
The tune is called “An den yaouank studiet” and, as a song, it is well-known in the “Pays Pourlet” area of Brittany (so we learn from the notes to Gwerz’s seminal album of 1986).
I have heard this tune played by Gilles Le Bigot as a “laridé” as well. Its structure is somewhat crooked but that is not uncommon for this type of ring dance.
So now, you know my comment wasn’t all “rubbish”.
The similarity between the first part of your polka and this traditional Breton song is perhaps limited but it is striking.
It may be pure coincidence of course but, if you have ever come near Brittany, or a Breton music concert or ball in your life time, it is possible you unconsciously reminisced this tune, or fragments thereof in your composition.
You alone can be judge of that.
“An den yaouank studiet”‘s melodic contour is far from unusual of course, other Breton and French ring-dances such as ’Sont les garçons d‘Saint-Martin’ or ‘Dans ma poche de gauche’ (a Quebec song) show similarities with it.
In your composition, you have made use of the same phrase in a minor tonality (A) and a major one (G, part two): this simple modal shift gives it, to my ear, a dual flavour: French in the first part and Irish in the second. This is of course my personal interpretation.
Maybe you’d like to tell us more about the genesis of your tune and why you named it ‘Lake House’. Cheers.
