A mix…

could work. Sometimes, people write out pieces in only either the “standard” major or minor and then use accidentals to get the real mode across. If the A major wasn’t a simple mistake (for A minor with F sharps written for dorian, perhaps), the transcriber may have been intending A mix (written in A major, but accidentally forgetting the G nat accidentals!). A major just sounds weird to me without some serious alterations, but A mix isn’t too bad. That being said, I prefer the dorian.

Re: The Green Linnet

For what it’s worth, I found this in an 1873 book of Irish “airs” (but it includes jigs and reels mostly), and it’s A mix in that version. It even puts a natural sign in the G space in the key signature, so I’m taking that as a signal from the arranger. In some places, it uses a C sharp and others a C natural. Those are marked with accidental signs. Fs are always sharp in this version.

↳ Posted by Debbie van Tuyll on April 28th, 2016.

Re: The Green Linnet

This tune is fairly well-known in Scotland as “Glenlivet” - I haven’t found it any earlier than Kerr’s Merry Melodies (c1870s). The similar sound of the titles “Glenlivet” and “Green Linnet” makes me wonder; I’ve long suspected that Francis O‘Neill changed many tunes’ names for his own reasons. His source was James O’Neill, who was extremely familiar with Scottish music. Or perhaps it was a mishearing, one way or the other.

Re: The Green Linnet

For what it’s worth I agree with Nigel, as I think this is originally Scottish. It is played regularly in Scotland and Cape Breton as “Glenlivet” but (I believe) very rarely in Ireland. It also (to my ears) has a more Scottish flavour.

Most of the albums from Ireland listed on this site as containing the tune under the name “Green Linnet” are referring to the unrelated song.
