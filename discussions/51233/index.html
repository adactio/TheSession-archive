Re: Help with transcription please

The transcription in the video seems fine to me, it’s the standard convention for notating a waltz. What I think might be giving you a pause is the fact that it’s played slightly dotted, giving it a subtle continental mazurka feel. But that’s often the effect of a tune played slowly: reels played slowly at a workshop may sound hornpipey, slides played slowly and steadily may start sounding like jigs etc.

If that’s what it is, unfortunately the notation is never perfect, it’s just the skeleton of a tune, and it’s there to remind you of how it goes. In traditional music, plenty of information isn’t normally conveyed in the dots and it’s not supposed to be 100% accurate style-wise simply because of convenience. Add too many details and it quickly becomes unreadable! Of course, you might keep experimenting with various custom conventions and annotations for your own benefit but a public post would probably need to use the existing convention like in the video.

I’ve been there before, feeling unsatisfied with how the notes are grouped for particular waltzes or airs and experimenting with time signatures. But if you try and publish an experimental notation anywhere online, be prepared for classically-trained musicians yelling at you over so much as “erroneously” grouping the off-beat as DGA – for many people, convention is king :-)

Re: Help with transcription please

It’s fairly conventional to notate swung tunes in straight eighths for ease of reading, with the understanding that they are played with swing (you could have a metric modulation on the score to indicate this).

You COULD notate a swung waltz in 9/8 for a more accurate playback, but that’s not really what the notation is for, and the lack of beaming in 9/8 makes it harder to read.

Re: Help with transcription please

Tune transcriptions in ITM and other traditional musics are not complete performance instructions - they are the basic outline of the raw notes of the tune, and the expectation / convention is that the player will use their knowledge and experience to play the vital extra stylistic nuances that the notation don’t include.

They’re also not in themselves performance data for mechanical/ software playback,unless (as in Michael’s example) parameters have been built into the playback software to go some way to providing the “bits between the dots” that the experienced human player would provide.

That’s also incidentally why so many highly skilled classical players sound lifeless and mechanical when they attempt to play traditional musics - they’ve been trained to play the score and nothing but the score, and unless someone creates a score for them with every dynamic and subtle change of timings or emphasis written out, they sound like it.

(It’s also part of the reason popular tunes here on TheSession have got numerous slightly different transcriptions!)

The original transcription looks fine.

I totally agree with sfmans.
Do conventions reign supreme? Really ? Why?
So that the vast majority of people who have taken the trouble (or been obliged) to learn conventions know what is being referred to, and can understand what someone else is trying to tell them.
The word “intelligent” comes from the Latin verb “intelligere”, meaning “to understand”.
In music, it is useful to be able to understand and therefore distinguish a waltz (3 beats, each subdivided into two quavers) from a jig (2 beats, each subdivided into 3 quavers) within a very short time.
We have, on numerous occasions, discussed here the advantages and disadvantages of traditional notation, and the practice of learning traditional music by ear (as simply reading the music certainly causes one to miss out on many very important elements). The two notations proposed on this site are the only two that are sufficiently well-known; in any case, in my view, they are the two “least bad” solutions for sharing traditional music in written form, the “best” of the two being standard musical notation, which allows for the inclusion of numerous nuances. Needless to say, the range of available nuances is still too limited, and it is therefore best to learn by ear – which is what I did (for several ears, sorry, years) before learning music theory.
That said, I’ve occasionally asked some of my friends who are classical violinists to have a go at a few Irish tunes, and not a single one of them managed to play anything other than the notes flawlessly – not quite like a sewing machine, but almost!
But, if the trad music really has to be written down, it might as well be written down correctly, otherwise it risks being read incorrectly and with difficulty. Let’s return to the distinction between a waltz and a jig, namely the grouping of notes:
**Howw oul dyour eact if I wroteit lik e thi sss?**
Difficult, isn’t it ? That’s more or less how some people feel – though I wouldn’t go so far as to describe it as a sense of unease – when they read a jig preceded by a 3/4 time signature, where four quavers have been grouped together…
In other words, you’re being shown green (a jig, green Ireland) when it’s actually blue (… The Bl… Danube)!
