Yes as Tony mentions each beat is subdivided by 3. In music this called “compound time”.

Simple duple time is 2/4, compound duple time is 6/8.

Simple triple time is 3/4, compound triple time is 9/8.

Simple quadruple time is 4/4, compound quadruple time is 12/8.

All of these rhythms exist in Irish dance music.

Re: i need to give a 10 min presentation on slip jigs

Let’s see what an academic book has to say about slip jigs.

In ’Folk Music and Dances of Ireland" author Breandan Breathnach says

"The jig, which is the oldest form of dance music, is found in three forms: the single jig (in 6/8 or 12/8 time), the double jig (in 6/8 time), and the hop or slip jig (in 9/8 time).

The word ‘jig’ undoubtedly comes from Italian, and the musical form itself may also have an Italian origin, coming to Ireland from Italy by way of the harpers.

This idea comes from the fact that Carolan, who was fascinated by the Italian music then in vogue in Dublin, concluded some of his compositions with a piece of 16 bars to which the title ‘jiga’ was attached.
These vary so radically in structure and idiom from the Irish dance music that we may dismiss the idea that one derives from the other.

Moreover, tunes similar to Irish jigs were in circulation in England long before Italian music became popular here.

Melodically the great bulk of Irish jigs are native in origin. The older ones may have been derived from ancient Clan marches and songs. Some few were borrowed from the English, and scarcely any from the Scots.

The Hop or Slip jig is in 9/8 time, the bars being filled by various groupings of triplets of quavers, crotchet and quavers, and dotted crotchets.
Rhythmic differences between types of this jig suggest basic differences in the steps of the dances formerly performed to them.

Petrie expressed the opinion that the Hop Jig evolved from a class of ancient Irish vocal melodies and was unique to Ireland- but the early English printed collections abound in 9/8 tunes which cannot be claimed on any grounds to be of Irish origin."

Re: i need to give a 10 min presentation on slip jigs

Unlike in the video posted above, in Irish step dancing slip jigs are usually dancesd in soft shoes. They require more complex steps than light jigs and single jigs and are meant to show the grace of the dancer. Thus they are part of advanced competition levels, while the other two jig varieties are only performed up to primary level.

↳ Posted by Jon Kiparsky on April 29th, 2025.

Re: i need to give a 10 min presentation on slip jigs

I was going to add the hop/slip-jig distinction as well, if there’s time to cover it. There are definitely two distinctly different ways of playing these tunes, even if there is a lack of agreement about the terminology. Most commonly, you’ll hear slip-jigs taken with a double jig feel, just with the added extra beat per measure. However, there is an older style (to me, this is what comes to mind as a hop-jig) that is felt more or less in one, with a very strong downbeat and reduced second and third beats. These are also played much more quickly and have more heavy-light pairs than triplets. They wind up sounding like very fast waltzes, almost being to slip-jigs what slides are to double jigs. While I mostly associate the “hop-jig” style with older recordings from Michael Coleman or Michael Gorman, I have increasingly seen younger musicians make the same distinction.

It might be a bit beyond the scope of a 10-minute intro, but I think it’s important to cover since these tunes don’t always play nice together in a set; hop-jigs always feel like they want to go faster, but you’ll break your fingers going into a slip-jig with all the triplets written out.

Here’s a good example of the classic hop-jig:

And a more modern set (especially note the transition from the second tune into the reel at the end):
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-ZFpuMruu98

Actually, while we’re on this topic, this may be a good moment for me to share my (probably) unpopular opinion about Slip jigs. Here goes: in Irish Traditional Music there are three different sorts of tunes that get called slip jigs, and they are: 1) Slip jigs; 2) Hop jigs; and 3) tunes with triplets that can be counted either in a slow three, or in six.
I only know of two examples of the third category: Kid On The Mountain, and O’Farrell’s Welcome To Limerick. I would be glad to know of other examples.
Disclaimer - I could well be terribly wrong here. But this is my honest opinion, as a bit of an outsider looking in (I’m a non-Irish player of ITM).

Re: i need to give a 10 min presentation on slip jigs

No, I think there’s a useful distinction to be made between representational accuracy and understanding. One of the most important indications of (and tools for) understanding is the ability to abstract away the unimportant details of a situation. Which details are important depends on your purpose - if you’re trying to do a careful analysis of a player’s ornamentation you might want to start by making transcriptions including every fidget they do, as precisely as you can. But as you see patterns emerging you might re-do the transcriptions to show the ornaments (rather than the individual cuts and strikes or whatnot). But if you’re learning a tune, you don’t want to see any of that, you just want “the bones of it” - you want to understand the tune, you don’t care about the details of how someone else played it once.

So yeah, someone might make a case that some nonstandard time signature is representationally more accurate than the standard one, but that’s probably not a huge help to understanding the tune.

Also, cats are another species that believes in its own superior intelligence, I’m pretty sure. And also I suspect that dogs are another species that believes in humanity’s superior intelligence, which goes a long way to explaining their enduring popularity.

Perhaps it’s worth mentioning that written notation does a mostly adequate job of representing the basic rhythmic structure of a tune, but the real magic is in the timing, the stretching and squeezing of notes that happens as much between the beats as it does on the beats. Notation can hint at this, though in trad music, the notation is often simplified (e.g., writing hornpipes without a dotted rhythm, leaving it implied) for ease of reading. In any case, it’s up to the player to interpret the timing, and that can vary depending on mood, pace, whether playing for dancers or no, etc.

Re: i need to give a 10 min presentation on slip jigs

“Personally I instinctively think the latter….tunes are played in their time signature and strictly so. ”
Well, no. There’s tons of evidence that tunes have been reused in other time signatures throughout the history of this music.

It’s also important to point out that the tunes are played for dances, not time signatures. A strict rhythm and structure is needed for the dances(*), but dances evolve and change and so do the tunes… and vice versa. Also, writing the music down and assigning a time signature has always been secondary to playing the music. But all of those things, dancing to, playing, writing, and reading music have also always fed into each other and influenced each other.

“Are there jiggly reels? Reel-esque jigs? ”
Yes - there are many examples of melodies that exist as both jigs and reels. There’s also an argument to be made that single jigs and flings are closer together than they are distinct… And many flings get played as hornpipes, reels, jigs, and slides. Single jigs, slides, and regular double jigs can often be transformed into each other. Slides and polkas also often get changed into each other, and there are examples of polkas and hornpipes (in Sliabh Luachra) being closer together than you’d expect.

“Rocky Road To Dublin is better described as a hop jig rather than as a slip jig”
It is and can be played as both.

"3) tunes with triplets that can be counted either in a slow three, or in six.
I only know of two examples of the third category: Kid On The Mountain, and O’Farrell’s Welcome To Limerick."
These are both completely standard slip jigs. Sometimes musicians take standard tunes and turn them into pieces by slowing them down and adding lots of variations, elaborations, and ornamentation, but they’re still examples of standard slip jigs. A better third category might be 3/2 hornpipes.

I believe that the current general trend in Irish music of having distinct hop jig and slip jig categories is relatively recent (50-100 years old), and certainly it’s clear from written records that the terms were interchangeable before then (and most likely regional). The slower, elaborate slip jig rhythm is possibly influenced by the competitive step dancing, where the much slower pace is needed to accommodate the elaborate steps. That said, I do believe that different rhythms or feelings for specific tunes existed prior to that time period, but the reality is that the size of the repertoire of slip and hop jigs has always been quite small, the habit prior to the 1900s seems to favor single tunes, rather than sets of tunes, and so it seems likely that triple time tunes got lumped into the same category (regardless of “time signature” or underlying beat subdivision), whether you called them slip or hop jigs (or something else altogether). We know this is definitely true in 1820 anyway, see Thomas Wilson’s Companion to the Ballroom.
