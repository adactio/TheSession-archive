Lapses of taste …

There are numerous examples of the really exceptional traditional musician or singer who begins to dabble in other forms. Some give it up as a bad job and return to their roots. Others never come back to their roots at all.

The thing that strikes me about a lot - not all, by any means, but certainly the majority - of such “experiements” or more permanent transitions - is that much of the new stuff which they choose to do is extremely bland.

So you have the case of the innately, instinctively talented player whose musical radar as regards traditional music is spot-on, who communicates that music perfectly with her/his audience, who genuinely excites the trad audience, sets new standards for interpretation and performance, etc. He/she begins to delve into other forms and the product is dull, middle-of-the-road, unadventurous, tiresome, lowest-common-denominator, bottom-drawer rubbish.

Why do people do it?

Do they see the journey from being a genius in playing/singing traditional music to being an inept performer of other forms of music as some sort of evolution?

It makes me sad and angry that some people choose to waste their true gift by chasing after something else that may appeal to more people, but which they have no real aptitude for and which has little or no artistic merit.

Anyway … there you go. My rant for the day …

Re: Lapses of taste …

I see it this way: creative musicians have to change and grow musically during their musical lives by branching off into something different. Otherwise they’d get bored. And if they’re bored, then their audiences will also be bored, because it’ll be “the same old boring ___ whose tunes always sound the same”.

It’s this simple: the early work of a musician appeals to your taste. Their later work is different and happens not to appeal to your taste. However, it will appeal to other people with different musical taste, whereas the earlier work would not appeal to those people. Solve your problem by listening to that musician’s earlier recordings and don’t bother buying the new ones. It’s not a “lapse of taste” it’s just “changing tastes”. Theirs changed, yours didn’t.

Re: Lapses of taste …

There are two directions I can see which can cause lapses of taste. One, where it is fun to experiment with other styles, and it can certainly enrich your technique as a musician. If you’re a fiddler, you can enjoy Gipsy, classical, jazz etc and learn a lot from playing those tunes, but if anyone wants to hear these styles, they would be better off hearing the original musicians rather than crossover versions. The other direction is a result of collaborations with musicians from other musical backgrounds, I’ve found those with eastern or asian music very disappointing indeed. Of course, these rules have exceptions: Edgar Meyer and Mark O‘Connor, for instance, are such superb musicians they can do anything they like and sound great. Or Aly Bain and Ale Moller from Sweden, who chose not to blend Swedish and Shetland music but played a selection of traditional tunes from each country and produced a superb record. But I’ve heard bluegrass mixed with jazz and found I did not like the ’compromise’ result, I’d rather hear one or the other separately. And while the Afroceltic sound are fun live, their recorded music is so bland I can’t bear listening to it at all.
As for those playing traditional music with an amplified, synthesised or rock n roll feel, they can get lost

Re: Lapses of taste …

Trevor, I don’t think it ever got recorded but there is an organ processional by Richard Purvis called March Grotesque, which is an example of nose-thumbing, though at a different kind of employer.

When you hear it without knowing the story, it just seems a thumping good processional march with a few quirky twists and turns which winds up to a conclusion which is a bit hard to describe but it has a definite kind of “splat” moment right at the end.

The story related by the organist I heard perform it is that Purvis, as cathedral organist, was forever being given a hard time by his bishop who was extremely heavy, took a long and quite repeatable time to process down the nave, and tended to collapse - “splat” - in the throne at the end of this long stagger…

(Nothing to do with Irish music or magic moments, apologies for the digression!!)
