How the Ancients Named their Tunes?

NOTE: MY QUESTION[S] follow the examples.
This is a General Question with some specific speculative examples and I’d like historical insight please for I’m doing an album with narrative on a sort of Celtic Music Appreciation of oddly named tunes. The examples?:
Hag at the Churn sounds more like NAG at the Churn and I speculate that PERHAPS it was named thusly because of the jangling hagging & nagging “A-G-A-D-D” motif which reiterates like a nag!
#2] Stool of Repentant, most unrepentant--as is the victim is imagining all the playful mischief he‘’s planning while sitting on the stool?
#3] As Session Member Will Harmon so aptly wrote: “When Sick is it Tea That You Want?” Anyone whose Grandma gave them whiskey to douse a fever or sooth a sore throat knows that this is a rhetorical question…
#4] Comparing the melodic structure of the “Protestant Boys with ”The Catholic Boys" I stretch far into the Speculative Universe, I admit and make judgments (right or wrong) about the carefree simple nature of the Protestant Tune with that of the more Complex Modal austere Catholic one! (sorry…)
Because there are 100’s of interesting titles, I limit myself to those involving sinners or saints.
MY QUESTION[S]
Since I’m elderly and don’t have time to do years of worthy research, I’m seeking the considerable combined wisdom of Session Members. I am sure that you have thought of this and I would like to have your input on the general naming of Celtic tunes, please?
For example, thousands of people and places have been memorialized by the naming of a tune after them? But what of “Oh those Britches Full of Stitches?” and “Queen of Sluts?”
Oh my! :-o
(Specifically, I have an idea how “The Pope’s Toe” may have been titled. Any thoughts on this?)
Also, I’m looking for two people to hire with authentic Celtic Brogue--no fakers please!--in the DFW area where I live to record them on the album.
Thank you, Wise Ones!
Patricia Nash

Re: How the Ancients Named their Tunes?

“Witches” is a translation of the Gaelic word buiseach.

“John Gregorson Campbell, in a brief etymological exploration of that word, argues that it is similar to the words baobh and baoth. The former means, “a wicked, mischievous female who scolds, storms, and curses, caring neither what she says or does, praying the houses may be razed and the property destroyed of those who have offended her.” The latter means weak or foolish. Dwelly’s Gaelic-English dictionary supports Campbell’s analysis. His translations of baobh include wizard, a “wicked, mischievous female who invokes a curse or some evil on others,” a “foolish disagreeable female,” and “madness, wildness, furiousness.” The dictionary contains similar words that suggest madness as a possible translation: boaghal, baoil, and baois. Other possible translations for baoghal are foolishness, stupidity, wildness, and dangerousness, and other possibilities for baois include lewd and lascivious.”

The negative connotations of the word ‘hag’ are manifold, and are all based on the fact that an unpropertied, unmarried, elderly woman was at the very bottom of the social scale in any English speaking culture when the word was in popular usage. The translation of ‘cailleach’ into ‘hag’ also reflects the bias of the middle class native English-speaking Irish men who predominated in academic translation from Gaelic to English in the nineteenth century.

It is worth remembering that language is the primary cultural marker, because it determines which processes of higher cognitive function are availble to any individual, as well as their expression. This is the reason the Gaelic Revival in nineteenth century Ireland was such an integral part of the anti-colonial nationalist movement. It is also an uncomfortable reality for many Irish native English speakers who are unable to think in Gaelic, and are, at least unconsciously, aware of the fact that they will never be, truly, linguistically, Irish in the most crucial cultural sense.

This is one of the reasons why Gaelicising English words like ‘crack’ as part of a marketing strategy that taps into the institutionalised Anglophobia of English speaking Irish culture has been so successful. It also allows other English speakers, for example the English, Scottish and North Americans, to partake in an invented ‘Gaelic’ culture without having to engage any further in the linguistic culture of Gaelic Ireland.

Since you asked, my take is this:

1) “Old languages have shades of meaning, many”. No, ALL languages have many shades of meaning.

2) “English is extremely practical, functional, built for quick, everyday conveyance of meaning…to get the job done…NOW…Ok?!!” and “illogic-intolerant”. So in that case why do people say “excuse me but I don’t suppose you could possibly do me a massive favour could you and open that window just to let a bit of fresh air in, would you mind? Sorry. It’s just it’s a tad stuffy in here isn’t it?” when you could just get the job done by saying “please open the window - it’s too hot”.

3) I agree with you for the most part about translation and meaning. The words “cailleach”, “hag” and “witch” all have different but overlapping semantic domains. I don’t know whether or not a native Irish speaker would view “cailleach” as having many shades of meaning. I suspect that most modern Irish speakers WOULD say that, because those speakers also speak English and can view Irish “from the outside in”. In ancient times when people were monolingual in Irish, there would be no “clutter” from English concepts, so to an Irish speaker a cailleach would simply be a cailleach, end of story. But what about “witch”? Even in English, “witch” does not have to mean a green-skinned, warty old woman with a pointy hat and broomstick. It can also conjure up images of past centuries in which villagers burnt perfectly normal people at the stake just because they were a bit suspicious for some reason, like they had fits or a mental illness or something and so were thought to be possessed. What about the Wiccans as a different shade of meaning? I met a Wiccan woman last time I was in London. She called herself a witch and was a bit eccentric and regularly frolicked naked with her witch friends, but she didn’t have green skin and a pointy hat. Then there’s the added complication of English as a world language. English is widely spoken in places like Nigeria and Kenya where people still hunt so-called witches down and kill them. When I think of the word “witch”, that shade of meaning also springs to mind, sad as it is. I don’t know what they call these witches in the African languages, but, like it or not, it’s translated as “witch” into English, so that concept is added to the semantic domain of “witch” in my head and the heads of many other people who read the world news. So you can’t generalise that Irish has many shades of meaning whereas English hasn’t. Both have very different vocabularies which express different concepts and which specialise in different areas (e.g. English has a huge science and technology lexicon), but as a whole it all balances out and you can’t generalise - you can only say that both languages are of equal value to us all as human beings.

“HAG AT THE CHURN. THE (Cailleach ‘sa Mhaistrim). AKA and see “Dinny Delaney(‘s) [1],” “The Maid at the Churn,” “The Merry Woodsman.” Irish, Double Jig. D Mixolydian. Standard tuning. AB (Mallinson): AAB (Sullivan). A highly regarded pipe tune, according to the Bothy Band. Caoimhin Mac Aoidh says a correct translation of the Irish title would be “Hag in the churn.” This refers, he maintains, to the folk superstition that witches would inhabit a churn to steal butter. They could not abide this particular tune, however, so it would be played as a ward when the chore of churning butter was done. It was a terrible and telling mark if a woman left the house during this ritual. See also the related “Church Hill [2].” Breathnach (CRÉ II), 1976; No. 43 (appears as “Gan ainm/No title”).
