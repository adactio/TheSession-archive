Re: Chords for Am tunes?

The choice of chords depends on a lot of factors beyond the tune itself, which of course is the most important consideration. Other factors would include the density of the playing (how many players and how much variation is going on - if three fiddlers are all playing different variations and harmonies, the guitarist would do well to keep as simple as possible, but if they’re all sticking close to one another rhythmically and harmonically, or there’s only one of them, the guitar can be more adventurous), the mood of the player (sometimes you want to stack extensions on the D major, sometimes you want to echo a piece of the tune in the guitar, sometimes you want to drone on a D for a while), the patience of the melody players (sometimes they don’t want the extensions, or the echo, or the tune’s not in D). In other words, unless there’s a written, arbitrary “right way” to accompany a given tune (ie, a local session tunebook), assume that the harmony is pretty open, and it’s not so much a question of playing the right thing as of not playing something wrong. A tune might at one point be ambiguous between, say, Em, G, and C - but F would be wrong.

Now, for Am and A dorian (most “A minor” tunes are closer to dorian, so watch those F naturals), the chords available within the mode are as follows.
Aminor: Am, B dim, C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am
A dorian: Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F#dim, G, Am

With the sevenths, those would be
A m7, B dim7, Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7
and
Am7, Bm7, Cmaj7, D7, Em7, F#dim 7, Gmaj7, Am7

This is not to say that you won’t find a place for an E major, or something like that that’s outside the mode, but these are a good starting point.

I hope that helps - I’m not sure that I’ve really got at your question. If you want to know more, your next step would be to start to understand the construction of scales and chords, and then to move on to understanding modes. None of that is very difficult, and it’s sort of necessary for talking about how to accompany a tune.

Re: Chords for Am tunes?

What Jon said. It’s no wonder you’re sick of the Am-G-F progression. An F chord will work in an A dorian tune but the trick is grossly overused, and a lot of the time it doesn’t work, so you have to know when it’s appropriate to slip it in. Think of Am, G and D as being your basic structure and then you can substitute C for Am, Em for G and Bm for D, altho’ D is a stronger chord so you’d probably use this less often. This is what Jon said but simplified slightly. Use the odd F or Dm but like I said, don’t overuse it. It’s not like you can just transpose exactly what you’re doing with Emin tunes where you can use that Em-D-C thing without ruining the tune. The dorian mode is a whole different kettle of fish. Once you’ve got the hang of backing in A dorian, transpose what you’re doing and practise backing E dorian tunes. Then try some D dorian tunes, just using those same chords transposed. Then try some G dorian and B dorian. All you have to do to be able to tell A minor from dorian is try and establish whether you can hear F sharps or F naturals. If you have F#s, the tune will sound a bit more “major” than one with nats, even though they’re both minor scales. Often you’ll get a mix of both. That’s your job - to decide whether it’s predominantly #s or nats, or whether you want to or have to respond to both.

Re: Chords for Am tunes?

Ha ha, I remember that quote as well !

If your looking for what other chords can be used other than the same old three, it’s often helpful to think of minors or dorians as being relative to a major key.

ie you know C Majors harmonized chords are C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am and BDim
(ie all minor except “three chord trick ones” and 7th is dimished)

And Aminor is the relative minor to C (6th note in scale), so the same chords apply.

Similarly ADorian is relative dorian to G (2nd note in scale) so the same chords that apply to G major scale apply to A dorian, ie G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em and F#dim.

I know Jon those are the chords Jon pointed out already, but I think thinking in terms of “relative to” a major scale is an easy way to view modes.

So FMaj7 idea should take into account the mode the tune is in.
Also be aware that accidentals can occur and tunes don’t always stay strictly in a given mode.
