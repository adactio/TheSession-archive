Felt like playing my gazoo for an hour today and said I would grab the first cd that I found that had a few reels and jigs and play along or stop when I came to a nice tune and play that for a while. It has a lovely mixture of well known tunes and some uncommon but nice tunes.Now I cant read music and that is sometimes a disadvantage but in my case I believe if I had read music from an early age it would have completely changed my approach to this music. My suggestion is that I would reccomend those that feel they are limited to learning by notation to throw away the books for two months and pick one cd with loads of tunes by musicians who inspire you and absorb it completely. Bring this cd in the car with you and sing along with every track . Have it on when you are going for a walk ,making love(might be better not to sing along here), going to sleep. Every chance you get for two months. Remember to sing and lilt along with the tunes. After the first month play along and concentrate hard on the notes and not the flowery bits. You might never retrieve those books.

Re: Sort of an addition to dots v ear thing

Hi Tab,

I’m interested in this subject, I have a few thoughts for you.

I don’t think it is a good idea to listen to one CD over and over again for months, or weeks or even days. For one thing it can just spoil the music for you, it loses its freshness. Maybe its ok to do this with music you don’t really like so much, as a way of getting the basic tune into your head, but that doesn’t take months.

I have recorded myself, playing the fiddle, and learning a new tune by playing along with a recording. It’s very interesting to compare the recording of just me playing, and the recorded mix of me and the other player which is pretty much how it seems to me when I am actually playing.

When I am playing with Bobby Casey I just sound fantastic to me, weaving in and out of his playing, adding fresh accents. When I listen to the recording of us both playing I still sound ok but I can see why he is a more highly regarded fiddle player than I am.

When I listen to what I am actually playing, it sounds embarrassingly bad, terrible, horrible.

And then next, when I try to play the tune on my own without Bobby, I find I can’t remember it, somehow I haven’t been learning while I was playing along. It seems I only learn in a permanent way if I have to actively produce the tune myself.

Singing is one way to do that, and sometimes I listen to a tune I want to learn at bedtime, then try to sing it over and over in my head. Sometimes it works, sometimes I just go to sleep.

But it’s probably better to just learn the tune on the instrument you are going to play it on. What I do then is listen to the recording, and then try to play the tune. When I get stuck, I listen to the recording, or I might look at the dots if I have them, just to remind myself. Once I can play the basic tune on my own I stop listening to the recording for a while, a few weeks maybe, but I have found recently that it is a good idea to go back and listen again to make sure you haven’t gone too far away from the original, and lost something good.

One more thing. The latest tune I wanted to learn is Noel Hill’s setting of the Pigeon on the Gate. I already know two distinct settings of the tune, and I find it very difficult to forget those and learn Noel Hill’s very different version. It’s also difficult to play, he jumps all over the place on the concertina in ways that don’t come naturally to the fiddle.

So it was great to find that somebody here had gone to the trouble of transcribing NH’s playing. Thanks Gian Marco. Looking at the music laid out visually in dots is another way of absorbing its structure, and in this case it is a big help in identifying the unexpected notes he plays. Really I should be doing that now instead of this.

As I’ve said before, if you really want to read the stuff off a page, nobody here can stop you. But you’re not going to convince anyone that sheet music is a good way to go. Either they already believe it (and have learned to keep mum :) ) or they know from experience that it’s not. Or they know from experience that those of us who know from experience that it’s not aren’t going to shut up about it, and it’s really just as well not to provoke us. :)

Really, you can learn tunes from sheet music, it’s possible. And once you know the music you can even learn to play them well! BUT, you’ll learn to play the music much better, much faster, if you throw away the printed sources and rely on your ears. You certainly won’t know the music well enough to play well from sheet music for a long time, and not until you know a lot of tunes and have played them for a good while, but tunes you learn by ear will sound right pretty quick.

Take it from me, I wasted a lot of time with sheet music before I realized that I wasn’t getting anywhere. How much time do you want to spend treading water before you start swimming?

As Will says, there’s other music that you can use to learn reading. For example, Wohlfahrt’s violin studies are excellent for mandolin practice, I can only assume they’re good for fiddle as well.

>> “I think Will underestimates the role of recordings in the transmission of tunes. Big Time.”

I think people keep missing Will’s point. Recordings may be good for the transmission of tunes (big time, even). But they’re somewhat lousy at transmission of the music. A recording is a fixed thing, so in some ways, it’s almost as rigid as a piece of sheet music.

When you’re first starting out, it’s easy to listen to a recording and think “this is the way that Kevin Burke plays this tune”, and use that as some “authoritative” source for learning how to play like Kevin Burke. But then go find another recording of Kevin playing the tune. Chances are, he’ll be playing it completely differently.

Yes, a recording can be really useful for learning a tune, as can the dots. But it’s only marginally better than the dots at teaching a newcomer how to play the music, because it’s still rigid. And if you’re listening to the same recording over and over again, you get the rigidity fixed in your mind. Learning to lose the rigidity is a big step in a new player’s development. The sooner that you get them past thinking and playing rigidly, the better.

Of course trad musicians learn tunes from recordings. We all know the impact Coleman’s records had when they were brought to Ireland--fiddlers learned his sets, approximated his style. Of course it’s handy to have access to so many tunes.

All I’ve tried to do here is point out the downsides--the same ones Hurler cites.

Similar to how televison and the internet have led to a decline in face time in our communities (See Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community). The ubiquity of electronic media is gradually diminishing “learning at the knee” and other interpersonal and community qualities of this tradition. Here’s where I see it most:

1. Step dancers using canned music. It’s become common that many step dance schools ONLY use canned music. Sadly, their pupils are incapable of dancing in time and with life to live music. I know this first hand as well as seeing it elsewhere.

2. Community events using canned music, even when experienced, competent Irish traditional musicians are available. This one’s not as widespread as #1, but I have seen it happen. As Hurler points out, being a traditional musician means being asked by the community to play for community gatherings. I get such requests fairly frequently, and almost always say yes, rarely for pay. At a recent fundraiser for the local domestic violence family shelter, we provided background music. One of the local step dance schools also performed--to canned music. In other cases, I’ve seen people use canned music because they weren’t in touch with the local Irish trad community and did not realize they could ask local musicians to play in person. But worse is when they choose canned (including Corrs, Celtic Women, etc.) over live *because they have no concern for the relationship between the music and the people, the community.* Sad.

3. Players who *do* in fact get stuck on learning from cds, and whose repertoires *do* sound like a replay of the latest Lunasa or Coleman recording. (leoj, pay attention here) Ever since Coleman, there are scads of people who *do* in fact play the Tarbolton Set just as Coleman did, week in week out. Ludicrous? Yes, but sadly it does happen.

4. Learners who bring a recording device to a workshop, turn it on, and then leave the room, when a living breathing incarnation of the music and the tradition is right in front of them. Or simply not paying attention because they plan on just listening to the recording later. I’ve seen this happen, at nearly every workshop I’ve attended or taught. Not only is this rude, but it reinforces the mindset that the people matter less than the tunes.

5. Players who actually do *prefer* recordings and slow-downers for learning tunes over learning face to face. This one is truly troublesome. I’ve heard the reasons and excuses from folks everywhere, including widely hear on the mustard board. Some folks say they prefer recordings because it saves them the embarrassment of being slow to learn in front of others. Some people say the recordings and slow-downers are better for catching the details and nuances. It’s not just me saying this. At a workshop last month here in Montana, James Kelly ranted (just a little) about people not putting the time into learning in person and at tempo--he said that skillset is an essential part of being a traditional musician, and other (good, experienced) trad musicians expect no less.

All of these trends are, to some degree, changing the music and the tradition, the sense of community. I’ve never said that they are so harmful as to threaten the music’s existence (as the Prof seems to think I did). But I do believe we’re seeing some erosion of worthwhile values that were once at the heart of this music. In some places, and for some people, it is less about community that it used to be. And that’s unfortunate.
