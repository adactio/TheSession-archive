Using abc notation to simulate a session set

In an attempt to simulate a session that I can practice to, I’ve been experimenting with and reading the “Guido Gonzato - Making Music with ABC PLUS” manual. The abc code I’ve put below (ignoring the headers for setting fonts etc) will play each tune 3 times and then on to the next tune in the set. To make this work, no tune can have any internal repeats in it - you have to copy and paste as required and do away with abc code for repeats such as “|1 notes :|2 more notes||” and so forth. The beginning of each tune will have a “|:|1-3” where 3 is the number of times to repeat and only the final line in each tune gets a repeat symbol “:|” . Another item that does not work in this scheme are parts call outs such as P:A etc. They all have to be removed or commented out. “X:” fields for the second and third tunes likewise have to be removed or commented out. There can be no blank lines within the context of the set although you can have the equivalent of a blank line if it has “%%” in it.

FWIW, in the first tune, there is a “T:” line to simulate the “%%sep” abc call as you can’t do that inside the title and header area but I wanted to delineate the set tunes from the first tune title and that’s what works for me. If there’s another way to do this, I haven’t found it. It’s not something that’s necessary, I just like the appearance of the set titles this way. A %%scale .62 keeps it all on once sheet. Only call out the Q: in the first tune unless you have some reason to change meter later. I chose three slip jigs at random for the experiment.

Note: I’ve been experiencing random weirdness at the end or beginning of a tune regarding the chord being played by the midi portion of my abc environment (EasyABC). It’s harmless but it is annoying. Kind of like someone at a real session glitching it and you just keep playing!

This is the way I made it work for me. Other abc applications may yield entirely different or perhaps useless results. YMMV.

**************************

I:abc-charset utf-8

%%titlefont Times-Bold 24
%%subtitlefont Times-Bold 18
%%gchordfont Times-Bold 14
%%composerfont Times-Italic 16
%%textfont Times-Italic 16
%%partsfont Times-Bold 14
%%printtempo 0
%%printparts 1
%%partsbox 1
%%bstemdown 1
%%withxrefs 1
%%pageheight 10.75in
%%topspace .15in
%%botspace .25in
%%titleformat T , R-1 C1

X:1
%%titlefont Times-Bold 20
%%scale .62

Re: Using abc notation to simulate a session set

Thanks for giving some background about why you would find this necessary - fair play to you… I have a few more observations:

1) I don’t have to find someone to record.

One of the best things about this music is the social aspect. This music is just as much about the personal interaction, and having something special in common with other people, even if you’ve never met them. Finding someone that you can record can be part of this. Don’t shy away from it because it’s more work than setting up a machine to do it for you. If I get a little time, I’ll record the set for you, or someone else here could as well. I don’t know Garech’s Wedding, so I would have to learn it.

2) The abc score is a framework. I just use it to practice to while I learn/add ornamentations and so forth while I’m playing.

The ABC score IS a framework, and CAN be a useful tool in this music. However, the fact that you are putting together a set and using it for practice means that you’re using it for more than a tune framework. If you’re playing along with it, then you’re relying on the computer for the rhythm. If you’re using a player with “stress programming” (the programmable rhythm where it can play notes with a bit of swing, which I believe EasyABC does now) then maybe it’s not the worst thing in the world. But it is still going to be way too rigid, without any phrasing of the music, and nowhere near as useful as a recording of a human.

3) I view YouTube videos for inspiration and nuances and so forth as there are no musicians locally.

Listening for inspiration and nuances is good. But playing them is how you learn to do it. And it’s much easier to get it right if you’re playing along with a recording of a person who does it right, rather than trying to do it right yourself while playing along with a machine that can’t provide that nuance.

An old, rather cantankerous, poster on this forum would have immediately latched onto the fact that you said there are no musicians around you, and would have asked why you wanted to play the music if you had nobody to play with. But I will take another tact, and commend you on having such a love for it that you want to learn the music despite the fact that you have nobody to play with. That’s where a forum like this one can be at least somewhat useful, in that you could use the community here as your resource to players who can help you. I had people to play with when I was first learning (and they would not have allowed me to try to practice sets by putting them together in an ABC player and playing along ;-))

4) I have a phobia about recording so I just don’t do it. That’s sort of weird but that’s just who I am.

It’s not all that weird, trust me. A lot of people have what is affectionately known as “red light syndrome”. It is something that I struggled a lot with too. But I will also say that it’s a really good thing to do. You don’t have to let anyone hear the recordings. But you can really get a good perspective on your playing by listening to a recording of yourself. It can be pretty amazing how different it is than what your brain thought it was like when you were actually playing, and it can help you understand what you’re struggling with.

I generally don’t like listening to recordings of my playing, at least right after I recorded it. I can spot every little blip, and remember what I was thinking at the time, and everything negative seems magnified. But if I record something, and then let it lie for a month before I listen to it, I have a completely different perspective. Occasionally, someone I know will be playing a recording that sounds pretty good, and I ask who it is, and they tell me that it’s me. Ha!

So recording yourself is a good tool. Don’t shy away from that tool either!

5) I can change the tempo to go really slow while I learn a tune. Since I’m playing with a cast on my left hand at the moment, that’s somewhat useful. Damn cast gets in the way though…

Yikes! That does sound somewhat daunting, I hope you’re healing well!

In this day and age of technology, there are a myriad of ways to change the tempo of audio recordings without changing the pitch. That can be another great tool for learning, because you can slow a fast recording down and actually hear all the ornaments and such. One caveat I have here is that a fast recording slowed down is different than the same players playing the music at the slower speed. The computer slow down is linear, and everything slows down and stretches out. So a fiddle roll played fast and then slowed down is rather stretched out and elongated compared to a roll played by a fiddler at the slower tempo. So that’s something to be cognizant of, when you’re listening to the nuances. But it’s still an extremely useful tool.

So Quicktime Player for Mac, Windows Media Player, Amazing Slow Downer, AnyTune Pro, and all sorts of other software can do these things for you (some easier than others, like ASD and AnyTune, which have this as their main purpose). Getting a moderate tempo recording is great, then, because you can slow it down or speed it up without too much audio distortion.

That would be a much much better way to practice. :-D

Those are some great observations Reverend, thank you very much for your input. I should have made it a bit clearer that in slowing the music in EasyABC, I’m slowing the tempo. Notes don’t get stretched like an analog tune slowed down, they just played at a slower pace. It’s useful when you’re first learning a tune. It’s also useful if you’ve been told you’re playing something too fast and it should be slowed down. Generally, slowing down a tune isn’t difficult but if you’re playing something where the dynamics of the playing change because of the physical properties of the instrument - like the hammer bounce on a hammered dulcimer - then using the slower tempo can be useful while you adjust the muscle memory for the hand move. I have gone into abc scores, made them represent exactly what was on a printed version of a tune complete with all of the trills, rolls and ornamentations only to find that when they play back - they sound very little like what is depicted in the score. That’s the nature of the the abc2midi portion of the software. Until I hear something recorded live, I’m stuck with listening to the abc output long enough to get the feel of the tune. I’ll practice it until I know it and generally interpret how I feel it would sound by someone actually playing it and try to do likewise. I can read notes on a score now but I’m not good at reading the note lengths/timing so I “cheat” and fall back on the abc stuff when I can’t seem to get it quite right. Here are a few abc2midi tricks.
The set title is input as a header. The chords aren’t included, nor the R:slip jig stresses. I pared everything down to the bare minimum.
This sample uses AB *parts* but they are hidden in the printout,
http://abcnotation.com/wiki/abc:standard:v2.1#pparts
http://abcnotation.com/wiki/abc:standard:v2.1#information_directives
This code isn’t using the actual parts in each tune, instead each tune *is* the part (A, B & C).
‘Fig’ is repeated 3Xs. ‘Butterfly’ is played twice. ‘Garech’s’ 3Xs, then ‘Fig for a Kiss’ ends the set ~ 1 time through.
