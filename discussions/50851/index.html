Re: Different styles of Celtic music?

Speaking of fiddle reels, I guess the most apparent difference would be in the emphasis placed on the off-beat (note 3 and note 7 of the bar) as well as syncopation or the lack thereof. Think of it as a litmus test: start playing the off-beats too heavy or too weak for certain styles and this will immediately be flagged as a faux pas by traditional players.

Québecois and Cape Breton fiddle reels are both heavily syncopated (when compared to Irish and Scottish styles) and they both have “pushier”, more aggressive off-beats that suit the local style of dancing. Of the two, I perceive the Cape Breton style as more snappy (not just in terms of “Scotch snap” but audible bow stops) and the Québecois style as more driving (pushing forward) – but that’s me comparing reels played specifically for local step dancers. Disclaimer: I’m not intimately familiar with these styles and others may offer a more nuanced comparison!

Irish and Scottish fiddle styles are *very* diverse and can hardly be lumped together as two uniform styles. However, there are some perceived qualities of the reel playing of prominent fiddlers that are common enough to be named. Of all things, I would again focus on the style of playing the off-beat in reels.

Irish fiddle players place a great deal of importance on “the lift” – the elusive quality of playing that doesn’t have a single definition. To me it’s the art of placing an implied emphasis on the off-beat without actually forcefully emphasizing it (much less so when compared to the North American fiddle styles). This can be achieved by a combination of speeding up the bow, exerting pressure on the string and careful timing (of both the downbeats and off-beats). Despite all the differences in regional styles, most Irish fiddle players have a certain degree of “the lift” and as such a steady subtle pulse on the off-beat.

Not so much with “classic” Scottish fiddle players. While there has been a lot of cross-pollination between the neighboring styles over the last century and modern players can play however they please, I feel like Scottish fiddle greats have always prioritized steady downbeat and clear, powerful, uniform tone of the notes as opposed to the off-beat dynamics in reels. So the off-beat is not necessarily pronounced in reel playing although individual fiddlers may choose to add more swing / pulse / lift etc.

To sum up, where danceable *fiddle reels* are concerned:

– Québécois style has a heavy off-beat emphasis and plenty of syncopation, the overall feeling is driving, further propelled by the constant tapping of feet (also: lots of double-stops!)

– Cape Breton style has a strong off-beat emphasis and plenty of syncopation, the overall feeling is snappy, further reinforced by intensely syncopated backing (also: plenty of speeding-up medleys such as march-strathspey-reel)

– Irish styles have a subtle off-beat emphasis, steady flow and no syncopation, the backing is relatively steady and ideally brings out the lift put into the tune by the fiddler (reels commonly played in sets with other reels – also: lots of different ornamentation techniques)

– Scottish styles may or may not have noticeable off-beat and often have little to no syncopation in reels (unlike in strathspeys), there is a clear emphasis on the downbeat and the tone of individual notes which feel relatively uniform (also: reel medleys are common, no obligatory syncopation in reel backing)

This is my take as a fiddle player submerged into Irish fiddle music – others may disagree! Obviously you can find exceptions to any of the statements above. Hope that still helps :-)

Re: Different styles of Celtic music?

I think Bregolas has it right, it’s instrument-specific to a large degree.

I spent many years around fiddlers from various traditions Irish, Donegal, Cape Breton, “mainstream” Scottish, Scottish Highland, Shetland, Quebecois, Appalachian (specifically West Virginian) and in most cases you can tell them apart just by seeing the way they hold the fiddle and bow.

And there’s a variety of regional styles in Scotland, Ireland, the USA, probably everywhere.

Then with accordions the instrument itself is specific in many cases, with the Irish B/C box, the older Scottish big button accordion, the newer Scottish piano accordion, the Quebecois melodeon, and traditions that boxes aren’t really part of.

More craziness with the pipes! There are three different popular species of Scottish pipes, Irish uilleann pipes, the traditional Breton pipes and the modern Breton piping using modified Highland pipes, the piping traditions in northern Portugal and Spain, a recent revival of Cornish piping, and so forth. (You did say “Celtic”.)

Then there are various banjos, plectrum instruments, flutes, whistles, percussion, etc.

You are correct in saying that these playing styles are related - but none of these styles is Celtic. The instruments commonly used to play these styles (e.g. fiddles) were not available to the Celts. The ancient Celts played mainly brass instruments, such the Carnyx, the Celtic horn and the Celtic trumpet.

Although Irish Gaelic and Scottish Gaelic are both Celtic languages (Goidelic branch), it doesn’t mean that traditional Irish and Scottish music is also Celtic.

Re: Different styles of Celtic music?

@CreadurMawnOrganig, fair point, melody players and backers might have different concepts of what “syncopation” or “syncopated rhythm” is. I mean it in the most basic sense (from a melody player’s perspective):

= unexpected placement or shifting of rhythmic accents that interrupts the regular flow of a tune

Key words for me are “unexpected” and “shifting”. If you play the same accents in every bar of the tune I think it would sometimes be meaningless to call it syncopation (though some might!) In fiddle playing, syncopated bits are usually interspersed between phrases with steady beat as an element of surprise.

So, this is a reel bar played straight:

fedf edcB

Same but with regular accent on the downbeat:

!>!fedf !>!edcB

Same but with regular accent on the off-beat:

fe!>!df ed!>!cB

In reality, it’s often some combination of downbeat and off-beat accents that varies depending on the player but is consistent between bars. So we end up with:

ONE-two-Three-four ONE-two-Three-four
or
One-two-THREE-four One-two-THREE-four
etc. etc.

The players usually called it lilt / lift / pulse / swing etc. etc.

Now to introduce a bit of syncopation:

fed!>!f edcB

We could modify the tune for an even stronger effect:
!>!fd2!>!f edcB

And now for some really syncopated feel:

!>!fed!>!f ed!>!cB

The latter is the introduction of a basic 3-3-2 bowing pattern – but with accents placed on every “one” of the pattern which interrupts the grid of the tune’s downbeats or offbeats. Put plainly, it’s *1*23-*1*23-*1*2 or ONE-to-three-FOUR one-two-THREE-four

This is where bowing patterns and rhythmic patterns get confused in discussions (they’re not the same!) Fiddlers use 3-3-2 bowing pattern in all sorts of places – but only in certain fiddle styles it is considered cool and normal to *shift accents* while using it. You might get some really weird looks if you suddenly start syncopating your reels on the fiddle during an Irish session. Or not, depending on the participants’ idea of fun and how serious you are. But it’s just not part of the Irish tradition – the norm is sprinkling in melodic variations and ornamentation against a steady rhythmic flow and consistent lift. Different rhythm-keeping strategies lend themselves to different styles of playing. This is how I would go about trying to describe the difference in styles of traditional dance music – get down to the most basic level of rhythmic patterns and compare!

Re: Different styles of Celtic music?

@CreadurMawnOrganig, when talking about strathspeys, I think you’re referring to the beats in the backing, such as in traditional piano accompaniment – which when played in a specific context (Scottish Country Dance etc.) could indeed be strictly limited to the downbeats and off-beats as in PAMM-pam PAMM-pam so as not to confuse the dancers following the downbeat.

What I’m referring to is the rhythmic structure of the melody itself. The “Scotch snap” is a textbook example of syncopation (a short note clearly followed by a long one) and it’s often sprinkled in irregularly, not following a specific pattern:

A2 a2 g<e g2 | e>dBA GAB<G |

(g<e, B<G = Scotch snap = clearly syncopated passages)

Dotted rhythm is a more general term that describes plenty of traditional tune types – hornpipes, for example, would often be described as having a dotted rhythm – so it wouldn’t be something that makes the strathspeys unique in the eyes of most people.

The Scotch snap, on the other hand, very clearly breaks up the regular dotted rhythm of the tune – and that’s the reason I brought it up earlier to contrast the strathspey playing with the reel playing. There’re other terms used for this which I’ve honestly never heard a traditional musician utter, what matters is it’s a type of “syncopated rhythm”:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lombard_rhythm

Moving on to the playing of reels in Cape Breton and Quebec, they contain plenty of syncopated passages which are way subtler than the “Scotch snap” used in strathspeys – but these subtle rhythmic shifts are used all the time by modern musicians. You only have to compare the way an Irish or a Scottish musician would play a well-known reel to the way a Cape Breton or Québécois fiddlers do it.

Sophia previously posted a few videos with young Cape Breton fiddlers and you can hear that pattern very clearly in the reel played here:

https://youtu.be/i4dO7Imah0E?si=Z7I4-s82ZGrGT3H_&t=237

This is Tommy Coen’s or the Crosses of Annagh reel – a version that is almost identical to the one played in Ireland if you were to look at the “bare bone” dots alone. What clearly tells it apart from the Irish (or Scottish) styles of playing are the constant shifts between the straight and the “jazzed up” rhythm inside the tune. Listen to the endings:

ea a/a/a a2 ga | bgab gedB | GABd gedB | GABG A4 ||

A fairly straight reel rhythm in the first two bars (but with heavier accents):
ea a/a/a a2 ga | bgab gedB

And now it gets funky:
!>!GAB!>!d ge!>!dB | !>!GAB!>!G A4 ||

They are shifting the downbeat accents = syncopating the rhythm for these bars. You’d rarely meet an Irish-style fiddle player who’d start alternating between the regular flow of a reel and a slightly syncopated rhythm. Cape Breton fiddlers do it all the time – and that in my view is a major defining feature of the style. I’m sure there are more things that someone could point out :-)

I guess you could also try describing the difference in terms of the “swing” (on a microscopic level) but counting the subdivisions of the notes would get way more technical for a forum discussion!

Re: Different styles of Celtic music?

I’ve never quite understood the debate over whether you should use the term “Celtic” to describe music or not. It’s always seemed a bit silly to me.

All terms to describe music - “classical,” “modern,” “Latin American,” “traditional,” etc. - are inherently imprecise, and if you try breaking down every definition, you’ll run into contradictions and gray areas. But that doesn’t mean we don’t use the terms. If a term is useful and conventionally used to describe a certain type of music, it’s fine to use that term. This is true even if the historical origin of the term is nonsensical.

In the case of “Celtic,” that term is used all over the place to refer to Irish and Scottish music, and it often (but not always) refers to Breton, Welsh, Galician, and Cape Breton music as well. Tons of people from these regions who play this music refer to it as “Celtic,” so there’s nothing wrong with using that term. It’s particularly useful given the similarities between these styles of music.

And even IF you want to get into the nitty-gritty particulars of how that phrase came to be - which, as I mentioned before, is NOT necessary if you’re going to use the phrase - then you’ll find that there’s nothing ahistorical about using it either. The aforementioned regions were occupied by the Celts or descendants of the Celts. The Celts had influence on all of their music, either directly or indirectly. They also had influence on these areas’ cultures, and music is downstream from culture. Therefore, there’s nothing ahistorical about calling the music “Celtic.”

You might as well complain about people using the term “Latin music,” because “people in Latin America aren’t from the Latin tribe” or “the Latins didn’t directly affect their music,” or “they don’t even speak Latin today,” or some other such nonsense. The truth is that nobody cares, because everyone knows what is meant by “Latin music,” just as everyone knows what is meant by “Celtic music.”

Re: Different styles of Celtic music?

Here’s my two cents on the difference between Irish and Scottish music:

1) The keys of A major and A mixolydian are more common in Scottish music. The keys of G major, E minor, and D mixolydian are more common in Irish music. The keys of D major and A minor are about equally common in both. (Cape Breton is like Scottish music, but there’s a lot of E major as well.)

2) Scottish tunes (especially reels) tend to have a lot more syncopation than Irish reels, but they are also more likely to be written mostly in highland-pipe-range (meaning they only span an octave plus one note). They’re also more likely to be mostly pentatonic. So overall, Scottish music often gives you fewer notes to worry about, but it tends to use those notes in ways that are more rhythmically and melodically complex than Irish music.

3) Scottish music has strathspeys and strathspey-style marches, which are nonexistent in Irish music. So there’s another whole genre you have to learn if you’re a Scottish musician. The flipside is that polkas and hornpipes are very uncommon in Scottish music, so you’re mostly off the hook for those.

4) The Scottish music scene has WAY fewer flutes, and it has fewer whistles as well. And of course, the pipes you use for it are different (small pipes or border pipes instead of Uillean pipes).

For instance, ITM reels are mostly made up of phrases of four bars of four beats (counting a beat = four fast notes), whereas with Quebec reels the number of beats per bar can vary more.
