thesession.org utilization

I was wondering if you know people who personally use the tunes database on this site. It seems like in this day and age there must be musicians who rely solely on the internet for music - is that really very common, or do people still get settings from books, or are most still learning by ear? By this stage I almost never get a setting out of a book anymore, if it’s something friends of mine are playing. I still like to trawl through books for the fun of it, but that’s a different matter. Certainly if you wanted to learn most anything played in sessions you wouldn’t need to go to the bother of purchasing tunebooks anymore.

Also, when I see a flawed setting for a tune here it makes me wonder if these bad settings are being used, with attendant dire results. In the past people have complained to me about this, not understanding that the settings in comments are often superior to the one initially posted, which of course was the only one you could view as sheet music without the aid of an external ABC editor. But since the makeover Jeremy did for the site we can view all the settings as sheet music if so desired.

In a way submitting a tune here puts a bit of a chip on my shoulder, too - tens or even hundreds of people are checking out things I transcribe for this site. And that goes quintuple for the truly prolific among you. Are Mark Dow, Will Harmon, and Gian Marco among the great collectors of Irish music? ;) Are we among the major forces in trad these days, whether for good or bad? Seems like some recognition is due. Or not - is this all just baseless conjecture, and the internet is still dead last as a resource for sheet music, or is sheet music just not much of a resource itself? Writers in the past have sure made a big deal about it, but then those doing the writing were usually collectors themselves.

Re: thesession.org utilization

I like to try to play stylistically and idiomatically, and I’m not at all consistent in the way that I learn tunes. Half the time, I learn directly from a good recording. But one of my favorite ways is to pick up an unstylized “skeleton” version from a place like The Session, get the notes in (maybe correct a few, if they don’t match what I’ve heard of the tune previously), and then go back to the recordings. The real advantage of doing things this way is that I know the “skeleton” version as the root, and this makes it much easier for me to switch between different fiddle idioms (i.e. Clare style, Scots Highland, Cape Breton, etc).

When I learn directly from the recording, I usually learn quite a bit about ornamentation or articulation in a particular style, but the thing with the tune itself is that putting it in a different setting requires almost as much work as learning the tune originally. It’s quite a bit easier if I have skeleton version down already. (This might be a musicianship thing though, maybe as I become more stylistically well versed, it will become easier to go from one stylized version to another? Time will tell…)

I live in rural Ireland in a well known stronghold for the music, in a community where trad is a commonplace. Is everyone within ten miles of us a musician, a dancer, a singer? Of course not, trad is and always has been a minority interest, even here. But does trad form a part of nearly every event, celebration, party, wake? Absolutely. Local GAA team (lads in their teens and twenties) win and there’ll be tunes, and songs and stories. Several of the team play. Why wouldn’t they, all children start learning to play at the national (primary) school. Christening of a child who’s family aren’t musicians, someone’ll turn up with a fiddle, another will be sent home for the box. We play tunes named after local musicians, sing songs about events from over a hundred years ago that happened within sight of my farm. We dance sets that our parents, and grandparents danced.

My family has played trad in this townland going back at least three generations. I myself have played for close to fifty years and taught for close to forty. What is it you think I’m inventing?

That most trad musicians don’t actively use this site? Surely you can’t argue with that.

That I’d be surprised if one of my neighbors own a tune book? Why would they? All learnt to play as children at the knee of family or neighbors. All can lift a tune by ear, as can all trad musicians round here.

That the beauty of trad is the people rather than the music? That’s my belief and I suspect the opinion of most every trad musician. It’s not really something you can accuse me of inventing.

That more people play trad then ever before? Again, are you seriously disputing this?

For me trad is a living, breathing part of my culture. It tells me who I am. It binds me to the land and to the people, past, present and future. If it doesn’t do that for you, so what? It’s an art form, and people respond differently to art. Doesn’t make one better than the other. Just different. Funny how most children get comfortable with difference at about five or six.

“Do most trad musicians eschew reading music completely, then?” No, obviously that’s not true at all, and nothing I’ve written would suggest the same. Setting aside the straw man. Even with the “wink” suggesting some trad musicians are illiterate is in poor taste. Shame on you.

Just because one doesn’t own a trad tune book doesn’t mean one can’t read music. There’s at least one complete set of the scores of Beethoven’s quartets in one house a stone’s throw from me, and I’m pretty sure someone else has a couple of scores for Wagner’s operas. Both trad musicians, both almost certainly without a trad tune book. I had a very extensive classical training, conservatoire and all, as did my mother. As did very many trad musicians here in Ireland to varying degrees, more so in recent years with the growth in third level ethnomusicology courses.

Of course plenty of musicians have taken tunes from O’Neill and CRE and other written sources over the years. I’ve taken tunes from Goodman myself. But I suspect few get most of their repertoire from books. Why would they when there are live musicians around them? It’s an oral tradition. Oral, not aural. It’s never really been about learning a tune by ear, that’s at best a poor substitute. The ideal is to learn at the knee, to hear the story of the tune. My grandfather spoke about having at least three versions of every tune: first the tune as taught to your teacher, then the tune as your teacher played it when they taught it to you, then the tune as you play it now. None of that is recorded in the database. But names are, by Golly, they are! Yet no-one I play regularly with cares much for the name of the tune, it’s whatever it happens to be called at the moment it’s played. Looking up a tune in a book or online? It’s not stamp collecting or bird watching. I’m not interested in “capturing” tunes or ticking lists. I want the human touch, the connection.

As to waiting, why the rush? Marinade in the fragments you have, wonder about the missing pieces. Let the tune seep into your soul. Then the pleasure of making it complete. All linked to a human connection. Memorable, meaningful.

It’s difficult to make a CD that stands out from the crowd so using resources from the likes of ITMA to tap into the old “new” makes sense.

You’ve got some very odd ideas. I’m not sure all musicians do, or ever have, consider(ed) O‘Neill’s “The Bible”. Even within Ireland, the wider trad community is surprisingly heterogeneous. For example, round here, Coleman is still considered by my father’s generation and earlier to be a clown, a music hall performer, not a “real” trad musician at all. Perhaps because we have a line of musicians that we can trace back (or at least believe we can trace back) to before O’Neill, recordings from America were given short shrift. Or perhaps it was because they weren’t playing our local music. Sessions, though not unison playing, started here in the thirties. The local parish priest was a renewed fiddler and dancer so the Public Dance Halls Act wasn’t quite the issue it was elsewhere. Why waste your wages on a book, when it’s on your doorstep?

From what I know of it the highland pipe tradition was a purely aural one for many years if not centuries. Tunes of the old tradition were passed down using Canntaireachd, a sung language which reflected the tune in all its forms, melody, timing and ornamentation. This in part shows the importance of learning by ear to the tradition as it is a damn site easier to remember a tune than to play it for sure, it also highlights that even the most stalwart of players need an aid memoire sometimes too.

The explosion in pipe playing and musical participation in most of the European forms though came with the ability to write a tune down and to share it unheard. In fact such was the power of manuscript music in popular culture that the charts of past years did not measure the popularity of a recording but that of a particular manuscript. Recordings have rendered such goings on to the history books. I can only wonder whether this has resulted in a per capita increase in the amount of musicians in this world or just x factor contestants and rappers.
