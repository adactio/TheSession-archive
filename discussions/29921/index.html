Re: The Irish Get All the Breaks

On the flip side (John J. and minijackpot) it’s a chance to say things like:

A session in public is a performance, like it or not, in the sense that any rendering of a piece of music is a performance. If someone can hear you, then you have an audience. “Performance” does not necessarily mean egotistical showing off.

You should use whatever means are available to learn the bones of a tune, be they sheet music, midi files, slow-downers, or whatever--keeping in mind that the only way to really learn this music is to absorb it by ear, and make the nuances and rhythmic pulse part of your being. Eventually, if you stick with it, you will be able throw away those crutches.

It takes practice to play any sort of music well, and that means work. You can’t just pick up an instrument, flail around at random, and make any real progress.

It is important to be patient with beginners who are impressed with inferior imitations of Irish traditional music, and try to see if there might be some way to lead them to a better understanding of this music.

It is good to know what you are talking about, but the best teachers are the ones who take a positive approach. It’s easy to snipe at someone’s naivete, but not constructive. You want to make friends for a tradition that you claim to support, not enemies.

As for the bodhran beaters who don’t even try to play another instrument--well, it isn’t a tune, is it? (Hey, I’m no saint.) ;-)

Re: The Irish Get All the Breaks

‘There is no excuse for bluegrass, played by anybody.’

I guess there’s nobody from Kentucky on this board.

Well, the whole ‘Bringing it All Back Home’ project (BBC TV series from 1991 with the soundtrack released on three LPs, later reissued by Hummingbird in CD format) was all about the suggestion, posited by Philip King and Nuala O’Connor (the makers of the series) that there were intrinsic links between the traditional music of Ireland and the music played in Appalachia.

Whatever the case, they’re can be no denying that country music has long been popular in Ireland, even if it’s a cloyingly, bastardized version of the sound one might encounter in West Virginia.

This year there’ll be the 21st annual bluegrass festival at the Ulster American Folk Park, a few miles north of Omagh (http://www.nmni.com/uafp/What-s-on/Events/-bluegrass) and my own town of Mohill has its own festival in the same month of August (http://www.mohillbluegrass.com/).

I can’t recall where I read it, but some years ago I came across an article which suggested that from Kerry all the way up to Malin Head and from Stranraer to Barra and beyond, there was ‘a natural leaning’ towards country music because 1) those were the records which returning migrants brought back home with them, and 2) the inhabitants of those areas along the west coasts of Ireland and Scotland naturally looked further westwards because everything to the east had always brought trouble.

It all sounds a little far-fetched, but there has to be some kind of reason why country music (with the songs translated into Irish and new songs written in similar mode) remains so popular in Connemara.

Re: The Irish Get All the Breaks

I was reading a book lately called “The Music of the Other” which I found quite interesting. One article in it talked about the “The Paradox of the Concert, or The Evocation of Tradition”.

One of the things that strikes me about the question, is somewhat related to it.

The musical performance is consumed and judged in different ways. For those with a knowledge of the music performed, it is often judged on its musical merits. But even in this case, this judgement are based on the knowledge, tastes of the listeners. So what somebody thinks is great could be equally dismissed as brutal by somebody else.

On other occasions however, the performance is also consumed as an indication of what it manifests, i.e its society of origin and its value.

Not sure if this adds to the discussion or not! But I think it explains a lot of the absolute crap music that does the rounds abroad under the ‘Irish music’ banner.

Similarly abroad, and to those outside the tradition, being from Ireland can confer legitimacy and authenticity to performance…
