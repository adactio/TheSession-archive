How is Civility Maintained?

Recent events have left me wondering…how do you maintain some semblance of civility at your session?

When someone insults other people, refuses to apologize or even acknowledge the offense, and then continues this behavior week after week, doesn’t this ruin the craic and drive the good folks away? How do you deal with it?

A session I used to participate in a lot once had a leader--in fact, the very person who’d started the session--who would pull people aside for such ugly behavior and give them a yellow card. If they didn’t straighten up, then he told them to not come back. But that leader got tired of being the guardai, and it took up too much of his time. So he let the uncivil stuff ride, let the participants duke it out amongst themselves. And sure enough, lots of good folks left. And the ones who stayed resorted to shining a spotlight on the incivility, and trying to bully the incivility out of the few troublemakers, but that just brought things down to an unpleasant level.

So is it possible to keep a session civil and welcoming and fun without some way of barring the door to persistent jerks? And whose responsibility is that--the founder’s, the leader’s, the whole circle?

“And whose responsibility is that--the founder’s, the leader’s, the whole circle?”

..then I’d suggest that it is the leader(s)’ responsibility. The founder and leader might be one and the same but it’s quite possible that the founder is no longer part of the session or too busy to assume an active role.

Many sessions(Some of you might argue most) don’t have a single leader. However, there’s probably a core of regulars who will keep things going and guide newcomers etc. One or all of them should take responsibility for the situation, in this case.

If it’s really egalitarian, then the whole circle or members thereof have equal responsibility to face and deal with issues as they occur. I doubt if you’ll ever get a situation where everyone is as equal as that. Some of the personalities will always be stronger and not all will be regular attenders.

Having said all that, I believe that we all still have a responsibility in these situations, whether there is a leader there or not, to behave civily and politely “pull up” people when they are out of order.

Re: How is Civility Maintained?

Uh oh, the ‘all-for-one’ solution and the call for the ‘egalitarian’ approach, the idea that ‘anyone’ might take up the ‘gauntlet’ and deal with a given problem ~ all sharing ‘responsibility’. It has been my experience that mostly that just doesn’t work.

The few times I’ve seen it happen, where there’s not focus, meaning no ‘core’ to the session, that is a few folks who are the ‘seniors’ and have the ‘last word’ ~ well, I’ve never seen it handled well at the end of a few pints or shots. One particular member I remember, recent, took it on his own to deal with an irritation, not a big one either, by telling them to F-off! They did, and they haven’t been seen since. This ‘group’ of hijackers hadn’t a clue, were session virgins. If that session had a centre, or heart, that little tight group of regulars that keep it together, then maybe the newbies would have been approached from the start, introduced to a few folks, and gently nudged into line with what the session had in the way of expectations and courtesies.

That wouldn’t be the first time I’ve seen a thing handled badly from the ‘who’s-turn-it-is-now’ idea of organization. What usually comes of that is a long spell of nothing, until the irritation grows to the point that someone blows ~ and it ain’t a pretty site. Those kind of delayed reactions don’t tend to be diplomatic or kind. They might be entertaining, for the moment, if slightly scary, but they aren’t a sign of health, and they don’t, in my opinion, lead to contructive ends…or beginnings…

The best settings I’ve ever had the pleasure of being a part of had some kind of leadership, and usually, still speaking about the ‘better’, that was more than one person, even if there was a titulary head of state. I favour at least three or four, and that might include spouses. At least then, if you’re really being wound up, discussing it with a few others, and having their support, can diffuse a potential blow up or rudeness. Most of us, just guessing, aren’t happy with being presented with a need to act, to tell someone to temper their ways or leave ~ but sometimes you find yourself in that situation. At least if you’ve got a few others who share the same hope for what your session or activity is about ~ well, it is easier to keep balance when there’s more than one carrying the dream.

I remember one sesh that had a list of other sessions and they openly shared that. Having that ‘choice’ made things a bit more open and fluid. If you wanted loads of reels and little else, there was a place for you. If you wanted things relaxed and with more chat between sets, there was another place you could go. Mind you, there might not be a choice for everyone, but you could always suggest they consider starting up their own session if their ways are out of synch with yours…

Sorry, I’ve been here, it is never easy, at least not for me. I’ve always felt like shight afterwards, even when others might be saying I was in the right, whatever that means. The last think I would ever want to do is close a door on someone’s passion for the music, but I have had to ‘redirect’ a few folks. It wasn’t because I wanted to, but because no one else would and they came to expect me to deal with problems. And yes, we had a ‘team’, a core of four in the centre, which includes spouses, and then an inner circle around that.

I can think of a couple of events that started out nicely and ‘egalitarian’, lacking ‘focus’, that got ‘taken over’, and eventually died off… But I’ve also see that happen when the core was one and they suddenly left, for whatever reason. It is best to talk openly about what you want from your session, to keep it in focus, to share the identity and hopes. But, it is better if only a few reasonable people have the responsibility to deal with ‘problems’…

Chat is the stew that holds this tradition together, music is the seasoning…

Re: How is Civility Maintained?

We have something a little like that near Bristol. Our fiddle tunes workshop teacher hires a room in country pub for an evening once a month, and the object of the exercise is to go through the repertoire of tunes we’ve been taught, so that pupils can identify those that they haven’t learned properly (or not at all if they weren’t at a particular workshop), and improve the playing of the tunes they know.
Typically, the teacher will start off with a set of three tunes that everyone knows, at a moderate pace to get us warmed up. Then she’ll ask each of us in turn to suggest one or more tunes taught at her workshops to go on a list she has in front of her.
She’ll either start a tune off, or the person who suggested it will. The tunes are usually played at a moderate speed (but not slow) because the players present come from her three weekly fiddle workshops - beginners, intermediate, and advanced. Occasionally, a set of tunes will be repeated at normal session speed for the benefit of the more advanced pupils.
We start at 7.30 (after first getting drinks from the bar next door, of course!) and we’ll play through until about 10.30, with a short break at halfway. There are around 20 pupils, and I reckon we get through at least 60-70 tunes during the evening.
What I’ve described is an enjoyable structured teaching session (and none the worse for that) in a controlled pub environment, and, of course, no problems with undesirables since we are in a private room - a mediaeval stone room with 2-foot thick walls, a massive open fireplace and very good acoustics.

Cee… hope you can make it someday you’d be very welcomed. The sessions you describe in Ireland are like the ones I’ve seen as well. But I think this thread isn’t about session styles as much as it is about interpersonal problems between the musicians. This is something I haven’t seen much of if at all. The only instance I can think of was back in the early 90s when a very formidable woman who played piano came in and began shoving people, (while they were playing,) away from the piano so she could put a chair there and play. There were two problems: 1) the session was wedged tight on the occasions where she did this and there was no room, and 2) she was very heavy-handed and would drown out any guitar or bouzouki players who were already there prompting them to pack it in and leave. I was asked to take action by both the people being physically shoved while sitting and playing, and the back-up players who were canceled out once she showed up. There were also musicians who preferred the guitar and bouzouki back up and they would complain as well. I knew I couldn’t allow her to continue physically shoving people around, but I didn’t want to shut her out either.

She couldn’t shove me and was forced to ask me to move instead… or rather, she demanded I move. But this opened the door to talk about it and I took her aside and explained that the session was wedged tight and there’s no room at the moment. I suggested she get a drink, visit with friends and wait until someone leaves and we can make room for her then. But she had no patience and instead went straight to the publican and lodged a complaint. Luckily he could see what was happening and basically told her the same thing I did. That didn’t stop her though; she then launched a propaganda campaign that lasted for months and told anyone that would listen to her that we won’t let her play in our session. As you might expect she did this without explaining the details and circumstances. Of course this had no basis in truth, but she managed to find people here and there that believed her regardless of what the truth was.

Eventually she alienated herself from just about every session around town and relocated to another country. What did I learn from this rare and unfortunate incident? I’m not sure, but I’ll just say I’m glad I’m not the host of every session at that pub anymore. I think the fact that there are multiple hosts takes the pressure off of any individuals to deal with whatever might up. But luckily these sorts of events don’t come up very often. The one I just described was the only one I can think of in the 19 years I’ve been involved in hosting sessions at our local pub. I think the credit for the lack of interpersonal problems between musicians goes to the strength of the ITM community in our area I suppose. I feel fortunate to be part of it.
