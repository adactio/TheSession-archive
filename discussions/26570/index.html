Music in Belfast and Derry

I would like to know to what extent musicians in these cities are playing music normally associated with the other “side”. To an outsider like me it seems really confusing: on the one hand there seems to be more of a willingness to mix peacefully and join together in different activities including music making, whilst some others seem to be more deeply entrenched and are really emphasizing cultural difference in terms of language and music. The “peace” walls in Belfast seem to be getting longer and higher all the time. Is it true that catholics generally play jigs and reels in pub sessions and protestants go for fife and drum marches in a non-session environment, or is that just a huge over-simplification? I realise that there are musicians who play both, but is this actually common practice in these communities? What I mean is, for example if a protestant sat down in a pub session in a catholic area and started playing orange march tunes on whistle just because those are the tunes they know, what would be the reaction these days? Would it always be seen as provocation? Or do those tunes sometimes get adapted for other instruments and played in sessions regularly? Also, do fife and drum players ever play Irish jigs and reels, or does their repertoire only ever consist of Scottish sounding march tunes?

The impression I get as an outsider is that there are some protestant musicians who play jigs and reels on instruments normally associated with Irish sessions and others who are fife and drum musicians who play for marches and who don’t play jigs and reels, but nothing in between. Is this actually the case, that you’re either one or the other? Irish session musicians, on the other hand, seem to me to be much less bothered about the tunes they play since a lot of the repertoire in Ulster consists of Scottish tunes anyway. Is this universally true in Belfast and Derry or are there some session musos who refuse to play certain tunes because they are too “marchy” or reels which sound particularly Scottish?

“Then you go back the Donegal Gaeltacht and they know more, and far better Scots tunes than us …”

Och Aye, but michty me, they just cannae play them properly! :-P

But seriously Dow, we’ve had a few marching band Fife players joining our session up here, fairly regularly, for the past 10 years & there has been a genuine mutual sharing of tunes. After all, when there are no stupid feckin’ words attached, sure they’re all just grand wee tunes. ;-)

Obviously the Fife players who have joined our session have been open minded & forward thinking, so they haven’t objected to any of our Irish tunes. However, that is not guaranteed to be the reaction we would get from all present, were we to go down to our local Orange Hall & play them today, although I understand that until the 60s, Ceilis were regularly held in many of those same halls & folks danced to all sorts of tunes & never batted an eyelid.

On the other hand, I have been in Irish sessions where Nationalist types have scoffed & muttered in disgust & refused to play a tune like the Soldiers Joy, just because it had the word Soldier in it, but then you get rude, backward thinkers anywhere. :-(

Most sessioneers I’ve come across up here, during the past 20 years, have been a pretty ‘healthy’ mix of Protestants & Catholics & very rarely has any Tune ever caused any offense.

Songs, on the other hand, are a completely different story & many’s the time some utter buck eejit has launched into some dopey feckin rebel rubbish, but usually those twits get no further than the first couple of lines before someone with a bit of wit gets them to wise up!

To qualify the above though, it has to be said that I do live in an area which was, relatively speaking, fairly quiet throughout the Troubles.

In any case, there has always been a strong element of Scottish music played by the local musicians in this area & it has to be said, I’ve never ever heard more marches played in sessions than I have around here.

It may interest you to know that in Bushmills, which has traditionally had a population of 99.999% Protestant, I’ve had a weekly session going here, for the past 12 years.
In fact, I even put on a trad music festival there, back in 2005.
http://www.causewaymusic.co.uk/cdf06.html

… & in case you think we just played Scottish Music on Dulcimers, & hid away quietly in a corner, we actually played a mix of Irish & Scottish music on a trailer on the main street.

Kenny himself called in to the Bushmills session for a few tunes, one year on the way home from Miltown & I’m sure he’ll confirm the fact that it’s not nearly as scary as the time a bus load of Rangers Fans suddenly wandered into the middle of our Aberdeen session, many years ago, each one wearing a T Shirt with the logo ……. Fife tunes like the Boys of Belfast and The Rollickin Boys from Tanderagee are played lots in Maddens Belfast by huff-puff flute players like Michael Clarkson, Brendan O’Hare and Harry Bradley and Desi Wilkinson when they lived there.

The trad music/sessions in Belfast have gravitated towards the nationalist areas and pubs because the unionist politicians and protestant clergy have nearly succeeded in wiping any sence of Irishness from that community. Their grandfathers and their fathers before them had no problem with their Irishness and it is only the recent banging of the sectarian drum (1950s to 1970s) that has alienated protestant/unionist communities from their Irish traditional music. In turn it can look like the nationalist communities have hi-jacked the trad scene. They just embraced it. protestant/unionist communities are now seeing the cultural attraction to the music their forefathers played however they are now notoriously associated with the military music past and orangism.

The recent interest of all things ‘Ulster Scots’ has value from a musical and cultural perspective (its not a language) but the music associated with the Ulster Scots is Irish music and should not be ‘cherry-picked’, isolated and called something else. It is part of the rich and diverse tapestry that is traditional Irish music, Irish refering to geography. Any attempt to ‘own’ part of that mix and associate it to any one community should be prevented. However, Nelson Mc Causland (Minister for Arts) is trying to do just that and divide the Planter from the Gael - But remember, Irish music has been influenced constantly from different waves of emmigration and military occupation with the eighteenth-century hornpipe from England and reel from Scotland, and the nineteenth-century polka from continental Europe etc. The Jig was known throughout these Isles leaving the Harping airs our only indigenous music probably. So there!
