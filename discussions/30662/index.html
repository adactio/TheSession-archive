Broken chords

The notion of “broken chords” has recently been discussed elsewhere.

There seem to be a distinction between these as opposed to a regular chord, i.e. three or more notes sounded simultaneously(Is a diad actually a chord?) and neither can they be described as arpeggios, apparently.

According to one poster, this occurs when the notes occur one after another but those played previously are still held… unlike an arpeggio, perhaps?

Maybe this topic could be discussed more thoroughly here for those who haven’t had access to the other discussion?

Re: Broken chords

You should be ruled by what accosts your ears. If three notes are played in succession by an instrument that sustains the earlier notes all through, you are hearing a chord. Three notes at once. If the same three notes are played staccato, or by an instrument that does not sustain the earlier notes, a whistle for example, then you are not hearing three notes played together so you are hearing not a chord but a short section of single-line melody. Those notes may well suggest harmony inside your head but what reaches your actual ears is not a chord. Call it a broken chord, arpeggio if you like, but the distinction is useful and we need to hang on to it.

Re: Broken chords

a broken chord is a chord where a string is skipped in the middle of the chord. For instance, voicing the bass note on the low E and then putting the 5th on the B string and the third on the G string would be a “broken chord”

we do this all the time playing jazz, and that’s what its called

There are whole families of these things. Try it with 7th chord harmony, too. I’ve seen these chords in some methods classified by where the skipped string falls:

o=skipped x= fretted

three note triads
(oxxx)
xoxx
xxox

xooxx
xxoox
xoxox

and for a four note chord:
(oxxxx)
xoxxx
xxoxx
xxxox

xooxxx
xxooxx
xxxoox
xoxoxx
xxoxox

not all of these patterns are practical, but you know how method books are

The idea is that any of the chords you presently know fits one of these patterns. This is a way of helping you find other voicings derived from voicings you already know

hope this helps fix those broken chords.

Re: Broken chords

A dyad is not a chord.
A chord has to be as least a triad.
An arpegiated chord (i.e. an arpeggio) is a chord.
One hears the separate tones combine in one’s brain.
This is how one can detect the cadences in single voice music such as, indeed, Trad Irish tunes.
A ‘power chord’ is not a chord. It is a dyad consisting of root and perfect fifth. And by the way, calling it a ‘Modal chord’ is even ‘more wrong’! It’s neither a chord nor particularly modal!
I understand a ‘broken chord’ the same as an arpeggio, although an arpeggio should be florrid and grand in the manner of a harp (which of course is what the word means). Melodic fragments may also be ‘broken chords’ as is obvious from listening to Trad Irish.
But remember chords aren’t just simple triads, extensions and alterations. E G# B D is a chord (E dominant 7th) but so is E F F# C (E augmented 5th suspended 2nd flattened 9th*). Some are rather more difficult to name and hear! So all melodic fragments are some sort of chord.

*or Fmaj 7th flattened 9th no 3rd or F#maj7th flattened 5th added flattened 7th no 3rd or C 11th flattened 5th.

Re: Broken chords

A chord: a series of notes sounding together. At least three separate pitch classes are needed (so C G c g c‘ g’ is not a chord, because it only contains two pitch classes regardless of the number of octaves played).

Arpeggio: A series of chord tones played consecutively, often in some repetitive fashion.

Rolled Chord, Broken Chord: Commonly used to describe the rapid playing of a set of chord tones where the notes are sustained together. Often for effect, or on instruments where circumstance makes playing multiple notes difficult if not impossible (as in strumming across 6 strings of a guitar, playing multiple notes on a hammered dulcimer, or playing notes separated far enough on the harp so they can not be sounded together.

Re: Broken chords

Chord = CEGc all played together.

Arpeggio = CEGc played in succession and separately

Arpeggiated chord (or rolled chord in popular music terminology) = CEGc played one after another, quickly with each note held down. Usually low to high notes but sometimes the other direction.
