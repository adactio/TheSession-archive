Bodhrans, sessions and tempo

I play in a twice-monthly session with low-mid to high-mid range players. At a recent session, I was accompanying an accomplished Uilleann pipes player on the bohdran, an instrument I usually play only if I don’t know the melody. It was not a pipes solo. Other melody instruments were also playing on the reel, but the pipes were fairly overwhelming. My tempo is pretty solid, and I noticed the pipes player going subtly off tempo. The first couple of times, when I noticed that the pipes and bodhran were out of sync, I’d adjust my drumming to match the piper. Then I changed my approach. Instead of focusing on keeping a consistent tempo, I consentrated on listening to the piper and making sure I stayed in time with him whatever his tempo.

I have read that in a session setting, tempo is set by the melody instruments and that rhythm instruments take their cue from the melody instruments. This is opposite of most modern Western music where the rhythm section sets the tempo and the rest of the instruments fall in line behind the drummer.

Although I first thought the piper should have had a better sense of tempo, afterwards it occurred to me that, whether he was right or wrong, I should stay focused on his beat and match *whatever* tempo he plays. It’s a new way of listening than I am used to, so I’m curious to hear other’s thoughts on this.

Re: Bodhrans, sessions and tempo

I think that bodhran players are more likely to get the blame if the tempo is off regardless of who is at fault. But I think in sessions we drummers are supposed to follow the tempo set by the melody players. At least, in my humble opinion, that’s what I think is the way to go. I generally listen to the tune first time around, and then only join in on the repeat when everyone else is settled, or maybe join in on the second tune of a set.

Even rock drummers have to listen to what the lead guitar is doing and make adjustments. It’s all about sticking together, regardless of the style of music.

Anyone can be off tempo, no matter what their instrument. And some people are thrown by the bodhran in a session, not always to do with the playing of it but maybe they aren’t used to playing with one. Maybe he had an off night, or was drunk? Sometimes you just have a brain fart, that’s all. That’s my general state of being, so I am sympathetic.

Bodhraners need to remember how powerful their instruments are. They have the power to lift or destroy a session or set of tunes. An excellent accompanist can bring a session to the next level. A bad one can send people to the bar.

Re: Bodhrans, sessions and tempo

Craymcla, what Michael was getting on about is that some session players feel (strongly, as demonstrated by Michael himself) that a bodhran player shouldn’t accompany a tune if s/he doesn’t know the melody.

Which goes to your point about a session being “people playing music…they enjoy playing.” Yes. But bear in mind that some people enjoy playing with precision and unity, and a drummer who whacks away without knowing the tune will detract from that enjoyment.

Some sessions _are_ free-for-alls, and that can be fun if that’s what you’re out for. But some sessions are about getting in a fine groove, and you can’t do that if someone (on any instrument) joins in who doesn’t know the tune. If you play at sessions often enough, you’ll grow weary and wary of uninformed, unskilled people wielding bodhrans who think that sessions are their god-given opportunity to explore their personal musical expressiveness, regardless of its relevance to the music everyone else is playing.

I like a good bodhran, and I’m lucky to play with several very good drummers at our local session. But they learn the tunes, just like the rest of us.

Take a reel, 8 notes in a bar, with extra twiddly bits. And what does the drum do? If the player’s good, he’ll bash the drum once for every note. Come on now, what’s the point of that? How would it sound if the banjo player just played one note over and over (in time of course) rather than play the tune. You’d think it sounded crap and you’d be right. So why should the bodhran get away with it?

Re: Bodhrans, sessions and tempo

We should distinguish between the session bodrhán and the concert gig bodhrán. In most sessions the bodhrán isn’t usually an essential part of the ensemble, and an incompetent bodhrán player certainly qualifies as an inessential!
A good band on stage, on the other hand, will have carefully crafted its sound and balance, and a first class bodhránist will be an asset in such circumstances. I’ve just seen the band Míse live at the Irish Centre at Hammersmith. Their bodhránist is a case in point; he didn’t play all the time, but when he did he certainly was an asset. And he had his own virtuoso solo spot so show what can be done with the instrument.
BTW, does anyone know who the young lady fiddler in the support act was? I think I heard someone say she had won a medal at Clonmel this August.
Trevor

Talking of sound and balance, it’s my experience that speech coming out through a music PA system in a hall often leaves a lot to be desired - bassy, boomy and echoey - and sometimes next to impossible to understand. I’d suggest that when the front man of a band talks to the audience it would be best if he uses a mic, amp and loudspeaker combination that is independent of the music setup and which is set up specifically for speech.
