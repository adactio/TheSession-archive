Re: “Tis of thee Oh perpetual beginner that I sing…..”

I agree with some of your points, Div. However, I’d like to make a few comments.

“ITM” as you describe it(Not everyone likes this acronym) is *not* the fastest music in the world. At least, it hasn’t been so historically. Certainly, some tunes are faster than others and benefit from being played at a “fair lick”. It’s just that it has become the fashion to play Irish (and Scottish) tunes at break neck speed. They don’t have to be played at that speed though and they shouldn’t if the players are unable to do them justice.

Also, I think descriptions like beginner, intermediate, and veteran are a little misleading and open to interpretation as are the time scales you mentioned. We all progress at different levels but learning is a life long process. Some of us may be slower at “mastering”(another contentious term) a particular instrument for a number of reasons. It might be laziness or lack of commitment but also because we might not have the same amount of time at our disposal. Also, we might not be quite as focused on one particular instrument; eg I started on the mandolin and have probably taken longer to play the fiddle because I keep alternating between the two. I should also state(before anyone else does)that learning the instrument isn’t the same as learning the music. :-)

However, I do agree that some people just don’t seem to bother at all and they can’t understand why they never get better. The rest of us know why! :-)

I don’t see any harm in going to workshops even if you’ve been playing for a few years. Obviously, you can be more selective. However, I’ve seen many well known and experienced musicians who will go to a workshop especially if somebody like Kevin Burke (example) is hosting it.

Re: “Tis of thee Oh perpetual beginner that I sing…..”

a would be a good bet, *if*. . .you can wrap your head around the idea of abandoning everything you know about your instruments. You’ve got to acknowledge that you don’t know squat and that what you do know is wrong, up front and deep in your soul. It can be hard for someone who “knows how to play” to go back and spend weeks, maybe months, just working out Twinkle, Twinkle properly.

In your case I’d advise private lessons, but make sure you find a teacher with experience in “remedial” work. Someone who is used to true beginners won’t know what to do with you, and the group, informal nature of a workshop just won’t cut it. Have a look around this website for inspiration:

http://www.guitarprinciples.com/

People who can’t do a can fall back on b. Approaching a new instrument helps keep the mind and fingers clean of preconceptions. As for what instrument, well, that’s a toughy. If you can approach a similar instrument with a clean mind whistle and bouzouki are the obvious choices and a cheap whistle is, well, cheap.

If these instruments are too close and you simply transfer your bad habits to them then you have to look for something as far away as possible. Fiddle would at least keep what you know of where notes are a constant, but is otherwise so far removed from mandolin that your concentration will be on the differences rather than the similarities. It’s the Queen Bitch of instruments though, and it will likely take you years to begin to like what you hear out of yourself, and your family must be *very* understanding about it.

Don’t rely on your family “putting up with” your playing. If you’re ready to approach playing seriously, take it seriously. For a number of reasons you want to isolate yourself when you are practicing as much as possible. Learning to play is a very internal thing and you must turn yourself inward. It’s very much a form of meditation. Dedicate time and space to it. In a pinch a temporary “sound proof” practice studio can be set up tent fashion with PVC pipe and bed comforters. If you have something akin to a den, use it. Practicing isn’t family time. What they need to put up with is your taking a lot of time apart, strictly ‘selfishly’ to ‘do your own thang.’

For God’s sake though, whatever you do, don’t do c. Sure, when you’re an Old Codger (and I’m right behind you in that regard, have a hot cup of coffee waiting for me, will you?) do as you wish, but, let me invoke that old maxim:

Before you break the rules, learn them. The line between Old Codger and Pain in the Arse Old Man may be fine, but it’s there.

“I know in my head how I want. Oh to be able to play it!”

That issue is a somewhat different one than the one you originally expressed.

Since you are the only one who can hear what’s in your head the solution is simple, although not easy. Lock yourself in a room for hours a day and play until you figure out how to do it.

Michael will probably think this vindicates his diagnosis of your having a problem with your ear. It does not. Your ear is fine. What you lack is the connection between the ear and the instrument. The physical analog of what you hear that will result in the instrument producing the sounds you wish it to. Your brain knows. Your fingers do not.

Go teach your fingers.
