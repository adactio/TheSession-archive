Ailin, I was making a more general statement about what people think of Riverdance in general, not necessarily the music, specifically.

I think that, as a whole, Riverdance popularized the genres of Irish dance and Irish trad music, but in both cases, they were commercialized, and taken a certain way beyond the tradition. That was probably necessary, to make it the worldwide sensation that it was.

But there have been some discussions over the years about whether Riverdance did more good or harm to the tradition(s) as a whole. On one hand, it certainly brought it to a wider audience, and got a lot of new people interested in it. On the other hand, it put forward a face of gloss, glitz, money, showmanship, and a lot of other things that don’t necessarily meld well with the tradition - and helped generate a whole generation of people who think that’s what it’s all about.

So I’m not dissing the time, effort, and amazing amounts of creativity that went into making Riverdance here… Just pointing out that it has been a topic of heated discussion in the past, and is another reason why Markmoloney’s post seemed like a wind up to people.

Riverdance is an outgrowth of Andy Irvine’s fascination with Balkan music that I first became aware of back in the days when he was playing with Planxty. He continued to venture down that path and eventually collaborated with Bill Whelan and Davy Spillane, along with some Balkan musicians, in a project that produced a recording called “East Wind.”

You can see them here: http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZMpj4jcR7x0

So even though Riverdance is an Irish product produced by Irish musicians, it’s basis is a fusion and not part of what is considered Irish traditional music. This is why you aren’t likely to find it being played at Irish trad sessions.

Re: Riverdance

Ailin, your assertion that people who think ITM shouldn’t be learned from black dots is a sign of being “too lazy to learn to read” is patently absurd. I’ll wager I sightread better than 99% of the people on this board as I’m conservatory trained. (Which has its problems, to be sure.) I do not learn ITM from black dots any more.

There is almost always a qualitative difference in the way a person plays a tune learned by ear and from sheet music. Personally I think it’s because of which neural pathways you activate as you learn the tune. Listening activates the auditory cortex and a speech-like area in the hippocampus, while reading comes in through the visual cortex and has to be processed differently in the brain. This is part of the reason classical piano players and soloists memorize. It forces the music away from the visual parts of the brain and the music sounds better.

The other huge problem with notation is conceptual. A tune is not a fixed piece like a part in a symphony. A written-down tune is a pale representation of what an Irish musician actually plays. People who buy into the black dots can end up very confused about what a tune actually even is.

As for Riverdance, the show itself was fine. The reason everyone around here hates it is because it brought an influx of dancers and musicians who wanted to reproduce Riverdance, not be part of the Irish music and dance community. Suddenly you had a bunch of Michael Flatley and Jean Butler wannabes crashing the ceilis and an absolute infestation of bad low whistle players in sessions.

Re: Riverdance

In addition to what the good doctor says, learning tunes by ear is good practice for playing tunes with people. It’s hard work for some of us - it’s been very hard for me, and I’ve still got a lot of learning to do there - but the more I work at picking up tunes from the air, the better I find I’m able to mesh my playing with someone else’s. That blending, that feeling of playing with someone else, not just playing the same tune at the same time, is what I really love in this music, so I feel that the learner should be made aware that this path, harder though it may be, is one that’s worth committing to as much as you’re able. More than that, it’s worth committing beyond your ability - it’s worth trying and failing, because you learn something there as well.

I’m not one to say you should never learn tunes by reading, but in my experience it’s been more of a hindrance than a help, all told. And I think that a novice should be told both of those stories - the one you tell, and the one I tell - and a few others besides, without disparagement to anyone else’s story, so they have a chance to find a way that works for them.

Will’s violinist who struggles to get a phrase by ear is a good concrete argument for emphasizing the aural part of the tradition over the written part. The aural part is a habit that, once established, does not interfere with the written part. But a student who gets in the habit of reading often fails to develop the habit of listening.

Re: Riverdance

@enlyke - I’ll attempt to answer your questions! ;-)

Compare (for example) the Major and the Mixolydian scales.

Both scales have eight notes (including the octave note)

The only difference between the two is that in a major scale the seventh (or leading note) is a semitone below the octave, whereas in a mixolydian scale the seventh (or leading note) is one whole tone (or two semitones) below the octave.

You can sometimes spot this “leading note” interval at or near the end of a tune, and sometimes near the end of the “A” part of a tune as well. If you can recognise the interval, it can help in determining the mode of the tune.

One way of getting the feel of (for example) the mixolydian mode is just to practise playing up and down that scale in the three main trad keys.

Also, just learn a whole bunch of (say) mixolydian mode tunes. Then, when you hear another mixolydian mode tune that’s new to you, the chances are that you will be able to spot it immediately.

Repeat the exercises for the other modes.

I wouldn’t say though that interval recognition is essential - its just one of the many things that helps - including a knowledge of “the dots”

Being able to play by ear is the most important thing, but written music does have its uses. For example, without a knowledge of it, how else would we be able to retrieve (and subsequently, play) tunes from old manuscripts?

Re: Riverdance

The modern major/minor system started with the Italian Baroque in the 17th century. Not sure who was first to publish something in a stated key, but I think it was either Biagio Marini or Corelli. The sort of music it started with was for violin(s) with bass/continuo accompaniment. Took a while for singers to catch up, as all their training came from the Church’s modal system. You find scores from this period where the composer is having a hard time trying to keep both the singers and instrumentalists straight about what to do.

What you calling your “ear” is what I would call “perception of mode” - everybody’s got ears, decisions like that take a brain.

One of the commonest modal distinctions I make is working out whether a tune is going to fit in the gamut of the Highland pipes. Mostly I can do that simply because I know the tune, but it’s not difficult even with a totally unfamiliar one - the standard melodic cadences of Highland pipe music are almost always identifiable in the first couple of bars, long before you’ve heard the tune reach all the notes it’s going to. This is what people playing Indian or Middle Eastern music do all the time. (Those 19th century fiddle-istic versions of pipe tunes that start out normal and then zoom down the G string in variation parts are a pain in the bum).

Re: Riverdance

Modal classifications can be an assist to the learning process in the way Will suggests, but they have many more uses than that. I mention some of them in my tutorial. They’re mostly about rather practical matters to do with arranging for specific instruments.

One simple case guitarists find very helpful: they like to be told (VERY VERY SLOWLY AND DISTINCTLY) when you’re about to do a tune in B minor. For some reason (I wouldn’t know why, not being a guitarist) it is very difficult for them to catch on to this in the middle of a set. A fairly natural key sequence for Highland pipe tunes is Amix -> Bmin -> Dmaj, and they’ll often leave you on your own in the middle one.

There is a very long tradition of using modes the way Will describes in Turkish art and “semi-art” music. It’s always learned by ear, but you are always given two bits of information before learning a new tune: one is its melodic mode. (The other is its metric mode, which Irish music doesn’t have). This saves a lot of time getting everybody playing in tune with each other - some instruments like the kanun (lever-sharpened plucked dulcimer) take quite a while to retune, for other instruments it takes a while to adjust to the fretboard positions for a new mode, and as with Irish music the flute/whistle players need to select the right piece of plumbing.

I don’t think many guitarists would think it was reasonable of a melody player to take off into C minor without any warning that a capo shift might be needed. Yes, a competent guitarist could work it out by ear in a few bars, but why deprive them of the opportunity to hit the right key dead on at the start?

Dear MarkMoloney, To get sheet music for Riverdance, first install ABC converter software (free) on your computer. I like to use this one, even though it’s got a funky interface:
http://members.quicknet.nl/j.coolegem/abcedit/intro.html

Then buy the Riverdance CD, take the list of tunes, (or just get the list from the Amazon sale page), and look up the ABC for each tune from either this web site TheSession.org , or from another really good one, JC’s Tune Finder, at http://trillian.mit.edu/~jc/cgi/abc/tunefind

Sometimes you can just Google a really obscure tune name with ABC, but if it was in Riverdance, it’s not obscure anymore.

Then pick one of the ABC versions you’ve found, and past it into ABC-Edit,. Select Display in PDF, and you will have printable sheet music for that tune :-)

I do this all the time. I can get the shape of a tune with my ears, but sheet music really helps my quality of playing a lot! :-)
