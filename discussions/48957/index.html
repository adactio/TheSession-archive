Neurodiversity incidence in trad music

Just a thought…

I’m neurotypical, but have a close relative with Asperger’s and have completed a few certifications on Autism/ASD to understand it better.

Having now chatted to hundreds of people in sessions and folk festivals in my area, it’s very clear to me there are far higher numbers of neurodiverse people involved in the local trad scene (amateur to high intermediate) than you would experience in other hobbies.

I can theorise why (hobby based on unusually high levels of structure, codified rules for how to engage socially, hyper focus etc), but can’t find any studies on this.

I was wondering:

1). Has anyone else observed significantly more neurodiverse people in their trad scene than the norm (or is it a phenomenon in my area)

2). Does anyone know of any studies/surveys on this (asking participants at festivals/sessions etc)

It might be another ‘divided by a common language’ thing?

In the UK at least, neurodiverse is used to describe people who are not neurotypical. They may have a range of differences, including autism, dyspraxia, attention deficit disorder, dyslexia, dyscalculia, synaesthesia, etc. Some only have one diagnosis, but it’s reasonably common for people to have two or three.

Neurodivergence might mean the same thing, but neurodiversity seems to be much more common.

How would you do that study? Go to sessions and ask people, “Do you have any mental health diagnoses or diagnoses along the lines of ADHD or ASD?” But even if people didn’t tell ye oan yer bike (and I bet they would), we know many adults learn how to get through life, one way or another, with ASD/ADHD/other stuff which remains undiagnosed. People with actual diagnoses are a small fraction of your neurodiverse population. Especially adults.

Okay, you could post a link on thesession.org to a survey, which uses the screening questions for ASD/ADHD, as well as depression, bipolar, OCD, et. al. But then you have the whole ‘self-selected online survey problem,’ which is a ballache for your data because it’s not all that representative of Irish trad musicians in general. It’s people who read online forums and who can be ar*sed with a (long, if it’s actually good) online survey.

I can’t imagine you would have much success at going to actual sessions and getting people to take 20 minutes out of playing tunes to fill out a long survey (and let me reiterate, if you’re incorporating established screening procedures for ASD, ADHD, anxiety disorders, etc., it will be long). And you’d need *a lot* of participants to generate meaningful data comparing amount of neurodiversity in trad music community to neurodiversity in the general population, a challenge, since prevalence in the latter is still up for debate.

Anecdotally, it’s easy to say, ‘Yeah, trad players are a bunch of weirdos,’ but you can say that about a lot of things. c.f. my posts on the other thread about horse owners.

As someone who has no diagnoses but you could probably find me one if you tried, I actually found that sessions are *rubbish* if you have any kind of social anxiety. Nowadays, I don’t find them as stressful due to (a) feeling like I’m competent enough at playing to not p1ss anyone off, usually (b) knowing enough tunes to sit in the backseat of most sessions but not feel like I’m taking up space, and (c) giving less f*cks about (a) and (b).

Re: Neurodiversity incidence in trad music

An unscientific opinion: I have observed over the years at sessions (not exclusively Irish) quite a lot of people, among which I include myself, that are not particularly good at social interactions of the usual kind. Whether this necessarily constitutes neurodivergence/neurodiversity, I could not say (and even a psychologist specialising in this field would, I suspect, need more than casual observations to make any such diagnosis). But, as I see it, the music provides an alternative means of interaction that does not have to involve talking, facial expression etc.; it is, I think, this particular mode of music making (i.e. playing in company but not performing to an audience) that fulfils that role.

Since my main musical experience is playing traditional music, either in sessions or in other situations where the musicians are not the sole or main focus (e.g. playing for dancing), I cannot speak for other genres of music or modes of music making. A few people have mentioned social anxiety and sensory issues, which make a session an intolerable environment; it could be that some people with these issues would be more comfortable playing to a quiet and attentive concert hall audience than amid the hubbub of a busy pub. To carry out any sort of meaningful study on this specific subject, you would have to go round the conservatoires, jazz clubs, rock and blues venues etc. – as well as participants in non-musical pastimes – as well as just looking at trad musicians.

To me, a traditional music session is an Autistic person’s dream social scenario: The music tends to happen in a dimly lit public place, where it is fairly obvious where to sit and which direction to face (aren’t circles great). You don’t even have to formally introduce yourself if you don’t feel like it, and eye contact is optional or brief if changing tunes, etc. I also was obsessed with session etiquette years ago (still am). It’s quite humbling getting a deeper awareness of yourself as an adult, and the same thing applies to how I feel about Irish traditional music. Most of the tunes are so simple. “So I could turn the question on its head and ask why do more ‘neurotypicals’ not partake in anything resembling an interest or hobby?”

I’m kind of reiterating what I said in my other post on this thread…

The thing is, you don’t know how many ‘neurotypicals’ v. ‘neurodiverse’ people are partaking in sessions or any other activity. You don’t know if there are a higher proportion of neurodiverse people. I think some conclusions are being drawn because people who are neurodiverse, whether that’s self-diagnosed or diagnosed by a medical professional, are self-identifying. ‘Neurotypicals’ tend to not do that. No one says “I’m incredibly normal and I like Irish music/horses/rock climbing/chess, etc.” So your availabilility heuristic is kicking in -- you’re aware of what you’re hearing about, but not what you’re not hearing about. You therefore might think the former occurs more frequently than it does.

There seems to be more ADHD and ASD spectrum disorders being diagnosed in the adult population anyway, and symptoms/behaviours that weren’t previously recognised as being involved in those, like being kind of scatty, spacey, and socially awkward, now are. Maybe some of those are also correlated to being interested in niche hobbies. That is possible, of course. Is the proportion of ASD/ADHD amongst traditional musicians higher than that in the general population? Who knows, since we don’t have great data on its ocurrence on a wider scale and that’s changing rapidly at the moment as more and more adults get diagnosed.

Comhaltas, a non-profit organization dedicated to preserving Irish cultural traditions, has recently created Cuimsuí (inclusive) sessions in counties Galway and Mayo, which are adapted to welcome “musicians with additional needs/disabilities. The event was successful enough in Galway to be featured in the local newspaper https://www.facebook.com/photo?fbid=1401418662025150&set=pcb.1401420152025001. Things are moving in a more inclusive direction!
