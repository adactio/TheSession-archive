Re: Do u remember your first time?

In a word, no. I began, as many do, with the piano, where learning from sheet music was required. I can still learn that way. I like to think that reading music is like reading anything else. If I were to read a story in a book, and then told it to someone else, I don’t think that my ability to accurately pass on the story would be materially different than if I had heard the story instead of read it. However, if I were to listen to someone tell a story, I would also hear (if they were a good story-teller) where they sped up or slowed down, or spoke louder or softer for effect. My ability to tell the story interestingly would depend not on where I got it from (or through what medium), but my ability to place the stylistic nuances in effective places. In traditional music, versus classical music, one needs to know what nuances to include, and how to include them. These nuances depend on the style. If one style includes repeating parts, or changing notes, then that is allowed. If another lets you change dynamics or add ornaments (as in baroque music), then thats ok too. It all depends what is natural for that type of music. If one knows what nuances to add to make a piece of music authentic, do they need to learn the piece by ear, or can they authenticate the skeleton of a tune written out in sheet music?

Re: Do u remember your first time?

I don’t remember the first piece of music I read, but I do remember that learning to read music was an agony for me. I had to come up with a shortcut to learning it (a note between two lines is played with an open string or second finger, a note on a line is played with a first or third finger) --- which I still use. I also still have to stop and count notes to remember whether that note on the second line down from the top is an, er, um…a, b, c, right --- a D. Unless I remember it forms an octave with the open D string. However, learning to read words was an agony also. I don’t regret the effort that went into either one.

But man, the rush. The day I played off a piece fluently from the dots was like the day I took my copy of Wind In The Willows off the bookshelf when my mom was busy and read it to myself. I knew I’d DONE something. And I knew the skills I’d learned could never be taken away from me; they were mine. The sense of achievement made me feel like I was flying.

Having since had a long illness that left me with severe memory damage, I find learning by ear a slow and difficult process as well, and one I’m having to flog myself through. (I’m getting there; it’s improving over time.) It helps me no end to have a set of dots to check when my memory falters. But I rarely try to learn a tune from dots if I haven’t heard it first --- and that’s true of any type of music, from a Vivaldi concerto to a Tommy Peoples jig.

And not long ago there was the magic moment when I realized I’d learnt Leaving Brittany entirely by ear before I found a set of dots for it. I might have had the dots in front of me (for the first time, having printed them off that morning), but I wasn’t looking at them. I was remembering the tune. Man, the rush. I knew I’d DONE something. And I was flying…

Re: Do u remember your first time?

“All I’m saying, and no-one has disagreed, is that it’s not enough”

Yes, but you said it in counter to a claim that no one had made. Why should anyone disagree, especially in the context of the first post?

Personally I’m of the mind that Suzuki was one of those people who was “borderline bright.” He was just bright enough to have a sudden revelation that rocked his world and inspired him to write books and found a school which people less bright than he was follow like a religion.

Unfortunately his “revelation” was something the really smart people had taken for granted as “obvious” for thousands of years already. The founders of “schools” typically get there by the Peter Principle. It’s just that their level of incompetence is a bit higher than the norm.

“. . .as far as diddley music is concerned, learning only by ear can be enough”

As far as *any* music is concerned learning by ear can be enough. That doesn’t mean it’s optimum, for *any* music.

I learned to read, in part, because it was easier to memorize a poem or a play if I could read the book. A wheelchair is enough to get around, but you might want to walk now and again.

And you might want to develop some real facility at reading music. Christ, just learn the key of D or something, but learn it well.

It is one of the great failings of the modern formal pedagogy that such high emphasis is put on reading, to the detriment of ear training. The ultimate goal is to be able to play your instrument as naturually as you whistle by putting your lips together and blowing. I actually practice this by randomly leafing through guitar fake books and playing songs, many of which I’ve never even whistled or sung before, by reference to “the ear in my brain.” I’ve reached the point where being able to do so on the first try isn’t a remarkable feat, although I could certainly stand improvement. I also put some work into learning classical pieces by ear.

I *never* use music, not even a simple chart, at a session or public performance. If I haven’t memorized a tune or piece I don’t consider it well enough known yet to perform.

But most people can learn to read easily. Those who can’t learn easily can at least learn, and learn well with a bit of stick to itness. And there’s not only no point in not doing so, but a good deal of reason *to* do so.

Because just like language learning by ear *isn’t* really enough if you want to understand music on anything higher than a raw, intuitive level, which may *suffice* for certain people, but isn’t truely knowledgable and is prone to error.

Not even about diddely. It might lead you to believe that “it isn’t just scales” when knowing that it’s *all* just scales, and what that really means to the music, might prove rather useful, and time saving, in certain circumstances.

Yes, I’m even going so far as to say that even if all you play is diddely you should not only learn to read, but you should learn some *theory,* even if all you learn is one key.

Just learn it well. All it takes is a bit of time and effort. Learning it on a diatonic instrument like the penny whistle can make it easier for some.

If you can already play by ear, which is more useful – reading music or understanding some theory? And do you, if you read, understand theory through notation?

For me, theory is more useful. I understood a good bit of theory before I learned to decipher the dots. I didn’t set out to actively learn theory until much later; I just accumulated it as I listened and played, and even now my understanding of theory has virtually nothing to do with reading music. I learned by relating it directly to the guitar fingerboard and that’s still the main mode through which I conceptualize things like harmony. Instead of visualizing a musical staff, I visualize a guitar fingerboard. Or, with vocal parts, I might just hear the voices in my head.

Reading helps me learn a tune, but I could also listen over and over and pick out the notes. Hearing a new tune and recognizing that it’s in dorian mode, but with a sharped seventh in the B part, immediately informs my understanding of the tune and makes it easier to learn the notes.

Re: Do u remember your first time?

Oh, definately theory. It’s what it’s all about. The dots are just a crude recording device. You can then relate the theory to the dots, but I don’t learn or teach theory through dots, which are just one possible representation (I’ve already posted about dots not being the only viable, or even the best, form of notation) of the music in printed form.

I use monochords, straight pipes, mountain dulcimers and keyboards to teach theory, and then relate that to the actual instrument the student (or me) is actually playng. It’s a hands on, ear and eyeball thing, not a book thing, although working problems on paper may well be part of the “hands on” part.

Then it’s on to picking melodies and harmonies out by ear and improvising to put the theory into real, practical use.

The dots always come, but they always come last. I’ll keep standing up for their importance, but I’d agree that they’re the least important, which is why the modern formal pedogogies fail so greatly by emphasising them. This is how people come to think of the notation as the music, which is completely bass ackwards.

Fortunately it isn’t an either or sort of thing. There’s no reason not to learn it all, because it doesn’t even take that much more time to do it, because the dots take less time to learn than any of it.

Michael I think what Trev was trying to say is that the dots aren’t enough for *any* music, be it trad, classical, jazz or whatever. He’s absolutely right. Classical music isn’t just about reading dots off a page either, and any good classical musician knows that. You might not have much flexibility as far as missing notes out or changing octaves, but musical expression happens in different ways than trad, i.e. primarily through dynamics, which aren’t such a big issue in trad.

I say this because I played classical piano when I was a kid. I began when I was 8, just picking it up by ear in the same way as trad musicians do, which I guess is kind of unusual. I used to listen to piano pieces and play them as accurately as I could from hearing them on a record, stuff like Beethoven’s sonatas. I can still pick up (and transcribe) pretty complex stuff when I need to because I can separate each layer of polyphonic sound in my head from having practised it as a kid.

Then when I learnt to read music it saved me so much time! I didn’t have to spend time working stuff out anymore. At first I used it as a guide to show me which notes I should be playing, and I continued to play from memory, but then my sightreading started to get better and I had the dots with me the whole time. I began to have trouble memorising pieces. Unfortunately my music suffered because I think I stopped listening but carried on playing. I had trouble coming up with interpretations for everything I learnt from the sheetmusic and I lost interest. In the same way, if you learn trad from the dots from the beginning, you don’t acquire sufficient skills in improvisation, ornamentation that should be automatic, rhythm etc.

Starting to play traditional music was a breath of fresh air because I could feel it again. It took me a while to realise that I was going to have to use sheetmusic differently, but I got past that idea that if you can read dots you can play anything, and now I can use written music without any detrimental effect on my actual playing style, because I took the time to learn to play the music first by ear, instead of assuming there was a shortcut.

I don’t play the piano anymore because I haven’t owned one for years. But if I ever went back to it, I think I’d approach my learning in a completely different way.
