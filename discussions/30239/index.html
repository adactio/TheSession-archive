Re: Just curious …

Modes of tunes do not have to be discussed with reference to guitar chords. However, most tunes are made up of phrases that outline triads like |(^)cAeA cAeA|B(=)GdG eGdG| so when you’re talking about the mode of the tune, it makes sense to talk about the mode in terms of the underlying chords under those triads, in this case A major and G major. The overall mode of the tune boils down to which of these triads predominate in the tune, and most of the time (but not always), which triad is suggested by the beginning and/or end of each strain. The tune we were arguing about ended on a suggested D major chord, but the fact that it started on a suggested A major triad and A major and G major predominated in both parts, made it A mixolydian rather than D major. Those who made reference to guitar chords in that thread were really trying to point out that if you tried to back it using predominantly D major chords and D major progressions, or, say, with a pipe drone on D (if you don’t want to refer to guitars), it would sound bad. If you droned on A, though, it would work because the predominance of A major triads makes A into the “home note” for the ear/brain.

Re: Just curious …

Tom Maguire’s Fancy is a great example of all this at work https://thesession.org/tunes/8353. When the tune starts, it sounds for all the world like the overall mode of the tune is definitely going to be D, but then after the first 2 bars, G major triads predominate over Ds for the rest of the tune, and each part ends on a G which “sets” the mode for the ear. If D major chords continued to dominate, and the part endings ended on D, then the mode would be Dmaj. Even if the parts ended on a G but there was a predominance of D triads, then the mode would still likely be Dmaj. An example of what I’m talking about here is Within a Mile of Dublin https://thesession.org/tunes/125. The parts end on G, but the predominance of D triads (|AD~D2 FEFG|AD~D2… containing mostly Ds, F#s and As with the odd passing note off the beat) make it D. The C nats in the tune make it mixo.

I agree with you about the ould fellas playing slightly sharp on certain notes.

The flipside of that, of course, is that modern players who are used to sophisticated harmonies being used to back the tunes are able to vary the tune in different ways… not better, but different. For instance, they can do a variation that suggests an underlying chord by sweeping up and down an arpeggio or something, even if they’re playing unbacked. Or they can use a particular double stop, e.g. an E on yer fiddle D string coupled with a low C on yer G string, suggesting a C chord, even if there’s no C triad in the melody. This makes the harmony inside the melody players head explicit to the listener in the same way as guitar backing would, I suppose.

The significance of all of this is, of course, that accompaniment may have changed the way melody players play melody. If that’s the case, then that’s a big thing happening right under our noses…

The melody MUST linger on certain notes in order to set the home note without accompaniment. For example, let’s take a plainsong piece I just made up off the top of my head. If I just sing with no sharps or flats:

E2F2 G2A2 G4 A4

…you can’t tell what mode I’m singing. You think it could be E phrygian, ’cause that’s what I started on, or it could be A aeolian (min). I could even just be in C ionian (maj). In order to make it clear I’m in Ephry, I have to linger on notes that belong to the main harmonics of E, especially the 5th. I need more Bs:

E2F2 G2A2 G4 A4 B8 B2c2B2A2B2 G4 F2G2 E2D2C2D2 E8

That strong 5th between E and B has now set E as the home note.

If I want A aeolian, though, I have to emphasize A and E and maybe a few C nats to hammer home the triad:

E2F2 G2A2 G4 A4 A2c2~B2G2 A4 e4 d2c2B2c2 d4c2B2 A8

That’s in Aaeo, even though we started on E. And I got a roll in there, lol.

Re: Just curious …

“Tell you what, Doc, I’ll go with your ”lingering on certain notes", but I think it’s a stretch to say that that means that it has to emphasise any particular triad. It’s probably just the notes themselves.’

It’s the intervals of perfect 5ths and 4ths that do it, because they are the main harmonics. That’s 2 notes of the triad. The 3rd of the triad just decides whether the tune is minor (aeo/dor) or major (ion/mix).

It just so happens that a lot of tunes have arpeggios in that outline the triads. This makes it relatively easy to discern the mode of a tune. If you don’t have triads, it’s a bit harder because you have to rely on gauging the predominance of tonics and 5ths and how they shape the melody contour. So no, it doesn’t have to emphasise a particular triad, but the more notes of a particular triad appear in the melody, the more likely it is to fix the mode for the ear.

The important thing is that you don’t simply use the melody contour itself to decide what mode a tune’s in. You have to see/hear the thing as a whole, bearing in mind that mathematics and physics dictate that some notes of the scale are more important than others because of harmonics. You also have to take into account the structure and phrasing of the tune, just like in plainsong, i.e. how the parts start and end in cadences (and no you don’t need chords to determine that).

Re: Just curious …

“Lingering on certain notes” was systematized in chant as the “reciting tone” or “dominant” of the mode. It didn’t have to be a fifth above the tonic, and for some modes the general opinion of which tone was the dominant changed over the centuries. It had nothing to do with triadic harmony.

Irish trad has nothing remotely like the structure of modal chant - notions like reciting tone and “flex” simply don’t apply.

What fits a bit better is some of the melodic theory used in modal music from India and the Middle East. They see each mode as coming with a set of standard cadential formulas. You get some of those in folk music from Western Europe as well (Irish trad not being all that different from anybody else’s in its choice of these).

Knowing modes is a handy way for a melody player to know what the general shape of the tune is. You can figure out a lot of it just by trial and error, but knowing the mode can help you know which notes should be emphasized or not, which notes you can amend without changing the basic shape of the tune, which alternate notes work and which don’t and why. Re: Just curious …

“Looks like it was fashionable to back reels with simple crotchets on notes of the scale as a bass line. That’s so interesting…”

Well, it would be interesting if it were true. However, the book is advertised as being with harpsichord accompaniment (or for harpsichord or pianoforte). It was the custom at that time to write out harpsichord parts as single bass lines, or the left hand of harpsichord parts as single bass lines. But the players were not expected to play like that. They were expected to fill out a whole harpsichord part themselves, complete with full harmony and counterpoint. In other words, you can’t really read anything into the apparent simplicity of the accompaniment.

Also, the accompaniment part in that book strikes me as something equivalent to editions from the 1960s - 1980s which were often required to add guitar chords. It has nothing to do with traditional playing. It’s about making the music acceptable to people who don’t play traditional music. In the case of the 20c books, this would be people who were used to ‘folk’ music (ie guitar-based, newly composed, sentimental songs extolling the virtues of the workers) and in the case of the 18c books, they would be pandering to rich folks who might buy the book and want to be able to play these nice, quaint, rural tunes in their posh drawing rooms with their posh harpsichord backing. Doesn’t make them less interesting as collections of tunes though.

To the point of the OP:

Championing the obvious, but: scales and chord progressions are *both* modal--you can say a given scale is mixolydian or dorian just as you can say that of a chord progression (or chord choices). So as players, we can think of either the tune itself falling into a certain mode, or the chords behind it falling into a certain mode, or (for those few ambi-auralists) both.

Because I’ve played both chord instruments and single-line melody instruments, my brain works either way and sometimes multi-tasks (uh oh) and does both simultaneously. So on fiddle, sometimes my variations and note choices are sparked by explorations around the notes of the scale I’m in, and sometimes they come from an understanding of the chord(s) behind the tune.

If you play swing, jazz, bluegrass, blues, or rock, these concepts and the ability to switch back and forth between scale-based and chord-progession-based improvisation are absolutely basic and fundamental.

Re: Just curious …

Of course there was a link between cello and harpsichord in the 18c. That was part of my point. It wasn’t a cello part. It was a bass. Sometimes, mainly if there was no melody line, figures were provided to guide the performers as to how to ‘realise’ the part. Where there was a melody, this was often not provided. What we saw in those 18c books was a typical drawing room piece of printed music, with a continuo part. We know that these were mostly not played as written. Why they should be played as written in this book rather than any other is beyond me.

Re: Just curious …

“A basic understanding of the Harp, Ireland’s national Instrument, clearly indicates that the tunes have been supported with harmony since Time Immemorial.”

No it doesn’t. The technique of the small wire harp - the older instrument - is purely melodic. The left hand doesn’t do chords, it damps the strings.

Daniel Dow’s “Ancient Scots Music” collection makes the point. He adds his own chords to harp tunes rom the 17th century or earlier (and without giving anything away about where he got those tunes from). Those harmonies are frequently quite bizarre. He must have felt obliged to provide a harmonization but didn’t have the compositional technique or musicological background to do it in an appropriate way. But with the dance music of his own time, he does a lot better.
