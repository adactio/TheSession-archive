Re: Beginner advice - how a tune is structured

Welcome to the music, glad you could join us.

Sessions vary in their practice, so the main thing is to suss out what the local custom is and work with that. That said, in general the default is “three”: three tunes of the same type are played three times each. There’s no “improvising” as in bluegrass, though sometimes people will play some “variations” (again, the degree of this you’ll come across will depend on the local norms at the session you’re at). It’s common to give a little grunt (usually transcribed as “hup!”) to signal that a change is coming up, others will signal with their eyes, recovering American players will sometimes lift a foot as is common in bluegrass and old-time playing. Some players will call out [their best guess at] the key of the tune they’re intending to play next as a signal to the strummy players in the room.

I suppose all of this amounts to a “secret code” of sorts - certainly it can feel like a session has some private language and customs. This is not usually an effort to baffle newcomers, though it can feel that way, it’s just what happens when people play together regularly for a long period of time.

Re: Beginner advice - how a tune is structured

Curious that no one has yet addressed the literal tune structure :-) Granted, it doesn’t matter so much if you learn your tunes by ear and do plenty of listening. But for some analytical-minded beginners, this could help avoid confusion down the road.

AABB is a common pattern for Irish tunes but it’s not the only one you will encounter. AB is another common pattern for Irish reels (where A and B are 8 bars long or 4 bars of music repeated twice). These AB tunes may sometimes be referred to as “single reels” or “reels played singly” (to avoid confusion with “the single reel”, a rare tune type). Some reels can be played as both AB and AABB, and it’s up to the person who started the tune to decide which version to play.

In the jig-time realm, AABB tunes are double jigs and AB tunes are either single jigs or slides. The latter are often notated in 12/8 (with 4 bars per part) and would rarely be played “double”. However, this convention is a relatively recent one and you’ll find slides notated in 6/8 in older collections making them AABB tunes. Set-dancers also use the same bar count for double jigs and slides.

Multi-part tunes (as in, three or more parts) often follow the same 8/16 bar pattern. Examples: Lucy Campbell (ABCD), The Bucks of Oranmore (ABCDE), The Gold Ring (AABBCCDD), The Frieze Breeches (AABBCCDDEE).

Some tunes have irregular structure which you just need to be aware of. In Castle Kelly, one part of the tune could be doubled while the other is played singly – I’ve heard it played as AB, ABB, and AABB – so you can never know for sure until the switch is made. The Maids of Mount Cisco (AABCC) and The Gravel Walks (ABCDD) are examples of irregular multi-part tunes that are always played that way. Liz Carroll’s “A Tune For The Girls” is an example of a composition with a custom arrangement (played AABB first time through, AABBC second time through, AABBCC third time through). Arrangements like this are relatively rare in informal sessions unless it’s a session favourite which several members know and like to play.

Check out this graph made from a limited sample size of tunes (reels present in the irishtune.info database of traditional music albums) – it shows AABB and AB tunes leading by a wide margin.

https://www.irishtune.info/rhythm/reel-structure.html

Re: Beginner advice - how a tune is structured

My general advice for playing in an Irish session would be to watch the person who started the tune and follow their lead. That solves most of the issues. As others have mentioned, signals for switching to the next tune vary and can be quite subtle – but you’ll have no trouble catching it as long as you’re watching and listening.

There is no “rule of three” at many sessions in Ireland, you just play the tunes as many times as it is enjoyable for you and everyone else. I’ve been told it’s quite common for sessions in England to play the tunes three times through (others might disagree!) and I’ve also noticed “international” sessions at various festivals lean towards that.

My theory is that the less the musicians are exposed to a living tradition around them (which may simply be non-existent in their area), the more they are prone to following the tropes from popular commercial recordings, where tunes are often played 3 times max (to maintain the excitement of arrangements for casual listeners).

These patterns of “N tunes three times each” or “three tunes two times each” come straight from the beginning of the recording era of Irish music in the 1920s when musicians had to fit medleys of tunes on one side of a 78 rpm record that is roughly 3 minutes long. Before the concept of sets was firmly established (and before pub sessions were a thing), a common way to play tunes would’ve been one tune at a time, as many times as the musician wanted – something that is still true for some musical traditions such as Norwegian and Swedish music (they don’t normally group tunes in sets, be it on recordings or in sessions).

There are no hard and fast rules at our session in Novi Sad but we do have an unwritten “play ‘em 4 times” advisory: one time to listen to the tune, one time to remember the tune, one time to finally play the tune in full force and one more time to add some variations to the tune or just relax and enjoy it. This largely applies to AABB tunes. AB tunes are so short in comparison that it makes sense to play them 4-5 times or more – unless no one happens to know the tune, then you might want to gracefully switch after a second time through :-)

In the end, while entire book(let)s have been written about session etiquette and a newcomer may understandably feel overwhelmed with the amount of unwritten rules, just remember that sessions are about enjoying the music and the collective experience of sharing tunes – there’s no rush and no expectations of you delivering a well-arranged performance. Just try and learn your tunes before you play them! ;-)

Slides and single jigs also follow the same AABB structure, but double jigs and single jigs notated in 6/8 have 8 bars per A or B part whereas slides have 4 bars. Re: Beginner advice - how a tune is structured

Perhaps the greatest strength of this site for me is the fact that when you click on a tune there are multiple “settings” (versions, arrangements).

Less-well-known tunes might only have a couple settings.

The more-well-known tunes might have a dozen settings or more.

When I’m learning a tune I go to its page and I spend a lot of time playing through ALL of the settings.

Why? Because each and every setting IS “the tune”, different facets of the tune, and all deserve to be explored.

In other words it’s not the dichotomy some imagine, where there’s a single unique string of notes that’s “the tune” and any deviation from that rigid sequence of notes is “improvisation” or “variations”.

So all settings are simultaneously “the tune” AND “variations”. Learning them all allows you to, while you’re playing, float between all the settings posted here (and settings not posted here) picking and choosing your path in the moment, which people not familiar with the music interpret as jazz-like improvisation.

(The exceptions are settings posted in keys I can’t really play on keyless flute or keyless uilleann chanter, and in some cases different tunes which happen to have been posted together with all the settings of the same tune.)

“I had been thinking of these variations as improvisation”

@Vikki Ings, could you define for us what you mean by “improvisation”?

I’ll explain. We once had an argument/discussion in a local session chat with a blues guy who questioned our understanding of what improvisation is and whether we improvise when playing traditional music. In the end it all boiled down to the definition of the term, which as it turns out can vary depending on your musical background and nerdiness.

For us tradheads improvisation basically meant a complete departure from the tune and a live creation of some new melody (probably within the constraints of the tune’s harmony), be it rehearsed or completely impromptu. That’s what Breton musicians do all the time in the modern setting: they play the tune, they depart from the tune to “improvise” along the harmony line, they go back to playing the tune or switch to the next one. This is not what Irish musicians usually do, most would say they “add variations” but not “improvise”.

For the blues guy, however, improvisation meant “applying musical ideas within the context shared by the players and listeners”. A musician uses certain music/harmony skills while operating within a certain musical context and fellow musicians and listeners are able to appreciate that creation or co-creation. So from his standpoint, what we call variation is also a form of improvisation within the narrow context of a tune or its phrase. Variations can likewise be spontaneous or prepared but even the former are rooted in a musician’s overall experience of playing traditional music, so that seems to fit.

As you can tell, the latter definition of improvisation is much broader than the former one and can be applied to a lot of things. Which I can now appreciate – but honestly, I still find our “narrow” understanding of improvisation to be more practical when discussing a certain music genre as opposed to the music theory.

However, the key takeaway of the story for me is that we need to agree on the definition of a term before the discussion begins – that rule alone could’ve prevented so many unnecessary heated arguments, corrections and well actuallys on The Session alone!

So what do you mean when you say “improvisation” and what is your current understanding of how the “variations” are conceived and played in traditional music?

Re: Beginner advice - how a tune is structured

“@Nico, confusing it might be but it’s not incorrect. They are called “single jigs” for a reason 🙂 … I did define “A” and “B” as 8-bar parts - what’s your definition of “A”?”
Sorry to nitpick, but the majority (vast majority, I believe) of people and sources would have single jigs written in 6/8, and so would be AABB, each part 8 bars, exactly as in your definition. And no, I don’t believe that the single jig is analogous to single reel - I think they’re separate terms derived from distinct origins. There are plenty of examples of double jigs that are only 16 bars long in 6/8. Writing slides in 12/8 means that each A part is only four bars, but either way the part is repeated, so I think it’s worth still describing it as a similar approach.

“By your logic 16-bar reels like Rolling in the Ryegrass are also AABB because they can be written as two 4-bar parts with repeats.”
Yes, functionally that’s exactly what it is. You can write a lot of 16 bar reels as two four bar parts, each repeated, or at least with the first part as a a four bar part repeated.

Another way of thinking about it (and the way I teach tunes) is that the basic structure of a standard Irish dance tune is, for each part: call and response, call and response (with the second response often being slightly different than the first). With reels, this set of “call and response, call and new response” is typically repeated. And in 16 bar reels, often the second part would be “call and first part response, new call and new response”. Reels are funny creatures because sometimes in 32 bar reels the call and responses are each longer, but sometimes it’s just a nominally 16 bar reel that’s been doubled (especially often when the second part has a second half completely different than the first and even more so when the first parts’ second response is markedly different from the first).

I wouldn’t get too hung up on it in general, but I think it’s worth pointing out the repetitiveness and reuse of phrases is a key feature of Irish dance tunes (and other traditions, really).

The semantics of “improvisation” but “variation” and “setting” also deserve some attention. If we start from the position that the notes are not the tune and vice versa, that the notes on the page are one way out of many paths through a particular tune, then I guess you could say that a “setting” is a map of one path, from start to end, while a “variation” is a detour at some particular spot on some path.

If we look at it that way, then it’s easy to see how “variations” could be improvised or canned, and it can be hard to tell the difference, even for the person playing them.

Re: Beginner advice - how a tune is structured

If you can put up with the (typical) overthink and sheer volume of data-dumping displayed in this thread, you’ll have a lovely and rewarding time with Irish trad. music!
Tunes are generally split into four or eight bar parts (typically labelled A, B, C etc.) . It’s almost always clear where one part ends and the next one starts.
These parts are played pretty much in unison, but with varied ornamentation dependant on instrument and personal preference.
Parts are repeated according to length and custom.
Sets are groups of tunes played one after the other. Some seem to have been passed down through the ages, some are newly put together. Sometimes the next tune in a set is known by everyone, sometimes only a few. Sometimes everyone swings into the next tune as if by magic.
There is no jazz-like improvisation, because there aren’t any fixed chord changes. That’s it.

I too love the concept of “the call and response” and it’s great for explaining how traditional tunes are composed in broad strokes. Thanks for mentioning it. But simple explanations stop working as soon as we hit an edge case like the Maids of Mount Cisco. The second part is technically BB but it’s 8 bars, twice shorter than part A. Each part has a call and response but how do you show the difference on paper? It’s much easier to avoid this duplicity altogether and define the minimal length of the tune’s building blocks for a schematic illustration. Since you don’t like AB (which Alan Ng used for his reel structure) – fine, we could be more granular and go by 4 bars. It’s for convenience, not for controversy!

In general, Jon has the right of it that Irish music has resisted being described by any one system for decades and it’s not about to change. @Vikki Ings, great, then all you really need to do is to go to the nearest Irish music session, observe and make friends. And do a bit of “fieldwork” while you’re at it. Take notes of the tunes you’d like to later listen to, or ask for permission to record some tunes and learn them at home – you don’t have to, but learning music from a living source helps form mental connections and associations for some of these jigs, reels, polkas, slides and hornpipes which number in the thousands. Taking the first steps in a new musical world is an exciting thing – enjoy your journey!

Re: Beginner advice - how a tune is structured

Vikki Ings, thank you for kicking off this thread for us to discuss. And thanks for anyone coming out of the woodwork to comment. It’s all good!

But, I have something to say which I hope conveys to beginners as well as tune players who have been doing this for much longer. The subject matter is tune structure. While it’s good to be curious about how the pieces fit together we’re still talking about tunes, or playing the melody. I would suggest that in sessions a common yet significant emphasis is placed on the concept of *the tune is king*.

For myself this carries across genres. It applies and evolves differently between various forms and styles of playing. So, when I listen to Irish tunes and play them in session I’m thinking less about structure. If anything… ~ I’m in the moment.

What does that mean in an Irish session?
It probably is listening, finding the lift, blending with the other players, appreciating playing with them and it’s ok if every set we play is less than perfect but every now and again magic does happen. And then it’s more than just ok.
