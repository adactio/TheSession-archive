I’ll answer the bowing q first. It’s a descent question:

20%? 50%? The trick is to vary it. Play some phrases all with the bow with loads of triplets, then play the same phrase next time round with just one long stroke and just rolls. Mix it up. That’s why the tunes are repeated. And make sure you can play tunes that cross strings with one direction of the bow. This is an important technique to have.

Next question: Jigs are indeed great. But when it comes down to it, reels are better. Any one who knows diddley music knows this. Any one who dissagrees with this doesn’t know diddley. Simple as that. Dow is spot on. And people who play reels are not at all bothered that every one else in the pub doesn’t know diddley.

“what is a slur ?”

Notes played with little to no discernable seperation between them. On fiddle multiple notes played with multiple fingers, but a single bow stroke, and a slide, multiple notes played with a single finger and bow stroke, are both types of slur, although each is different in effect.

Slurring notes or not slurring notes in tunes is a valuable variation of how you should approach a tune. It’s easy on a fiddle. On a flute its more subtle, in that it has to do with a reduction of (percussive) breathing and no tongues. On the pipes it’s even harder, you carefully change your style from the closed fingered “pippertypop” thing to a more open style. But whatever instrument, it’s a great way to vary the approach, and one of the bedrocks of diddling well.

Such subleties should not be lost in the persuit of diddley. Establish where the tune is, then miss bits out

How many of you try to play the fiddle staccato? Paddy Glackin does a great job of this, the famous Sligo fiddlers put it into their playing as well - when they were young Coleman and Morrison played with the piper Johnny Gorman, who was renowned as a staccato player. Also Frankie Gavin’s a fan of piping. I think it sounds grand myself, and can add a lot to a tune, but it’s taking me a lot of practice. Also it’s very tricky to get up to speed.
In the old days pipers considered “Open” piping “Very bad flute playing,” which is a stigma that still prevails these days - at least, stacatto (“tight”) technique is considered very impressive, also an indicator of which “camp” you’re in - a Liam O’Flynn or Paddy Keenan person, roughly speaking. The fact that Paddy’s one of the best tight players ever isin’t really a consideration - the open stuff stands out. The overall phrasing he uses is open.
If all you do for practice is go down to the sessions and thrash away you’re probably not interested in these distinctions.
