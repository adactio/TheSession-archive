It’s not a session if __ __ _ ___________

Imagine the first sessions when musicians truly did not have rules; simply a love of playing tunes with your mates. But now thanks to global social networking (gone awry), advances in abc interfacing, & young healthy horny belters sporting colourful caps people are becoming a bit guarded. The session of the innocent is becoming the session of jaded geriatrics.

The mantra, “There are no rules!” is slow but steady becoming a cry for help. A faint shadow of it’s former free spirit.

Are there rules? I’m hearing a low hum which seems to be increasing & is suggestive that what you (in the most general sense) call a session is not a session.

I don’t think sessions have rules in the sense of ‘keep off the grass’ or ‘the ball must pass over the net and land within the service court’ - we wouldn’t have the (frustrating/entertaining) discussions if they were capable of such unambiguous expression. As Al said, they’re relative to a particular social group but surely more elastic and indefinite than rules which can be formulated (we might well disagree in ‘our’ session about whether an outsider’s behaviour was OK or not).

For instance, is there a rule about how many times you play one tune in a set? It’s probably ‘usually’ three, or four , or two but I doubt that the ‘wrong’ number causes any upset. If a stranger comes to the session and plays a tune ten times through there’ll probably be (inward or outward) sighing and eye-rolling, if I do that then my “mates”(to use the Gillian terminology) will be laughing or throwing beer mats at me before I’m in double figures.

Re: It’s not a session if __ __ _ ___________

Ben - I’ve never, ever been to a session where any “rules” have been stipulated, other than the normal, respectful behaviour towards other musicians which is expected - and this especially applies if you are the visitor to an established session which has been going on for maybe quite a number of years.
There are undoubtedly norms of behaviour in sessions in Ireland, but these are unspoken, understood, and accepted by all, and only occasionally have to be explained perhaps to visiting musicians - usually politely, but not if a more robust approach is required. The musicians I’ve met and played with in Ireland take their music seriously - they don’t worship it, and in the vast majority of occasions, they have great fun in playing it, and love it. But crass insensitivity, and not adhering to these norms is not usually tolerated. and their traditional music is in a very healthy state because of that refusal to put up with any behaviour which might kill a session.

This - in my experience - is not the case in sessions in Scotland. There is undoubtedly an “ anything goes” mentality abroad at the moment , - has been for the last 10 years - and we’re all too polite to ever say anything about it - myself included, [ apart from one or two occasions, which got me the reputation for being a cross between Hannibal Lecter and Dracula ] . Apparently a few members here are less squeamish about that , and I have enormous respect for them for that.
I can’t comment from experience on sessions in England or North America, but I strongly suspect in the majority of occasions, the situation is more likely to be similar to my experiences in Scotland than in Ireland.

Re: It’s not a session if __ __ _ ___________

One of the more irritating unspoken rules, though sometimes it is voiced too, is that there should never be anything critical or judgmental spoken, in other words, when someone does muscle in on an established session tradition and makes a big enough splash, nothing should be said. I have come across something like this in North America, a kind of ‘anything goes’. But it’s still a session, if not one I’d choose to frequent.

The other extreme might be the folk club session, where people take turns. I was told about and invited to one not that long ago, and it was just ,for me, very weird, though the people who took part in it were quite happy. Most had sheet music, and it was with people all around in a semi-circle or circle, and they did the round robin thing, each person in turn started a set. But, it was still a session, with its own set of rules.

The sessions I enjoyed most in Ireland were more as Kenny describes, nothing formal, just basic hospitality and courtesy, respect for one another, and for the music. Some were in homes, some were Comhaltas related, some were very community oriented, some were in very small pubs, and others were in back rooms and didn’t take off really until the official closing time, when the front door was locked and the curtains drawn. Those that didn’t seem to have a dominant single force but just seemed to happen, and yet, someone had organized them, and most were at least weekly.

But the odd little ones we’ve stumbled across here and there, whether or not we were comfortable with them for our purposes, were still sessions in their own right, or by their own definition…

And then there have been the ones with extra, free drinks, sometimes on the pub, sometimes on the regulars, and even food too, and sometimes dancers too…

They’re all ‘sessions’ because that’s what they were called, that’s how people thought about them, whether or not we were comfortable or not with them.

Kenny writes in response to my saying sessions are whatever the people having them want them to be: “Agreed 100% ……………..but I will always choose whether or not I want to join in. End of………………….”

Exactly… this is the point that many people fail to understand. When you go to someone else’s session it’s up to you to decide whether or not you want to ask to participate… not whether it’s right or wrong according to your own criteria. I have come across sessions that I either don’t find to my liking or that I can’t participate in… it’s very simple really. But too many people seem to think sessions they come across are supposed to adhere to some universal set of session rules… but any rules are only relevant to the people having the session.

I have posted session guidelines on a website for our local session’s pub, but the people having sessions there are in agreement with those terms and I posted them for visitors and newcomers to those particular sessions. If I’m out and about I don’t expect all the sessions I encounter to have the same guidelines. Many do, but only because many of the points in my guidelines are common among sessioneers.

So what I’m saying is there really are no universal rules or criteria that defines what a session is… you either like it or you don’t… that’s it. The people having the session are the ones who decide what it is or isn’t.

Article: “Getting to the Heart of the Music: Idealizing Musical Community and Irish Traditional Music Sessions” by Helen O’Shea

Abstract: The power of shared musical experience has inspired scholars to theorize collective musical performance as capable of producing an embodied, transcendent experience of an ideal society. Scholars who have written about the group performance of Irish traditional music demonstrate a similar understanding. Such models tend to idealize musical performance (as if it always produced a transcendent experience) and to elide the experiences of participants, representing them as harmonious (at one) and homogeneous (as one). Drawing on fieldwork conducted among musicians playing Irish traditional music in East Clare, this article considers the social and musical consequences of idealizing group performance and proposes a more nuanced understanding of musical community as a process of dialogue.
