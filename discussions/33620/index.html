Professional musicians: Do you pay to play?

You probably like to eat out or visit clubs where there is live music, right?

You probably don’t realize that most venues do not pay the bands that play. Yes, it’s true. Some area venues offer things like “pass the hat”, or they will give a meal or drinks to the band, but many do not pay an actual salary. Some area venues will also offer a percentage of the door as an incentive for bands to bring their fans.

Can you imagine a plumber or electrician or attorney being paid by “pass the hat”, a meal or drinks or a percentage of the door for that day? No, neither can I.

Venues that don’t pay bands are hurting the local music scene. Read the article on the website below to learn how and why. It’s important.

Here’s how you can help:

1. Ask the wait person of your favorite venue if the band is being paid.
2. If the band is not being paid (or it’s pass the hat, % of the door, etc), ask to speak to the manager.
3. Tell the manager that you like the venue and you want to come back, but you think the band should be paid. It’s important that you communicate this information to the manager and not the wait person. The wait person can’t do anything about it, but the manager can and will take your comments seriously.
4. Leave a tip for the band with the waitperson.
5. Bands/Musicians: update the database with known venues that pay!

Thank you!

http://dontpaytoplay.weebly.com/

Re: Professional musicians: Do you pay to play?

Here in Colorado, where I live, the paying gigs in the surrounding area dried up around 2005 -06. Several things happened that probably contributed to the demise such as old club owners selling out and new owners being to cheap to pay. The biggest contributor IMHO was, the rise of “Open Mic” nights. The local, and I use the term loosely “Musicians Guild” started the push to try to increase membership and give local talent (I use this term loosely as well) a venue. The caliber of musicianship and ability varies from bad to mediocre to a few that show promise. Many of us, who were members of the guild, protested but the leadership chose to ignore the protest in favor of more dues coming in and giving “the New Talent” a chance to show themselves. So now we have at least a dozen venues in town that feature Open Mic as their main entertainment 2 to 3 nights a week. You’re not even given a free drink or meal for your “moment in the spotlight”.
From 1988 to 2005, we had a weekly paying gig at the original Irish Pub here. In 2005 the new owner chose to not renew the contract we had. We found another pub that kept us going for a little over a year, but then it changed hands as well and is now defunct. And that is pretty much the sorry state the local music scene finds itself in now.
I won’t play for free unless it’s for a very good cause. I spent too much time, money and sweat, blood and tears to get to the level I play at, and to maintain it. I hope others feel this way as well. When you’re willing to give your talents away, you cheapen that for which you’ve worked hard to attain.
I’ll close with a brief story. We’d spent some time working up a demo disc with a half dozen or so tunes to try to drum up some gigs. We went back to one such venue and were told that the management had decided against live music. We had to listen to this drivel while our own demo was being played over the joints sound system. We redid our demos after that to only contain a short section of the song rather than the whole tune. Live and learn I suppose.

Re: Professional musicians: Do you pay to play?

For me it’s a question of quality and commitment. I’m a musician trying to make a living, but I do go to sessions/open mics and play for free as well. If I’m there, I’m usually doing it for fun, so I’m much more relaxed, and more willing to try new things out, dig up older stuff, and otherwise risk not sounding good. I’ll also chat, eat, walk away for a bit and come back, and generally screw around. If I’m getting paid, I’m sure to be playing stuff that’s well-rehearsed, and I play longer sets without much of a break except for a sip of water or maybe some on-stage banter. The people paying me get more of my attention and focus, and benefit more from my practicing and training.

The issue I see often is that people who love a certain style end up being more discerning than others about it. You may care that the person playing the Kesh at a pub gig sounds wooden or out of tune, but most of the punters probably don’t notice or care. The owner then has a choice: get someone to play for free or drinks, or pay someone who may play better. If most people don’t know or care enough to notice the difference, it’s good business sense to choose the former. Now, I would argue that putting a great musician up on stage will make people stay longer and appreciate the venue more, even subconsciously. But it’s harder to quantify, so you can’t blame a publican, especially one who’s working at thin margins anyway, for choosing the cheaper route.

The thing you can do to change this is vote with your cash. Spend money at places that have good live music, so they can continue to have it. If you want music at a funeral, wedding, etc., pay someone you like to do it. Complaining about it doesn’t help anyone, and it doesn’t put any more money into the pockets of musicians or musician-friendly owners.

As for small men and big men, it’s always been that way. There have always been acts that can fill stadium at £300 per head, and others struggling to make ends meet. The earning potential of the big boys has actually been hit far harder than the small man by recent social and technological changes.

But what the small man has lost over the last decade or so are the free entry, paid musician pub gigs, where the publican was able to pay the band from the increased bar takings they generated. Those gigs have gone not because other people are willing to play for free, but because bar profits have been squeezed to the point where it is no longer financially viable for a pub to pay a band. If there were no free musicians there still wouldn’t be gigs for paid bands, it would be widescreen sports TV or quiz nights instead.

There is still plenty of work and income for professional bands playing paid entry concerts and festivals, but the route to getting your foot on that professional ladder has changed - these days you have to build up a fan base first, using whatever means you can - youtube and social media, or even playing for free.

i think the “pay to play” thing came out of the rock milieu. i live in one of the rock-band capitals of the globe, and “pay to play” is ubiquitous here at showcase venues, or at venues where bands “pay to play” on off nights to get exposure. supposedly. i think more creative or artistic bands would pass this up but play for free on occasion like in my post just preceding this one---when the quality of the experience is potentially cool or creative or interesting enough that it seems worth while…

look, for the history of traditional music until, say, the 1960s, people who played it had trades or professions. paddy canny said in the interview that he and the tulla ceili band went all over the countryside to play dances just for the sheer fun of it, and you were lucky if you got a pint or two out of the deal. to the degree that pub owners started to pay irish musicians real gig money--that was a five-minute bubble born of a short fad for this music that has pretty much passed. but all it’s done is revert to the way things have been for traditional music.

and….i really don’t think the current era of the “career” or “pro” folk musician is showing us much that meets the bar set by those folks who played for free or next to nothing after they got the cows in for the night. god, i never want to see another “Kickstarter” or Facebook post for some professional-in-my-own-mind’s self-made CD, badgering people with specious rhetoric equating “supporting this music” with, you know, supporting them. fascinatingly, many incredible contemporary traditional irish musicians do have put in the education or training to garner professions or trades, and the music of these folks is as good and in many cases better, than that of the “i’m a professional musician” cohort…
