Experienced guitarist, new to trad sessions – where to start?

Hi everyone,

I’m an experienced composer and guitarist, but new to Irish trad sessions. I went along to a local pub session this evening to listen and get a feel for things, and I’d love to start joining in. I’m a capable musician, but fairly green when it comes to playing in a Irish folk session, so I’d really appreciate some practical advice.

Firstly, could anyone recommend five or six well-known reels, or perhaps a couple of commonly played reel sets, that would be good starting points to learn before joining in?

Secondly, I have two steel-string acoustics: a Martin D12 dreadnought and a Larrivée parlour guitar. There was already a very good guitarist at the session, playing a deep-bodied guitar, so I’m wondering whether the smaller Larrivée might complement his sound better, or whether the dreadnought would be more suitable. Any thoughts?

Finally, I’d welcome any advice on session etiquette, particularly when to join in, how to approach tunes I don’t know, and how best to accompany without getting in the way. I’m keen to find my feet respectfully and contribute when I’m ready.

Thanks in advance for any advice!

Danny

Re: Experienced guitarist, new to trad sessions – where to start?

Honestly, my first advice would be to get a whistle and learn some tunes on it. This is not “don’t play guitar in sessions” - though, to be frank, I’m always happier when there’s no guitars - but rather, if you want to play along with the tunes it’s very helpful to have a lot of them in your head.

You can also learn to flatpick tunes - I do a lot of this at home, and it’s great fun - but unless you’re in a very small session with very sensitive and skilled players, you’ll have a hard time being heard picking the tunes on the guitar.

As for “which tunes”, the tunes played at your local session are probably the best ones to focus on. Listen for a while, and when you recognize a tune that you’ve heard there before, that’s probably one you want to learn. It’s also possible to get the “most popular” tunes on this site here https://thesession.org/tunes/popular - these are for the most part well-known tunes that are probably familiar to the folks at your session.

Re: Experienced guitarist, new to trad sessions – where to start?

Ah…

One thing to understand here is that trad tunes don’t necessarily have set chords, so if there is already a good guitar player at the session, adding another guitar may “muddy” the sound as you may not be playing the same chords or the same rhythms.
Sometimes what happens in these situations is that the players take turns at accompanying. That is, if the resident player is willing to share. Sometimes they are not and then you are kind of stuck.

I’d suggest going along several more times and listen to the guitar player and study what they are doing - whether they play the same each time through a tune or vary it on subsequent passes, progressions and chord forms, rhythm on various tune types, etc. It’s much easier accompanying tunes you know (in your head) and it helps to be able to hear changes.

Cross-posted with Jon. Yes to flatpicking tunes on the guitar as way to learning them and improving your chops.

Re: Experienced guitarist, new to trad sessions – where to start?

By D12 you mean a twelve string guitar, right? While there is a place in the right session for a big jangly sound, you might find some musicians can be a bit side-eye about it if they don’t know you.

Another alternative, along the lines of Jon’s suggestion, is to acquire a mandolin, mandola, bouzouki, or cittern and use that to learn some tunes. Tenor banjo is another option but again can be a bit much till you’re confident.

In terms of tunes to learn, the “popular” listing on the tunes page on here is usually standards that everyone knows, but because everyone knows them, people can be a bit bored with them, especially if they often hear them played by less experienced musicians. In a similar vein if you google here for “Dow’s List” you’ll find a useful starting list.

What I would do is learn enough tunes from those sources that you feel confident about learning tunes, finding different versions, and playing them idiomatically, and then at your local session every week just pick up one or two tune names, or record them (with permission), and learn them over time. Every session repertoire is different so the best source for that session is the session itself. Don’t worry how much you can play; the trick is to just go back week after week and build experience.

Re: Experienced guitarist, new to trad sessions – where to start?

FWIW, the Martin D12 is a 6-string guitar.

Regardless, many, many sessions won’t tolerate two guitars, certainly not backing simultaneously. Some sessions barely tolerate a single strummer. If there’s already an established guitar backer, they may politely bite your head off if you try to sit in. And many melody players don’t want a wall of sound drowning the actual tunes.

As DonaldK has said elsewhere, learn to play melody—it’s easier than backing. I agree with Calum’s suggestion to apply your flatpick skills to mandolin, bouzouki, or tenor banjo. A tenor guitar is also an option, although best for quieter sessions.

“It’s lovely to find such an active and friendly forum!”

I think they are all busy on the other thread. :-P

Seriously though, as others have said, the best thing is to familiarise yourself with the tunes and style of music in general. When I first started going to sessions, I played guitar accompaniment as I thought it was “easy” just to play the chords but aside from the very obvious simple tunes it is anything but… Also, all the advice about a maximum of one guitar in each session is valid as it is very possible to do lots of different things which might well work but not if another guitarist is doing something different. Aside from that, too many guitars and other non melody instruments can drown out the actual music. There’s also different rhythm possibilities to consider and various combinations of chord voicing. You’ll hear lots of discussion about modes and the like too. As I say, there’s not only one way to do things but it has to work and not clash with anything else.

Re getting familiar with the tunes, playing another instrument isn’t essential but but you have to listen to all the music and get the tunes into your head. Many follow a similar pattern and you can predict or instinctively feel what is required but even they can surprise you at time.

“Flat picking” on guitar is the obvious route but mandolin is good too. Not because it’s any easier..that’s a myth…It’s only so in the early stages. However, it’s also a good half way house to playing the fiddle. Then you don’t need to worry about the guitar at all. :-)

↳ Posted by Michael Eskin on September 23rd, 2026.

Re: Experienced guitarist, new to trad sessions – where to start?

As far as tunings go, it’s a pretty personal thing. The two most popular tunings for trad music are drop D and DADGAD, you might as well try both and see what clicks for you.

In either case, I think a lot of players appreciate a “less is more” approach to harmony. You don’t need to play all of the strings all of the time, or even all of the notes in the chord. Look for sparse voicings that suggest the chord but leave some room for the tune.

In a similar vein, when it comes to rhythm, you don’t need to spell out the beat - the players don’t need any help there, and if they do it’s not something you’ll be able to help with. Don’t bother learning purported generic “strumming patterns” that people will try to sell you. Instead, you want to find something that supports the particular way the players are playing this particular tune tonight. And above all, don’t ever play something just to be playing something. If you’re not feeling the tune, it’s a great kindness to all concerned if you just lay out and listen.

Re: Experienced guitarist, new to trad sessions – where to start?

Hi Danny, and welcome to thesession! It’s great that you’re interesting in getting involved in this music! The discussion above certainly provides you with some good starting points, and also points out to you that the music is considered complete without any accompaniment at all… But good accompaniment in this music is in demand, and you’re starting out in the right way, by asking questions! You will find that you will get widely different answers to some of the questions, but that can be illuminating, too!

I started in this music on guitar, and progressed through bouzouki (so I could more easily accompany and play melody), some mandolin, and right down the slippery slope into the tenor banjo, where I play melody almost exclusively these days…

The things that really tripped me up at the outset were that the tunes often do things that were very strange to me. For instance, we play a lot in Mixolydian modes (Dmix and Amix are pretty common), and that was pretty unfamiliar to me as a guitarist. But Irish tunes do a lot of strange things, in general. A lot of the music is considered “modal”, where it may be switching back and forth between modes with the same root (some people prefer to think of it as using more accidentals, instead of switching modes). But tunes will sometime switch keys in the middle of the tune, too. At any rate, the twists and turns of this music can be surprising sometimes, even to experienced musicians that play other genres. So knowing the tunes is a big part of being able to accompany them well.

With regards to multiple accompanists at a session, different sessions will have different feelings about this, of course. But if you’re playing at the same time with another accompanist, it can be important to provide some contrast. If the other accompanist is generally playing straight chords, as mentioned, you might be choosing different chords that might work quite well with the tune, but not work with what the other accompanist is playing. So in those cases, it can be beneficial to switch your playing to a more sparse accompaniment. (More of the “tinkly” harmonics, or a contrapuntal line, for instance). But a lot of sessions will expect the accompanists to take turns.

I also want to reiterate to you that the melody players are the ones defining the rhythm! The accompaniment should follow their lead, and support what they are playing. You should never feel like you are “providing the rhythm” that they are playing on top of, which is generally how a lot of other styles of music are built, with the melody (and vocal lines) sitting on top of the “rhythm section”. The melody players are also likely to be moving their accents around, and playing tunes a bit differently each time through. So it’s important for you to be listening carefully, and adjusting what you’re doing to what they’re doing.

So again, welcome, and here’s hoping that you’re beginning the journey to becoming one of the great accompanists that are in demand!
