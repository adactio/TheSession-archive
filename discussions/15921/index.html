Are we too elitist?

You know, I’ve been reading these discussions now for a couple of years and over that time noticed peoples comments on the playing of other performers and fellow musicians. Now I may be wrong, (I frequently am) but it seems to methere is an increasing trend toward elitism in ITM.

Of course it may just be that I am getting older and more crotchety. Indeed my friends tell me that I do a grand impersonation of a grumpy old man. Nonetheless, this apparent trend to ‘slang off’ YouTube videos, performances and fellow ‘sessioners’ disturbs me. Last year I went to a festival and enjoyed the company of many fine musicians who honoured me by allowing me to sit in with them. Now I do not profess to be a good player. In fact if I was to be critical of my own skills, I could use a four letter word that starts with “C” and ends with “P” (I am confident you can fill in the blanks). These wonderful people welcomed me and accepted me totally without judgement. I was not surprised because it is a welcome I have found everywhere I have been.

One of my attractions to ITM was the fact that the musicians I encounteredoffered nothing less than unstiniting support and encouragement. Perhaps I have been lucky, or maybe I just know the right people.

Whatever my fortune, I pray that ITM does not fall to the depths of sniping at other performances as I hear on these boards. I hope that sessions are far more accommodating than the ‘bad news stories’ I read of daily. I came to ITM because the music ‘called’ to me - an experience I am sure we can find in common. We share a passion .. a love of traditional music, and with such a love must come the chance of fellowship and togetherness that brings with it acceptance and tolerance.

So whether you play flute, fiddle, whistle, mando … and yes .. even a bodhran. Let’s all sit together and if teh msuicanship isn’t up to the standard of a Spillane, Begley or Shannon, does it matter as long as the musician gave it their best shot? A long time ago I trained to be a teacher and I was taught that if you want to give feedback that people take on board, offer two affirmations and then one criticism. I left teaching but the feedback has served me well in life. So - a suggestion .. let’s stop the bitching and smart comments and just sit together in wondrous harmony and enjoy the craic!

D

Re: Are we too elitist?

I don’t know what videos or comments you’re talking about, but if someone puts up their stuff on youtube, they’re inviting negative comments as well as positive. If a musician cares about the music, then they will take negative comments on board, and either work hard on the aspects of their playing that have been criticized, or else decide that the comment is not valid and can be disregarded. I’ve learnt more about my own music from inviting negative criticism than by doing anything else. Sometimes you’ve just got to grit your teeth and take the beating. I’m afraid that’s largely the reality of how people learn trad. The people who don’t understand this end up playing mediocre music in sessions for 40 or 50 years of their lives. It’s those people who have the big egos, not the people who are giving and taking criticism. Criticism is a good thing, and you should think carefully about whether it’s actually bitching or not. Here’s the difference:

“That girl on the fiddle can’t keep time to save her life” = criticism. She should work on her rhythm and timing.

“I met that girl once when I was in Ennis. She’s fat and doesn’t know how to dress and she’s a crap musician but thinks she’s amazing” = bitching = disregard.

Re: Are we too elitist?

I’d like to point out that there is a big difference between criticizing skill versus criticizing style.

Skill comes with experience, everyone is at a different level at any given time, and there’s usually no real need to comment on it.

Style is something else. It does not necessarily improve with experience, has nothing to do with skill level or how “elite” you are, and is absolutely fatal to the music if it’s too far off.

If you’re a skilled player in a trad band who doesn’t know that the style is not right and you aren’t doing enough listening to figure it out on your own, how are you going to become a true trad player if no one ever says anything? If you were in that situation, what would you want?

Re: Are we too elitist?

I think Andy V hits the point of my original analysis - that there is no ‘correct form’ of traditional music. Those that insist on an idealised notion of what they presume is traditional purity are guilty of the elitism I identified in my first posting.

Wolfbird contests an interesting opposite which purports that the ‘correct’ style is born by the bearers of the tradition. But who are these bearers? Surely we all are ‘bearers’ of the tradition with a responsibility for perpetuating and encouraging a LIVING tradition. After all, tradition like language is not static - it is alive, vibrant and ever-changing.

Now I do not condemn those elitists I mentioned earlier. Indeed it leads me to consider whether it is sometimes necessary to have these ideological ‘puritans’. After all, it could be argued that there would never have been a folk revival had there not have been an Ewan Maccoll or an A.L. Lloyd.

My only fear is when the elitists hold and maintain the centre stage

Re: Are we too elitist?

Wolfbird;

The argument you refer to is that of R.P. Elbourne, outlined in his article ‘A Question Of Definition’ published in the Journal of the International Folk Music Council (JIFMC) in about 1975. There’s been a lot of publishing of papers since then, but the main downfall of Elbourne’s argument is that he relies on the concept of ‘traditionally minded cultures.’ Personally, I’ve read far too many excellent rebuttals of his paper to accept it: however for an overview of the main trouble with traditionally minded cultures, Eric Hobsbawm and Terrance Ranger (eds) ‘The Invention of Traditions’ is worth a read.

In context of the YouTube video, unless the violinist states she is referring to a certain traditionally minded culture in the music she plays there is no argument that what she does is wrong, simply that it does not coincide with any of the ‘traditionally minded cultures’ we are familiar with. However, I suspect her troubles lie in her technique, not her concept of style.

There’s a number of other articles worth a read, however as none of them are in the public domain I can’t post direct links. However, the following are very interesting if anyone has recourse to follow them up (ATHENS in a public library gives you access to JSTOR, which is where I accessed these articles from - although I went via my uni)
M. Karpeles, ‘The Distinction Between Folk and Popular Music’, JIFMC
M. Karpeles, ‘The Definition of Folk Music’, JIFMC
W. Wiora, ‘The Conception of Authentic Folk Music’, JIFMC
And, A.L. Loyd’s ‘Folk Song in England’; & D Harker’s ‘Fake-song’ are worth checking out too.

Re: Are we too elitist?

Every folk tradition has its accepted forms, although they are often mutable, especially when an extremely strong player with a lot of influence pushes at the boundaries or brings in new approaches.
Regarding the youtube clip that started this discussion, the woman has a gorgeous tone and clear crisp articulation that many would envy. But she does not play within the accepted forms of ITM. That does not make her music garbage, merely different.
I play in a group with my wife, who has a fairly traditional approach, a classically trained silver flute player, and a jazz guitarist who has always been intrigued by Irish music but never played it. We use music stands, and sometimes arrange what we do. We often use harmonies. We use more classical approaches especially when we play in weddings or formal functions. But mostly, we all sing. We do not claim to be a traditional Irish music group, but there are those who mistake what we do for trad. If there is any tradition we fit, it is probably the Clancy Brothers. I find this a fun alternative to the straightahead trad approach of the local sessions I attend, and hope there is enough room in the world for different approaches!
