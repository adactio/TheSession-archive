Good musicians playing “daggy tunes”

I recently read a post which stated something like “If the tune is good enough for John Carty, it’s good enough for me”

So, you are in a session in a pub or at a festival when a musician you admire joins the company and plays a tune that you regard with some disdain for whatever reason. It could be Paddy Keenan who starts “Kesh jig” or Andy Irvine plinking out Dennis Murphy’s and John Ryan’s polkas (Mind you, I still like all these tunes) or another great musician playing something else which you consider to be a bit “naff”. How do you react?
You could sit there all smug and say “I don’t play that one. It’s a bit of cliche” or “Is it not time you were learning a few new tunes, Paddy?”. Perhaps, you can be two faced and play the tune--or listen intently--saying “I’ve always loved that tune”.
You might also argue that because a particular tune is associated with this musician, then this is an exception.
My response would be just to be play the tune(if I could) and enjoy myself. The chances are that I would probably see it in a new light and, after all, there’s no such thing as a really bad tune, is there? :-)

It strikes me that one of the issues is that one person’s daggy tune is another’s classic, depending on the context and delivery. Tunes that might be overdone in one session / one pub / one part of a country, might be completely revelatory in another.

Generally the tunes that seem to be most often derided are those that are most popular or well known. But presumably everyone liked them at one time or else they would have remained obscure and lets face it there is no shortage of obscure tunes in trad music.

I guess that well known tunes, like obscure ones, have their place and role to play and neither should be done to excess!

Yep, They’re all boring, repititious, annoying and tasteless tacky ear candy. In fact all the tunes are. Did you know that there was originally, many years ago, only one tune ever written. It was so good that everyone just kept repeating it over and over an over again, sometimes getting it wrong, and other people picked up the wrong bits, and yet more people got the wrong bits wronger and wronger every time they played the tune. Not only did they and others get the wrong bits so wrong, they drank so much alcohol while they played, that they forgot what the name of the original tune was, and were so embarrassed at the memory loss that they just made up names for them on the spot. Some couldn’t even think up a name when asked, so they just said the name of the person next to them at the bar. That’s why you have Joe Cooley’s, Benny Sullivan’s, etc.

That’s how the tune got so many names, and everyone else made the mistake of thinking that they were all different tunes, because by this time they all had different names, and were now being played differently. And of course, so many years had passed that no-one could remember the real name of the tune. The honest people just admitted that they couldn’t remember the name, so when the music collectors came along, they collected hundreds of them and called them “No name”, or “Gan Naim” in Irish.

Daggy tunes? Well, it’s obvious now that I’ve explained it all.

I LIKE the Bucks. But I’m always trying to play it like Seamus Ennis - tight, swingy, and interesting. I’m not even close to Seamus’s league, but am enjoying the results of my effort.

Even the Kesh and the Washerwoman can be salvaged if you’re determined to play them with some invention and a bit of fire. Another thing: it takes time to get to know a tune well enough to experiment with it successfully.
