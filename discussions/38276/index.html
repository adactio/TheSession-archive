Re: Bow hold, Cape Breton style?

I don’t think it’s a Cape Breton thing - Cape Breton technique is generally closer to classical than Irish and other traditions are. Nor is it an authentic baroque technique - baroque players held the bow at the frog, although in the early part of the period, before screw adjusting frogs, the thumb was placed on the hair to control tension.

Where you do find players choking the bow it’s usually because they play a lot of fast jigs and reels, and are willing to sacrifice power and the ability to play long legato notes and phrases in exchange for a bit of speed.

Re: Bow hold, Cape Breton style?

Mark M, it’s how a lot of classical players hold a modern bow if they’re trying to get close to a ‘period’ interpretation\technique, not because that’s how baroque players held their bows, but because it’s closer to the length and balance of a baroque bow.
Sligo cousin, I don’t remember seeing cape Breton fiddlers using it, but it’s common enough in Irish trad. There are plenty of very capable fiddlers who don’t hold their bows that way mind you, so it’s not a prerequisite or anything. It makes short, fast, light bowing easier, arguably, at the expense of long powerful bowing. Again, you can do both or either and still sound great, or awful for that matter. I would say that how many fingers you have on the stick and how rigid or flexible your hand is has a far greater effect on your sound than where exactly on the bow your hand is, but that’s just me.

Re: Bow hold, Cape Breton style?

As to the original question, there’s no advantage to holding the bow up the stick. It might seem like it makes it easier to control, but it’s a limiting factor. You’re effectively shortening the length of the usable bow (so you’d struggle with slow airs, etc).

You’ll mank up the bow hair with constant skin contact (and maybe the wood too) - so if you do decide to use the whole bow, you’ll get a problem when you hit the manked-up area.

You can hold a hammer halfway up the shaft, and still hit the nail …

I live in Cape Breton; it is very, very common here. Most players (except from the Northside, which has an Irish tradition) don’t play with an Irish ‘long’ bow…. tends more to the short and sharp kind of bowing, although that is a big generalization. You folks are all probably well aware of that. My daughter (15, started playing at five or six), has been taught to hold the bow at the frog, but her teacher, who is a great fiddler, is also a ‘violinist’ and concertmaster of the local orchestra (daughter is in that too), and comes out of that Northside tradition so all his students hold at the frog.

So, I’m not sure that it holds that it is an ‘older’ way of doing it, but I do see many or most of the older players holding it that way, and the younger ones holding it at the frog. Except for all the ones that are the other way round.

Re: Bow hold, Cape Breton style?

I think perhaps whether it is an advantage or not might depend on who you are. If you have learned to control the bow holding it at the frog, then changing to a choked grip won’t give you any advantage. But for anyone else it does make the bow easier to control, reducing bounce or ricochet and allowing you to play faster than you could with a less controlled bow held at the frog. The other point where there might be a slight advantage is in changing strings - your hand is nearer to the strings, so the movement needed to change strings is more economical. (But against that, the chances of accidentally hitting an adjacent string are increased.)

Re: Bow hold, Cape Breton style?

> I’m also curious about the reason, if any, for this grip. <

As with all physical and musical questions: Try it, and learn from your discoveries.
Words cannot replace experience, but I will mention what I have learned.

I wondered about this years ago, and tried it. I learned that the effect is noticeable, even tho the change is slight, such as a few mm or 1/4 inch. The reason is because moving the hand from its accustomed place changes the amount of bow on each side of the hand, thus changing the weight of the bow on each side of the hand. The lower (frog) portion of the bow is always a counterweight to the portion higher than the hand, and how much of a counterweight it is allowed to be does make a physical and musical difference.

Moving the hand higher makes the bow’s upper portion lighter and the lower portion heavier. With your hand higher up the stick, notice that your hair-string ‘sweet spot’ (or spots) is automatically in a different place higher on the hair. This balance is useful when playing lighter, faster music. Less bounce is produceable (purposefully or accidentally) when the hair-string contact is higher on the stick and the bow ‘halves’ are more evenly weighted -- ie: your hand is higher up the stick. String crossings are easier due to the more-evenly-balanced weight above and below the hand. String crossings are also easier because ‘the bow is shorter’ and therefore less motion of the R arm and/or hand is/are required to cross strings.

Your hand higher on the stick will be useful for playing fireplace music, in cars, and any music where it helps the musical feel, such as quiet, light, fast, or perhaps to effect a regional sound. However, as YouTube shows, higher hand placement does not preclude playing loudly. And, if the hand is only a bit higher, long sounds are possible.

Your hand lower on the stick allows more weight above the hand, making playing louder, easier to play more strings at once, and bow bouncing more readily available.

Obviously, the more the RH moves up or down the stick, the more these opposite effects will pertain. Therefore, when experimenting, go to the opposite extremes where the bow does not operate, and find the places between the extremes where things work well for you. It all depends on you: what you play, and the way you play it.

You will also notice that bow hair tension affects your physical and musical approaches and their results. Experienced (discovered) mixing of hair tension and hand placement will provide the most musical results.

Having made my personal discoveries, I adjust hair tension and move my hand up or down slightly according to the music and the venue, sometimes changing both from one piece of music to the next.

Here’s to exciting experiments and musical playing!

Re: Bow hold, Cape Breton style?

Something to keep in mind: Every bow is slightly different in weight and balance. Every fiddle is slightly different in responsiveness to the weight and speed of the bow. People can find many ways to make the fiddle sound the way they want. Some people start when they are very young (and small) on instruments that are full size, so they choke up from the beginning (one of many reasons I’ve heard given for doing so).

In short there are a ton of ways to hold a bow and get a good sound. If you’re first learning, there is no reason to adopt a style that will make it harder in the long run, unless you simply can’t make it work any other way. I would definitely recommend holding the bow at the frog from the beginning because you just have more to work with. I’ve also heard two of the famous fiddlers mentioned make a point of telling students in workshops not to hold the bow the way they do. Use the full bow if you can, but if you can’t, it won’t stop you from going far.

Re: Bow hold, Cape Breton style?

What FiddleK said about holding the bow at the frog.

There’s no real advantage to choking up on the bow, and a lot of disadvantages. Fortunately for hackers like me, the vast majority of Irish dance tunes are simple enough that you can execute them even if you have a number of suboptimal habits, when it comes to violin technique. Even poor mechanics can be sufficient to execute this music with, and execute it well.

But there are lots of styles of music out there, and a lot of pieces, where you’re going to need every technical edge you can get, and that you’ll have trouble getting if you are holding the bow well up from the frog, or collapsing your left wrist, etc., etc., etc.

Some people excel *in spite of* these technical handicaps. Nobody excels because of them. I would also caution that poor mechanics make one prone to injuries as well - though probably not from choking up on the bow.

People have been thinking and writing about violin technique… the *mechanics* of playing the violin… for centuries, and dealing with much more technically demanding pieces than we’re used to playing in sessions over a few pints. There’s a lot of wisdom and experience out there. I always recommend paying close attention to what they have to say.
