The secret cult of paid hosts.

I’ve reviewed some older threads about paid sessions and I know the idea of money changing hands for a session is a sensitive topic for many. I don’t really want to stir up that beehive again - I’m mainly interested in feedback from people who *do* get paid on the topic of secrecy. Do the other players KNOW you’re getting paid? If they did know, do you think they would be peeved?

There seems to be a tradition of allowing all the guests at a session to believe it’s just dumb luck and enthusiasm that brings you all together every week, from exactly nine PM until exactly one in the morning. It took me years to clue in that the couple players that were always fed, beered up and ready to play right from the start to the end every week, come hell or high water, were getting a bit of cash for their trouble.

What’s with the secrecy? What are we… masons? Just this weekend I filled in for one the usual hosts of a session and I felt I had to be kind of sneaky when a fiddler from out of town started asking me probing questions about my bar tab. Now, I’m not secretive or sneaky by nature, so why did I feel like I had to hide from this woman the fact I was getting a couple of beers and a little bit of cash to commit to the entire duration of the session and keep it together?

Maybe we’re feeding the controversy by not being open about who is getting paid, and how much, and why - allowing anyone who is inclined to believe they are having a totally egalitarian experience and everybody is going home with pockets equally lightened. I’m not saying we should all stand up and make a speech, but stop making a conscious effort to hide it. That is, if you *do* make a conscious effort to hide it. Maybe you don’t. I usually don’t, but on this particular occasion I sensed a very unfavorable attitude a-bubbling under the surface of her questions and was evasive just to avoid an angry debate.

Re: The secret cult of paid hosts.

Firstly, I think some form of payment either in cash or free drink is very important. It helps keep up morale and encourages musicians to continue coming to a venue. There are some sessions I know where the landlord/publican provides the venue and nothing more. In theory, we are welcome but we don’t always feel that way, especially if the barman is having an off night--in one particular place, every night is an off night!! His bark might be worse than his bite but we might be more inclined to believe it, if we were getting something back from the pub.
As to where the money goes, that’s another argument. Some sessions split it evenly in cash and/or drink among those present. However, I wouldn’t disagree that it be paid to a host(s) who should distribute it(or not)as he, she , them sees fit. I certainly wouldn’t worry about not getting a pint if I’d only played a couple of tunes all night. On the other hand, if I or others have been playing continuously while the host(s) sat on his, her, their ar-e(s) all night and did nothing, then, maybe, it’s a bit unfair if the fee/tab isn’t shared. Of course, the host(s) are also getting paid for keeping the session going or making sure that one happens and possibly getting paid for what they *might* have to do. This is something which happens in “real jobs” so there is a good argument for it in sessions too.

Re: The secret cult of paid hosts.

Good topic Kerri. This was a sore point for me back in the late 80s when the publican at our local approached our group to secretly “anchor” the session on Sunday nights. I didn’t like the idea, but my cohorts weren’t ashamed. Up until that point the session, (that began in the mid-80s with Kevin Keegan) had been “volunteer” and continued after Kevin’s death. But by 1988 the “volunteer session” had fizzled, and one had started up down the street. In an effort to rescue the session, the publican came up with this covert session idea. After a while it seemed to work because that session became monstrous, and even though musicians and punters have scattered to the wind -- it still persists to this day.

My problem was that I refused to be secretive about the compensation to our band. For a while it was a big controversy that made some musicians stay away for political reasons, and others, who had been part of the original scene, resented us for becoming the hosts because we didn’t have the proper lineage and were considered undeserving in the role as "hosts.” I have to agree because I had only recently arrived on the scene and there were a lot of better players than me who were over-looked for some reason. I didn’t realize it at the time, but after reflecting back it seems like I was thrust into host position rather than inheriting it the way it might normally occur.

But that was the crux of the problem; it was that dodgy gray area between a paid session and a volunteer one. I have to give credit to the “paid session” for the consistency that results. If you have a volunteer session they tend to fizzle after a while, whereas a paid session tends to last as long as the publican supports it and encourages it. A little cash and some free drinks will inspire strong players to be hosts -- and that will guarantee a decent session even if no one shows up. For a volunteer sessions to fizzle all you need is one or two times where someone bothers to comes out for a tune only to find that no one else is there that night. Or -- that only the novices show up and the music is desperate and the customers start leaving. (Please don’t attack me for this last bit… it really happens. Having been one myself, I have nothing against novice musicians.)

Wicked thread idea. I’m not sure what I think about this one. My 1st reaction is to say “I don’t mind if people are being paid if it means that the session keeps going”.

But musos getting paid can cause problems. What about situations where you have a fairly average musician who gets paid to turn up for every session, and an experienced musician who doesn’t get paid and doesn’t turn up regularly. This doesn’t necessarily have to be a problem, but if the experienced muso is unaware that some people are getting paid, or finds that people are relying on them to start tune sets on the occasions when they turn up, that’s when the difficulties arise.

I much prefer the idea of a session that people enjoy so much that they want to turn up regularly anyway, whether they are rewarded or not. So from that I suppose I’d have to conclude that musos should be going to a session to be rewarded with the music, not money. Hmmm, I’m not even sure whether I agree with myself. I haven’t spent much time thinking it through to be honest - just a few first thoughts. And if I’m honest, if I went to a session and I was offered payment for it, it’s not like I’m going to refuse it and say “no thanks mate I don’t want your stinking money, I came here for the music and the craic”. It’s just business in the end. I figure that if the publican is making some money off me and my friends--yes, he’s gracious enough to give me a room to play on a set night, but let’s be realists, shall we?--then the least he/she can do is to provide a bit of incentive in the form of free drinks and food. I don’t get paid, but I consider myself a strong enough player who can provide a base for some of the weaker players to coalesce around, and for the stronger players to groove with. Not being paid, I have the freedom to not show up if I so choose. I first was a bit surprised that some players would get paid, but they have the responsibility of being there, even if it’s a slow night or there’s a couple of hack players making things not quite so much fun. It’s also part of their “job” to put up with the hacks and to encourage the newbies. If things aren’t happening, well, I can either go home or go sit at the bar until some better players show up. The paid session leaders don’t have those freedoms. Think about that.
