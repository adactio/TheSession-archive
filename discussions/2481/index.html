The emotional power of music

Last night I was in the pub till 3am (as usual) and there were about 4 people there apart from the landlord and maid behind the bar. The landlord sang this song which he had composed himself and which was so deeply sad that 2 of us (including me) had to get up and go outside halfway through so we could distract ourselves by talking about the weather or something. The song wasn’t even in English, but I had had the words explained to me the previous day. A few hours later, he sang it again, and I was completely overwhelmed and had to escape outside again almost immediately. I was a complete mess for the rest of the evening, even though I hadn’t had that much to drink at all. I am just about able to read the words written down on paper without being reduced to a jibbering wreck, but I found that the combination of the words and the tune was too much. It made me think about songs and the power of language which is lacking in “tunes only”, and that I should maybe try and learn to sing one of these days…

Re: The emotional power of music

Last year I was the trad-music consultant for a piece of orchestral music to be based on Irish tunes commissioned from a noted American composer. Eventually, our little band was incorporated into the second movement in a section that segued orchestra-band-orchestra. The first movement, though, is all based on slow airs which I supplied to the composer on CD. He used several, including Port na mPucai and Blind Mary (I know, the latter is a harp tune).

At the premiere, the composer stood up and mentioned that he’d been working on the slow movement when the jets went into the World Trade Center--specifically, on the setting of Blind Mary.

When he said that, I got a very clear image from the footage I saw on 9.11, of firefighters, a lot of them Italian and Irish Catholics and African-American Baptists, running full-tilt *into* those towers as they were about to come down, and was reminded of a story I’d heard from an escapee.

This office worker was stumbling down the stairs, fortunately for her with a bottle of water in hand, when she met some of these guys charging up past her. They asked about people upstairs, and then she said, “do you guys need some water”, and one of them said “we don’t need no f*ckin’ water” and then they were gone up into the smoke. They never came down. That image made it difficult to play the balance of the piece.

Now, one of us who played the premiere will trot out Blind Mary at least once each September. There’s usually a silence afterwards.
