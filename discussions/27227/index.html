Re: What is the fastest and hardest fiddle tune?

My guess is that he wants a tune that, when played, will impress people. Either that, or he’s looking for a benchmark to work towards… But both of these show a fairly fundamental misunderstanding of the nature of Irish trad.

He’s probably a former classical student (Suzuki?) , who became accustomed to the idea that a tune was something written indelibly on a page and not the manifestation of an individual musician’s style. In classical circles, it would be quite common and reasonable to say that “tune X is harder than tune Y”, and you progress through the repertoire from “Twinkle Twinkle Little Star” until you’re playing Paganini and Ysaye (or whatever you might consider the peak of difficulty).

Of course, in Irish trad, there’s really no such thing. Sean Maguire can play the Mason’s Apron in a way that was technically difficult, and Micho Russell could do in in a way that was technically easy. Is it a “hard tune”?

Tunes are as hard as you make them for yourself, and (frankly) nobody cares how hard it is if it doesn’t sound right. There’s nothing respectable about making a tune so difficult that you can’t play it right.

You want something to work towards? Try to learn how to play the Tarbolton set like Michael Coleman did (pay special attention to rhythm, bowing, variations, and ornamentation). That’ll keep you busy for months.

Re: What is the fastest and hardest fiddle tune?

The Hangman’s (Hanged Man’s) Reel is usually played particularly fast and hard.

The story about it is that a fiddler was sentenced to the gallows but told he would be let off if he could play the executioners a tune on his fiddle. These, though, had had it re-tuned in some way, so as to make it difficult or impossible for him to play it properly and save his neck.

However he sussed the new tuning and instantly worked out and played a new composition on it, thereby escaping the noose.

Even allowing for the man’s commendable ingenuity (if there is any truth in the story at all), The Hangman’s Reel is one of the most awful tunes that has ever scourged the sensibilities of man. It has about three notes, so it has to be made to work by arranging and re-arranging these as fast and as loud as possible. It sounds like foxes mating in one’s ear. It should be left to buskers operating dancing dolls and dressed up like exploded taxidermists’ shops, in public places where grockles are many and which sensitives can flee.

With this reasoning and these qualifications, then, I nominate The Hangman’s Reel as the epitome of what you may be looking for.

But you might be better off looking for something else - really!

Hmmmm…..“hard” has nothing to do with shifting up the neck. (A fair number of us on here have classical training you know - as did many Irish greats, including Michael Coleman).

Hard is finding the soul to play the music in a way that honors the tradition AND discovers something new (without resorting to bringing in gimicks from other traditions - well unless you’re Tommy Potts LOL)

Can you play something at a blistering speed but make it SOUND like it’s a lazy Sunday stroll? THAT, my friend, is the point. The music can be deceiving that way. So many times I’ve sat down to learn what I thought sounded like a fairly simple tune from a recording, only to discover that not only is the person playing at light speed, but there are so many tiny twists and turns in there it will take a while to absorb it all to be able to play it.

The tunes I listed before (Lord Gordons, Col Frasier, Bonnie Kate, etc) are ALL that way if you listen to recordings of good players. Every time through, there are differences and twists and turns and little jokes strewn around - can you hear all that yet? It’s taken me 3 years of constant listening plus lessons with one of the Irish music greats (Kevin Burke) for me to even start to hear everything that’s in there. It’s taken me knowing who THOSE people were listening to, to get the references back to other great recordings and little variations they do in tribute to player X or Y.

And this is all at a speed that leaves logical thought behind - but it sounds like they are sitting down to read the Sunday paper.

If you can combine fast playing with sounding “comfortable” AND loads of creativity also, then you’ll be a great player.

good luck - and try not to fall prey to the “flashy” tunes - they do get boring rather quickly….try to find a meaty tune that will lend itself to endless variations - that’s how you avoid “boredom”

If it was a trivial matter for a classical violinist to really learn to play Irish music, you would see it happen much more often than it does. If you REALLY want to learn to play Irish trad, you’ll have to spend a fair bit (probably several years’ worth) of energy understanding the different aesthetic.

Yes, much of the technique you learned in your classical lessons will be useful… But if you’re going to use it correctly, you will need to understand what pieces of it to use, and where to use them (and where not to!). While your tone and intonation might be well above average for a trad musician your age, most likely your sense of traditional rhythm, repertoire, ornamentation, bowing, ability to improvise and recognize variations, phrasing, and understanding of social norms are all quite wanting. In short, if you actually want to learn this stuff, you’ve got a long road ahead before your skills and senses balance out properly. Re: What is the fastest and hardest fiddle tune?

I’m not of the opinion that the relative difficulties of playing fast or slow is at all relevant. You should be getting to a stage where difficulty doesn’t matter any more. In exactly the same way that it’s irrelevant whether playing the fiddle is technically harder than playing the whistle … they are both the same when at their best.

But the one to really keep a look out for is that while it’s a lot of fun to play fast, this is often missing when playing slower. I often hear players who have a right old blast when cranking up the speed, but get really boring and bored when playing slower. You should be putting in all the same amount of energy and life and humour etc into your slower tunes. This should be easier, of course, because you’ve got more space to play with, but for some reason, people often miss out on this.
