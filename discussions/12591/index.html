Question about ornaments

When I look at some tunes in the database, I find that there are roll ornaments written above certain notes (see https://thesession.org/tunes/800 for an example). In some cases there are roll markers in places where it seems awkward to play a roll, at least on a fiddle. So I have a theory that these roll markers perhaps should be interpreted more broadly as “do something interesting here”, not strictly as “play a roll here”. For example, where a quarter note has a roll marker, I find that it often sounds good if I play a triplet rather than a roll. I am curious if this is valid and if anyone else uses the roll markers in the looser sense I describe here.

Re: Question about ornaments

Yes, I think it is valid, because some ornaments are more suited to one type of instrument than another. Ornaments aren’t compulsory, because if you use the same ornament in the same place every time you play a tune then, for you at least, it will become part of the tune, which it shouldn’t be. Ornaments, to me, are an excuse to do a little bit of extemporisation on the hoof, and to decorate the tune the second or third time through.
What I have said above is an important reason why I don’t like to see ornaments (or chords for matter) in the ABC of a tune as posted on TheSession. Another equally important reason is that the presence of ornaments in the ABC messes up a computer search for an ABC fragment to identify a tune. There’s no reason of course why ornamented and chorded versions of tunes can’t be put in the Comments section.

Re: Question about ornaments

It’s probably best to think of the twiddly marks not as rolls always, but rather as generic ornament marks meaning roughly: “I suggest you do something with this note”, as you surmise. That something will depend on the note, the melodic context, the instrument you play, your personal style, the phase of the moon, and what you ate for breakfast.

And while I agree that notated ornamentation should be kept to a minimum in generic settings such as are posted here, in some cases an ornament really is more or less an inherent feature of the basic tune. The tune you cite (Tom Steele / Hand Me Down the Tackle) is a good example. I’ve never heard that low D played plain. Fiddle might do a bowed treble, whistle or pipes a cran, plucked strings a picked or hammered triplet, box a mid cut, etc. Of course, notes of dotted quarter (dotted crotchet) duration or longer are usually implied targets for some sort of ornamentation anyway. But in this case, I think it’s reasonable to consider the ornament an essential part of the tune and worth notating. (And llig is right, that any note short or long can be ornamented effectively if you choose.) It’s also ironic here that that low D is one note that none of fiddle, whistle, flute, or pipes can actually roll.

Realize also that the conventional notation |dD~D2 FDFA| as in Tom Steele is actually deceptive. This might be written as |d~D3 FDFA|, to capture the generalization that the low D is really a single ornamented note.

Re: Question about ornaments

Crazy-fingerz, it really is that subjective. How many times have you listened to a recording and only recognised the tune when it has played for a good while already, because it’s a very personal interpretation? Good examples that come to mind are Oisin Mac Diarmada’s versions of the Trip to Durrow, and the Lark in the Morning, and Tommy Peoples‘ versions of the Lark, and the Monaghan Jig, on ’Waiting for a Call’.
If you listen to Peoples in particular, you’ll notice that what is left out of the tune defines his particular version more than what is put in. It exemplifies perfectly that a roll can only be played on certain notes if the notes that follow (on a skeletal interpretation) are omitted.
What is really crucial is to make the tune so familiar to your own ears, and fingers, that you don’t think about playing ‘that’ roll at ‘that’ place, every time, or only every second time. Learn the tune so that you can only play every second note, or every third note, and still play the tune. Then you can ornament wherever, and however you want, within the limits of your instrument and your ability.

Re: Question about ornaments

It helps a lot if the person submitting the tune cites their source and/or what instrument the transcription favors. I notate tunes differently for flute than for fiddle, for example.

Also, many (tho not all) wind players like to distinguish between a long roll (F~B3) and a short roll (FB~B2). But the tilde symbol (~) is typically used for rolls and crans, so again, it helps to mention this in the comments.

And my own personal preference is to think of all these things not as “ornaments” appended to the tune, but as “articulations,” ways of articulating the notes. They are integral to the tune itself, but also changeable, similar to how we all use inflection to clarify the meaning of the words we speak.

Consider how the meaning varies in the following:

The cat BIT the dog.
The CAT bit the dog.
The cat bit the DOG.
The cat bit the dog?

For even more variation, change up the melody line and rhythm a little:

The cat ate the whole hot dog, but it took a while.
The cat suckled on the chihuahua.
The mountain lion bit the dog.
The kitten nipped the pit bull on the nose….

A good trad musician plays with the tune in the same way, teasing out different senses of the tune by articulating the notes, phrasing, and timing differently each time through.

Re: Question about ornaments

Iris, in 30 years of playing this music on fiddle, I can’t say I’ve ever thought about rolls, trip[lets, etc. “easing the transition” to the next note or phrase. In fact, one of the most common problems I’ve noticed in fiddle students is that they struggle with the notes immediately following a roll or triplet.

That said, there are places where a bowed triplet, say, makes it easier for the left hand fingers to get on with the tune than if you played a roll in the same place (because a roll involves those fingers more). But the point is to learn how to do the roll in just those situations without it interfering with the subsequent notes. Enhancing those notes, even. So you can choose among the whole bag of articulations any good trad fiddler might use and so change things up each time around.

I guess it begs the question of easier than what? The alternatives to a roll or triplet or cut are to hold a single note, catch a drone or double stop, or play some melodic phrase in the alloted space. For less experienced fiddlers, these options are usually easier to do than the “ornaments.” And more experienced fiddlers won’t notice a difference--it’s all “easy.”

Re: Question about ornaments

Rolls, triplets, cuts, and the rest *do* sometimes make things easier--just not (in my experience) transitions to the next notes. They fill time in a rhythmically interesting way, so it’s “easier” in this respect to play the opening to the B part of the Dmix Toss the Feathers with rolls or triplets on those repeated “d” notes:

A~d3 A~d3|A~d3 ed^cd|
or
Ad d/d/d Ad d/d/d|Ad d/d/d ed^cd|
rather than just bowing dotted quarters there (the rather memorably horrid way Boston Pops once played this tune)
Ad3 Ad3|Ad3 edd2|

So yes, if you have rolls or triplets at your command, they make it easier to enliven the music and think in whole chunks of notes instead of individual eighth notes.

Also, I sometimes alternate between rolls or triplets simply to give one hand or the other a rest. If my left hand is tired in the wee hours of a long session, I might use more triplets and other bowing tricks for a spell to let my left hand recover. Similarly, if I’m feeling any fatigue in the bow hand, I might lay off the triplets a while and use more rolls, cuts, and left hand articulations.

As for “easier and harder places to ornament,” I guess I don’t think that way about it, at least not on fiddle. At some stage, technical constraints no longer enter into decisions about where to do a roll or whatever. Those choices are based on what strikes me as a way to move the melody forward, to add pulse or lift. It usually comes down to bringing a note or phrase to the fore, giving it significance or freshness, or emphasizing the descending or ascending direction of a melodic phrase.

Re: Question about ornaments

If the tradition had no standards (“rules”) at all, then it would cease to be its own recognizable genre.

Trad music is pulled between two turnbuckles. One pulls toward the status quo: do it this way because it’s always been done this way. The other pulls toward change: don’t be afraid to try new ideas; otherwise the tradition becomes irrelevant to the changing times and dies.

Each player finds his/her own way to balance these tensions. And the trad community overall weighs in, favoring some changes and not others, honoring some diehard habits and not others.

For example, Seamus Connolly and Brian Conway argue that the old way of doing rolls--where you can hear the pitch of each grace note--is being lost to the more percussive style popularized by Kevin Burke and others. Time will tell which wins out. Perosnally, I enjoy knowing and using both approaches, depending on my mood, who I’m playing with, and what the tune itself suggests.

Re: Question about ornaments

Kennedy, what I meant for open string rolls was starting on the open D string and playing E-G-E and back to the open string, all on the same string. The fingering is 0 (open string) - 1 - 3 - 1 - 0, and you can use it on any open string. The fingering for the alternative open string roll (which I said I don’t like so much) would be 0 - 1 - 2 - 1 - 0.
The fingering for the roll on the D on the G string would be 2 - 3 (or 4) - 2 - 1 - 2, which is the standard fingering you’d use for a roll anywhere other than on an open string.
BTW, the open string roll I’ve talked about above is described by Paul McNevin in his book “The Irish Fiddle” as a variation of the standard roll played with two auxiliary notes above the main note because “a [standard] roll on an open string is not practical because you would need to cross strings to play the lower note of the roll”. He goes on to say that because the open string roll is technically difficult to master, many fiddlers would use other ornamentation such as a triplet, melodic variation etc.

Re: Question about ornaments

On standard-tuned diatonic harps, including tremolos, you can do what passes for a roll with all except critically-listening tradition police by playing five notes in the same air-direction very fast (well, just fast enough). I’m thinking for example of the start of the B part of Over The Moor To Maggie, where there are three opportunities in quick succession. They go BDBGB (blow), ACAF#A (draw), DGDBD (blow). All in the high register of a G harp, that first B being hole 8 on a blues harp. I suppose the last note isn’t really part of the ornament. Now I know that the notes may not be the correct ones, but do ‘em fleet enough and not over-loud and it sounds good to me and it’s definitely better than leaving ’em out. Of course, you can do little cuts and proper triplets in several places in tunes, and if the cut-note isn’t the correct pitch, well, it still works as long as you’re not too lugubrious in your execution. Brendan Power learned the “jaw-flick” technique from the Murphys of Wexford and it’s a more reliable way of getting a cut (or the move in any other ornament where you need to move to an adjacent hole and back quickly in the same air-direction). You can tongue a same-note triplet, banjo-style, but it’s best to avoid tonguing “diddley,” which is very tempting but too corny (we probably all do it though). And, of course, you can bend as an ornament as opposed to bending to get a missing note, the beauty of it being that the exact pitch you bend down to isn’t as critical as it would be if you were going for a missing note, and you’re allowed to slur. The high blow-bends are very useful especially. All must be used judiciously and the ornament tail must not wag the tune dog. On paper it might all sound a bit contrived to the purists, but listen to someone who can do it and your doubts will be dispelled. Smile emoticon.
