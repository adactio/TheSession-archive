Hiding behind the tradition

Disclaimer notice: I like old stlye trad.

Does anyone here find that some players of limited ability criticise modern virtuoso styles of playing (John Mc Sherry, Mike Mc Golderick, Brian Finnegan etc) because they know they could never attain the dizzying heights of modern technique?

I’ve met a few people like this who go for the pure drop stuff and insist on avoiding the modern stuff, citing different idealogical preambles as their reasons. I realise that some of these people genuinely do want to play in ‘old styles’ on their given instruments, but to me a lot of them are taking the easy way out.

Thedon, sir. Once musicians put their heads above parapets they are open to criticism. Hopefully constructive. The appreciation of music can happen on many levels. If you’re steeped in ITM as both a listener and a player you will come to appreciate the music on very many levels after a good number of years. But it matters not whether your appreciation is arrested at the foot-tapping stage or whether it progresses (if that’s the right word) to the very core of the tradition, the latter requiring not just listening and playing but also a considerable degree of scholarship. I regard myself as being about half-way down that road. Doubt whether I’ll go any further. But wherever you are on the scale you are entitled to express a view, and the view that some of the younger bands that can play with great technical agility and speed are missing out on the soul of the music is perfectly valid. You could of course challenge that standpoint by requiring a definition of what that “soul” really means. But if we knew that we’d also be able to prove that God exists and that cold fusion could solve all the problems of the planet. No-one is saying that “they don’t like such-and-such’s playing so they’re crap.” And no-one around here is a “special person who gets it” any more than anyone else, or at least if they claimed so to be they’d be laughed off the board. What is so wrong with saying “I think Lunasa are technically brilliant but I detect big egos at work and I don’t think they have yet connected with the true spirit, whatever that means, of ITM?” That happens to be my opinion (and I’m not jealous of them because they don’t use harmonicas anyway) but it is just an opinion. It may grate because it may dent someone’s Lunasa-worship when they read it (and I’m not being patronising - I worship the Bothy Band and quite a few posts have taken pot-shots at them recently!). You have to let us talk about it without saying that a view that varies from yours is silly. You’re beginning to sound just like my dad.

Re: Hiding behind the tradition

The Hayes paraphrase comes from an interview he did with Fiddle Magazine. It was once posted online, but it may be gone. He was talking about musicians, no one in particular.

I wasn’t using it to “add authority” to anything. I just thought it was an interesting idea, relevant to this thread. And my experience tends to gibe with the gist of it--I tend to get greater enjoyment listening to music that carries me into the music itself, rather than into a conscious appreciation for the musician’s skill.

I like thedon’s post above. Music is expressive. Sometimes it “expresses” virtuosity and inventiveness and precision. Sometimes it expresses simplicity and transparency. The same tune can conjure up a sense of a player’s lifelong relationship with the music or his/her relative newness to it, maturity or nascence. Although we may enjoy one over the other, there’s no reason to go disparaging someone for expressing themselves. If you don’t like it, don’t listen.

The same player can sound soulful one night and mechanical the next, heart rending to one listener and trite to the next. Making music is an ephemeral thing, and so are our responses to it. The same tune is never the same tune twice.

the view that anyone preferring tasteful simplicity in art is sour grapes or “hiding,” is comically provincial beyond belief. the issue is not one of sour grapes or of slamming players. the issue is one of aesthetics, which are debated in all art forms ever invented. and news flash, the simplicity-versus-flash debate is one of the big aesthetic issues in all art forms, from jazz to classical to japanese calligraphy. you can differ---but to call the simplicity-as-elegance school sour grapes or “hiding” is just ignorant.

in jazz, thelonius monk and miles davis were two of the more famous proponents of simplicity over flash. it may have been monk who made the famous observation that the soul of the music was not in the notes…..but in the silences between the notes. and once you’ve heard monk, the pyros just seem…..tacky and kitschy.

i recently read a great line by the anglo concertina player chris sherbourne of the uk band “last night’s fun” to the effect that he spent his first 10 years of concertina playing trying to get as many notes and effects in there as he could….but that his most recent few years have been spent in the more challenging endeavor of trying to get rid of as many notes as he could….not coincidentally, i really like his playing!

tony macmahon has written brilliantly and very fairly on this issue in some paper that is on his web site. like me, he has nothing against experimentation and technical wizardry. like me, his issue is with the lemmings out there who take a stylistic development and treat it as the last word or some kind of a pinnacle.

so to the degree that you hear some hostility in those of us who like elegance and taste in our art, it is not at the lunasas or the post-noel-hill-postmodernists. it is at the ticky-tacky kitsch-lovin’ lemmings who treat that stuff like the last word………
