Re: Tips on learning by ear?

All of the above is valid, especially the point about being able to whistle a tune without any training, but I have a suggestion for you. The trick to playing by ear is to understand keys and intervals:

1. Keys: In Irish music, this is fairly easy, because in most cases your F is going to be sharp (key of G or E minor) or F and C are going to be sharp (key of D or B minor). If you are in G, the tune likely ends or begins with G. If in E minor, it likely begins or ends on E. If in D, it begins or ends on D. If in B minor, it begins or ends on B. This is useful but not crucial to know. It just helps knowing the basic structure of the tune, which you will learn by trial and error. Once you have a feel for the key you are in, you reduce the number of mistakes you can make by playing a note that is not in the key.

2. Intervals: This is crucial. By practice, you get to know approximately how far apart notes are within a phrase of music. You should be able to tell if the second note of a tune is one note away, or two, or more from the first note. At first, you’ll likely go to within one note of the one you actually wanted. But in time, you’ll get more and more accurate, and with even more time, you will discover that certain intervals can be anticipated, so you’ll go where you should without even thinking about it. With time and confidence, your ear will simply lead you to each note of a tune, but it is a learned skill, easier for some than for others. As previously suggested, practice nursery rhymes or anything simple that you know really well. I think you’ll find that the intervals are less mysterious than they seem, and the patterns of how they work will reveal themselves so that with each new tune, where you go next will become second nature.

Re: Tips on learning by ear?

Irish tunes are pretty regular in structure. Typically there are two 8-bar sections which we call the A and B parts. Generally, the A part is played and repeated then the B part is played and repeated. Eight bars is quite a lot to get your head round in one go, but, luckily for us, the A and B parts of eight bars are usually each divisible into two distinct four-bar phrases. A four-bar phrase isn’t too hard to learn at one go if you listen to it a few times. Get the first half, four bars, of the A part under your belt then do the same for the second four bars. Keep playing them until you get it. Then move on to the B part and do the same. Then string the whole thing together. If you’re like me the whole bloody thing will go out of your head very quickly, but that’s only because you haven’t yet played it enough. Nil desperandum. It’s there really, and now all you have to do is play it a few times, playing along with your source, preferably. The beauty is that, once you’ve learned a handful of tunes by ear, you begin to detect little patterns that crop up in tunes again and again. That makes the learning process so much easier for future tunes. You won’t get any of that if you try to learn from notation or ABCs or tablature. The way to learn tunes is to listen to what it is you’re trying to learn and to listen to what’s coming out of your instrument. Stop worrying about which key/string/reed/fret/button/hole you’re playing. Music is about the sounds you’re making, not where you put your fingers or gob!

Re: Tips on learning by ear?

Lots of useful thoughts here. Let me add:

1) Be sure you are comfortable with the instrument you are trying to play the tune on. I play several and can do much better with the instrument where my mind has already figured out “where the next note is.”

2) As already said, listen for phrases. most in ITM are 2 or 4 bar, and as already pointed out one 4 bar phrase and the next may share the first 2 bars and have differing second 2 bar parts. It really isn’t important though that you know how many bars you are listing for, just learn to hear the phrases and the number of bars will take care of itself.

3) Listen for patterns. I’ve a friend who teaches and constantly uses various patterns for which she has names. For example the “Frere Jacque” pattern is GABG , Inverted FJ is BAGB etc. One that shows up all the time in ITM is gedBG or gedBA. Realize these patterns can move around depending on starting note. ABcA is still FJ, though the intervals in it are different. I’ve a friend who is a fine oldtimey player who says that when he was learning his teacher would not teach him tunes, but rather the “tags” that are used at the end of sections or tunes. When he went to a jam after about 6 weeks he found he could play most of the tunes because he’d learned all the patterns from learning the tags.

4) No one has suggested lilting the tune (that is singing it with nonsense syllables). Eventually you may even be able to lilt the “twiddly bits,” and not just the skeleton of the tune.

5) If you are going to try this in a session learn to play your instrument softly enough and sit far enough out from the center of the group so you do not interfere with the enjoyment of others.

Re: Tips on learning by ear?

Since I was the one to bring up learning intervals, let me put it in a less technical way. Learning intervals is just gaining the knack to be able to hear how much higher or lower each note in the tune is. There’s no musical theory involved nor technical knowledge. The value in learning intervals is to understand that finding where you go next in a tune in not guesswork, but it is a bit different than lilting, whistling or singing. To varying degrees, those of us who play by ear find the intervals with no conscious thought - which is the goal. But if it doesn’t come naturally or easily, it is good to understand that breaking tunes down into phrases and even individual pairs of notes to find how far down or up in the scale you need to go develops the capacity to anticipate the right interval quickly and automatically.

@ Mollie Doherty -- There are two things happening more or less in parallel when learning a tune, IMO. You can make a comparison to hearing a joke for he first time, except that it is rather more easy to grasp the ‘whole concept’ of a joke. One is to internalise the thing as a whole -- the story, if you like, the overall pattern, emotional content, style -- is it laid back, driving, light-hearted, sad, etc etc. The contours of the tune come under this category -- the phrasing, the hooks, the twists, landmarks, the things that make the tune instantly recognisable rather than just a collection of notes (and there are ‘tunes’ that can be classed as such, usually recognisable by their forgettability). These things have to be negotiated when learning the tune as opposed to learning to play it, and are the equivalent of ‘getting’ the joke to the extent that you can tell it to someone else even they don’t think it’s funny.
The second aspect is learning to play the tune -- that is working out where your fingers go, bowing, bellows or picking or breathing for whatever instrument you are playing. The mechanics of it, and the technique you want to employ or replicate. In order to work out what is going on, you have to pay close attention, really listen, and not make assumptions, because your ear can deceive you. It helps, of course, to have someone show you what is going on, because you might not be able to produce the same sound by doing the same thing, and at least knowing what to do gives you something to work with until you get the hang of it. These last things are equivalent to rehearsing the joke, making it word-perfect, and getting the timing right so that you’re more or less guaranteed to get a laugh.
As has already been said, the more you do it the easier it gets so don’t give up because it is ‘too difficult’. Just simplify the source, or try tunes you already know. Before long (months rather than days!) you will be wondering what the problem was.

I think some of you are still confused about intervals, at least in terms of my original suggestion. I did not intend the term in a technical way, as in fifths and thirds and suchlike. As another poster clarified, it’s simply a matter of recognizing how far up or down the scale your next note is. It is an aural rather than technical process, but it can be practiced separately from learning a specific tune. When you learn a tune, you can simply memorize it phrase by phrase, but that is not truly playing by ear. When you train your ear to hear the intervals (not name them, mind), you can then play any tune you have in your head on the first try. Make no mistake: That is a skill and one that can be practiced by simple repetition. Take any tune you know and try to anticipate the intervals. At first you will be off by a note one way or the other, but in time you will have a feel for how far you need to go to get the next correct note.

There’s nothing wrong with knowledge or musical theory but “playing by ear” is simply just a matter of replicating on your instrument what you’ve actually heard.
So, it’s a case of finding out where the note is on your instrument, play it for the same length of time as how you heard it, and if you are playing several notes together .. i.e. a tune, it’s also a case of playing them in the correct order and also to leave any spaces in the appropriate place too.
As others have said, it’s best to start with a very easy tune that you already know in your head..even something like Happy Birthday and just progress from there.

It’s not rocket science and most of us can do this instinctively without having to worry about the other technicalities. It’s also possible to work out your own musical theory or system as you go along which may or may not be similar to that which is officially recognised. Re: Tips on learning by ear?

My 3 most important principles for learning anything by ear…

Know the instrument
Know the genre/style
Know the tune/song

It’s terribly difficult to learn anything by ear if you don’t know how to play the instrument. It’s the one most important thing in my opinion.

Genres and Styles of music have patterns and structures specific especially to that genre. Knowing what the style of music sounds like will be the roots of expectation and anticipation, and makes remembering tunes and songs easier.

Knowing a tune/piece, inside and out, note for note, phrase for phrase, instrumentation, rhythm, melodic contour, harmonic patterns, etc makes it extremely easy to learn it. You know what’s there, and where the music is going.

Another tip, don’t let the music intimidate you. Our brains do most of the work when learning music by ear. From the 100’s of neurons that connect when we figure out a phrase, to the millions of neurons that connect for muscle memory. Trying and practicing is all you have to do.

With this music specifically, if you’re into just learning notes, this is the easiest music to learn. You’re only focused on melodies. Note by note. No dense, noisy harmonies in your way. No crazy drums, horns, and choirs in the background distracting you from the melody. Just the melody.

Pick up your instrument, turn up your speakers and give it a try. It takes time but it gets easier. This is purely a trial and error practice, so don’t let mistakes keep you from trying. It’s how you learn. Keep it simple. If something is too hard, work on something more simple, and come back to the hard stuff later. It’s great for assessing your progress. For a long time, it was the only way I knew I was getting better.

I could go on and on about this, so i’ll stop hear… Now…

Learning the *notes* is easy. As for *playing* this music the way it’s meant to be played, sorry, I can’t help you much there.

Sources: 15 years of experience learning by ear privately, publicly, impromptu, and alongside sheet music :P :)
