POXY MELODY PLAYERS

HA! Made you look!

Actually I meant “players of poxy melodies”, not “melody players who are all poxy”. I’ve been having a look through some archival discussion threads about “most hated tunes”, and found there to be no solid conclusion as to why some tunes are regarded as being nicer than others. After some thought, I have come up with a theory. I’m not going to mention any of the names of my most hated tunes, because that’s been done to death in previous threads, but as a back-up player I have noticed that the tunes I dislike do not suggest interesting harmonies in their melody lines. That is, there is little or no flexibility for a chordal accompaniment implicit in the tune.

The strange thing is that the tunes I dislike are also ones that are cited by non-rhythm players as “most hated tunes”. I always assumed that non-rhythm players are not concerned with chordal harmonies as long as they aren’t too obtrusive, but I’ve come to realise that flautists or fiddlers or whatever in a session *must* have some idea of the exact harmony they expect to hear while they are playing, because if I do something unusual that works when I’m backing, it often gets acknowledged with raised eyebrows or smiles even by people who’ve never touched a guitar or bouzouki in their lives. On the other hand, if I make a hash of something or do something weird that doesn’t work, I get frowns, (or a different kind of raised eybrow!). It makes me wonder to what extent lead players have the harmonic pattern of a tune in their heads already, even if they don’t know much about chords.

If this is so, then it would explain better why some tunes are regarded as crap. It would also explain why the tune might not have been regarded as crap or too simplistic historically, since chordal accompaniment using guitars and bouzoukis is a relatively recent thing. Are modern trad players more demanding of their tunes because they are accustomed to hearing the harmonies played by their favourite bands?

Tunes that are a bit show-offy perhaps fall into this category, because the melody players are necessarily too busy trying to cope with technical difficulties to be able to give much expression or provide much variation, and the backers find the chord progression spelled out by the tune as being too insistent and inflexible to do anything interesting.
Maybe it comes down to our individual limitations or gifts as to which tunes we filel into that category -- naff, or insipid or whatever.

In response to Bridie’s and Cuchulain’s points, I’d point out that if you played 3 hours of nothing but spine-tingling “high quality” tunes, they’d lose some of their luster. What really makes a hair-raising tune stand out is dropping into it from a more plain tune. I really value some of the simpler, less emotive, less surprising tunes precisely for those qualities. It’s the breadth and depth--the *variety*--of the trad repertoire that grabs me by the heartstrings, not just Toss the Feathers and College Groves. Frankly, if *every* tune is melodramatic and flashy, you’ve got a Leahy concert…not my cup of tea.

So I don’t think of tunes as being on a pecking order of quality, from low to high, because I don’t believe there’s any such measuring stick. Instead, I look for the unique *qualities* each tune brings to the set. It’s a bit like food--if every course of every meal was dessert, and I had to eat that week after week, pretty soon I’d be craving a poached egg, some unsweetened oatmeal, or a plain peanut butter sandwich.

Maybe that’s just me. But I’m quite happy to get my jollies from tunes like the Killavil Fancy and the Kesh Jig and whatever else, along with the adrenaline tunes, and consider them all to be “high quality” despite that they bring different things to a set or session.

Incidentally (and I’m not aiming this at you Bridie, or anyone else in particular. I just think this is worth mentioning here), I really like Barry Foy’s advice in his book, Field Guide to the Irish Music Session. In response to the beginner’s question, “Which tunes should I learn first?” Foy says:

Every one you can get your hands on. And not only reels, either, despite what’s been said about their constituting the bulk of a session--you never know when a mazurka or fling might spring out from some well-concealed hiding place. A serious Irish musician lives in a state of permanent low-level anxiety over how many tunes she doesn’t yet know. It’s common among beginning players of Irish music to resolve to learn only those tunes that really appeal to them. This makes them feel tasteful and discerning. But a little experience soon proves such a campaign to be a waste of time. Sure, there will always be favorite and less favored tunes. But at the end of the day it comes down to this: The more tunes you know, the more playing you’ll get to do. So learn ’em all, anywhere you can."

Foy adds, “If your repertoire is so small that your regular session mates can count on you to haul out…that very same old worn-out tune time after time and week after week, unvarying, as if you carried the poor thing around with you everywhere in a velvet-lined box--well then, you’ve got too few tunes, and you’d better get to work. Do the world a favor and learn some new ones.”

When I was starting out, I tried to high-grade the tunes and just learn the ones that really appealed to me. After several hundred tunes, I gave up, mostly because the more I learned, the more other tunes appealed to me.

Along these lines, one thing I’ve noticed is that some tunes might not sound like much, but they can be hilariously fun to play. Mulqueen’s on fiddle, for instance, or Whinny Hills of Leitrim on whistle, or Terry Teehan’s on flute. I imagine this is in part why guitar players seem to like Music for a Found Harmonium--that chord progression. So before you dismiss a tune outright, consider how it might lay on another instrument, and let other people haul it out at the session without rolling your eyes or huffing noisily over to the bar.

LaPrise, a Detroit native whose full name was Roland Lawrence LaPrise, concocted the song along with two fellow musicians in the late 1940s for
the apres ski crowd at a nightclub in Sun Valley, Idaho. The group, the Ram Trio, recorded the song in 1949.

In 1953, bandleader Ray Anthony bought the rights and recorded “The Hokey Pokey” on the B-side of another novelty record, “The Bunny Hop.”

“Everybody was doing the ‘Bunny Hop’ before long, which meant that everybody was doing ‘The Hokey Pokey,”’ observed LaPrise’s daughter,
Linda Ruby.

There followed a steady succession of recordings.

After the Ram Trio disbanded in the 1960s, LaPrise, by then a father of six, went to work for the post office in Ketchum. At about the same time,

That’s not to say that the Kesh is irrelevant, but if people were to stop playing it, it would at least survive in some places, and would be preserved on CDs or in books until someone revives it in 100 years time. There is a constant trickle of new tunes filtering into the tradition, and fortunately (unlike Irish Gaelic) there are enough people involved and attitudes are positive enough towards it that the tradition will live healthily at least for the forseeable future.

*However*, if for some reason the number of people playing trad music decreased, because of persecution or whatever (like what’s beginning to happen in England and Wales at the moment), or if there was general attitude of snobbishness towards all tunes, then that would be a tragedy. This is like minorities who are not able to achieve cultural self-regulation so their language and culture are usurped by those of a greater power. So I don’t think that it’s necessary to play tunes we don’t like solely “for the sake of maintaining the tradition” since the tradition doesn’t die just because a few particular tunes lose popularity amongst the players. It’s an ongoing thing, and that is how it has always worked. Some tunes live forever, some have a short lifespan, the new replaces (or exists alongside) the old. We’re all part of an exciting aspect of culture that has been allowed to morph naturally (not necessarily grow in any terms other than popularity, just change constantly). If the ongoing transmission were to be cut off, however, then that would be a worry because it would mean that the tradition would eventually die. I really think that a lot of people view their playing of what they call traditional tunes as a means of maintaining the tradition in itself (it’s like: if they play too many new tunes then they’re not being true to it) , but I don’t think it really works like that. I think it’s a much more fluid, ever-changing thing that works only because it *is* constantly changing. It just takes a while for attitudes to change. The bouzouki for example is a really recent addition to Irish trad; now it is difficult to imagine what it would be like without it. We wouldn’t have had Planxty, Altan, Dervish etc etc in the same form. These days people see the bouzouki as being a really traditional sound because of these bands. If trad had not been a constantly changing thing, then this would never have happened and our music would sound very different, but no more or less “traditional”. In saying all this, I don’t mean to disrespect the ancestral heritage thing and all the older tunes. I just think that what has been passed on to us from our ancestors is as much an “ability”, or “way of being”, or “framework of thought and cultural value” as it is a “body of tunes that comprises the ‘traditional repertoire’”.

I agree, Mark, that the tradition is more a way of being than a set body of tunes, and I don’t mean that someone would write exactly the same tune as Harvest Home if it didn’t already exist. But someone no doubt would sooner or later write a very similar tune for the reason you mention--because the traditional “way of being” would make such a tune fairly obvious (at least to a fiddle player).

Which maybe gets at this “naff” business. Maybe the so-called naff tunes are the ones that are more *obvious,* where, for example, the A part of Drowsy Maggie is a fairly straightforward example of a not-too-cryptic 4/4 string crossing pattern in an Em double stop on fiddle. Same for the B part to Morning Dew. Compared to Dispute at the Crossroads or something like Beare Island Reel, those parts of Drowsy Maggie and Morning Dew are more predictable within the traditional approach. Give an Irish fiddler enough time and Guiness, a muse in E minor, and before long you’d probably get something close to those parts.

Of course, *I* don’t think that makes them “naff,” but perhaps it helps explain why some of you do.

And I don’t disagree that new tunes are entering the tradition all the time. It’s just that so many of them also quickly *exit* the tradition. My theory is that such tunes don’t last because they either (1) don’t have an acceptably traditional structure (Music for a Found Harmonium is a nice novelty tune, but it probably won’t be played at sessions much in 2035), or (2) they sound too much like some other existing trad tune (consider the case of Maudabawn Chapel, with that Drowsy-Maggie-like B part…Maudabawn’s a great tune, but will it last as long as Maggie has? Time will tell.). For a composer within the tradition, that’s a fine, netless high-wire to walk, between “too innovative” and “already been done,” especially when you have a database of thousands of existing tunes in a limited number of accepted keys and time signatures, mostly developed as a product of the trad approach to playing a given instrument (so you have “fiddle tunes” and “pipe tunes,” etc.), which if you copy or even just echo too closely, people likely won’t play your “new” tune.

(Mark, when you learn to use paragraphs, please don’t use mine as a model ;-)

I think this is why we end up with “families” of tunes, where a handful of reels or jig or hornpipes share enough similarities to sound like cousins or even siblings. Sometimes this happens because it’s an aural tradition and someone picks up parts of a tune but not the whole thing and they have to fabricate the rest, ending up with a “new” tune. Other times, I’m guessing that a player begins noodling around on an existing tune and decides to take it a different direction…"Okay, I’ve played |G2 BG dGBG| enough times in a dozen different reels, what happens if I do it: |G2 BG cGBG|….and so on. You can see these forces at work in tunes like Mouse in the Mug and Roaring Barmaid, or Harvest Home and Fisherman’s Favorite, or Rambling Pitchfork and I Buried My Wife and Danced on Her Grave and Frieze Britches. I’ve used these ideas myself to try my hand at composing tunes in a trad style, and I’ve been told by other (successful) trad composers that this is not an uncommon approach.

If there’s any validity to any of this, then I’d suggest that it’s useful and instructive to keep playing tunes like the Kesh Jig (which, if you ignore how many times you’ve heard it and listen one more time with fresh ears, is actually a potent little jig). At the very least, it helps us avoid re-inventing the wheel.

Lastly (this post is turning out just like my nascent attempts at phrasing on the flute--always trying to fit in a few more notes as my breath dies away), I’ve said this before, but one of the finer qualities of this music is that the tunes are good enough to withstand repetition. We play them over and over, and we build a shared repertoire, in part because they’re damn good tunes, and in part because it makes it easier and more enjoyable for all of us to join in. I for one embrace that (historically recent) aspect of the tradition and so I enjoy playing “common” tunes, and doing so repeatedly. Doesn’t mean I don’t also enjoy the more obscure ones, but they’re only obscure till everyone else learns them…..
