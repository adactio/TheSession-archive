“Picking up”/“Jumping in”/Finishing someone else’s set

Picture this scenario: someone’s started a set of tunes, they’ve played their tunes, they’re clearly coming to the end of the set, they’ve given clear signals that this is the last go round, and as they conclude the last tune in the set, someone picks up where they’re leaving off and carries on with another tune (or tunes).

This is a thing that happens from time to time where I play, and I’ve noticed that it can either be a real lift to the session, or it can be a real drag, depending on how it’s done. I’m interested in hearing from other players - is this a thing that happens where you play? If so, what do you think about it? When it works, what makes it work? When it doesn’t work, what does that look like? Is it something you do? Something you wish other people wouldn’t do? Basically, I’m curious to know people’s thoughts about this particular aspect of session playing.

Of course, we should recognize at the outset that session etiquette varies broadly by session, and there aren’t a lot of universal rules, so this is really an open blather, not an attempt to establish some sort of Authoritative Position on the matter. Just interested to know what people think.

↳ Posted by ross faison on October 16th, 2026.

Re: “Picking up”/“Jumping in”/Finishing someone else’s set

In our session, when someone starts a set, the general guideline is that they own it until they either end it or pass it to someone else.

Passing it to someone else I define as:

Towards the end of a tune, when someone doesn’t have another tune they look around with a facial expression that says “I’d like this set to continue, but I don’t have something to play next”, and then generally there is some non-verbal exchange of looks or gestures with another player that says “I got this”.

That person now takes control of the set and owns it until they either end the set or pass it along themselves.

We established this as a guideline when there was specific objection voiced by one of our regulars about the practice of “set stealing” or "set appending’, where someone who owns a set clearly ends it, only to have someone tack on another tune or two.

I personally strongly prefer that this practice be avoided, and here’s why…

When I start a set, I’m building a story in my mind based on the emotional energy of the tunes in terms of keys and modes. I’ll start a set out moderately happy in a neutral key like G Major, then go a bit dark in E Dorian, maybe toss in an ambiguous D Mixolydian tune, then end with a happy tune in D Major. I’m writing a story.

If someone then tacks on another tune to the end, to me it feels like this:

"And they lived happily ever after!
.
.
.
.
.
Did I tell you about when I went to the store to get some milk and they were completely out?"

It feels like the story gets derailed and feels a bit disorienting.

Additionally, there are historically some tunes that often get played after other tunes. Perhaps they are common sets, or played one after another on a famous recording.

If someone ends a set on a tune, and then someone plays the “other tune that comes after than one”, it persists the inability to play the tunes standalone, and to me feels like “Oh, I really didn’t want to follow that tune with this one yet again, but here we are.”

So, I think it’s important that we all be considerate of others about this.

So in our session, if someone passes the set to you, no problem taking it over. You own it until you end it or pass it to someone else.

If someone who owns a set ends it, it’s over.

Every session and situation is different of course, but this is the preferred practice at our session.

Re: “Picking up”/“Jumping in”/Finishing someone else’s set

It’s certainly a touchy subject. In general, I subscribe to the belief that the player who started the set should determine the entire set, with the exception of when they give indication that they’ve got nothing, and nod to another player, or plead with their eyes for someone to help with the next tune.

But it does happen, and I will do it occasionally. I just spent a week hanging out with some really great players, and it happened maybe three times during about 10 hours of playing, one of which was by me.

I remember feeling a bit offended the first time it ever happened to me, but it was done by a much more experienced player, and I realized that it was a good addition… A couple times, it has happened to me when I wasn’t done with a set, as I was planning to go into another tune and someone hijacks the set. That pisses me off, but it’s not the end of the world.

Nowadays, it’s most often that I will do it when the person who started the set is a student of mine, and the one rule that I try to live by is to make sure that it’s a tune that I am sure that the person who started the set actually plays. But when I did it in the room of great players recently, I was pretty sure nobody else in the room probably knew the tune, but it fit so well with the tunes that were being played at the time, and I wanted to introduce them to the tune… So there is certainly some nuance to how and when it can work.

Re: “Picking up”/“Jumping in”/Finishing someone else’s set

Interesting discussion, y’all, thanks. For what it’s worth, I’ve got profoundly mixed feelings about this practice, so I’m glad to have so many perspectives. It makes me realize that most of my feelings are shared by at least someone out there :)

I like Michael’s image of the storyteller, it captures exactly the feeling I get when this goes wrong. For example, I like to end a set with the Em version of Toss the Feathers, as it’s a great upbeat tune that most people know and you can get to from a lot of different places. Every now and then, I’ll play it and we get to a good, satisfying finish, and then I hear someone launching into the D version, a completely unrelated tune with a completely different upbeat feel that is much less well known (at least, around here) so you get this roaring climax followed by a sort of bedraggled anticlimax. I mean, it’s not the end of the world - worse things happen at sea, and all that - but it’s a bit of a letdown. On the other hand, when it feels like a collaborative effort, it’s great. One thing I’ve noticed is that newer players at my regular session will often play two-tune sets. This is great, they’re pacing themselves, not overreaching, nothing at all wrong with it. But sometimes they’ll get to the end of their second tune and there’s a third one that fits nicely, and it’s one I know I’ve heard them play, I might tag that one on. This often works, in the sense that (a) the person who started the set catches on to the third tune and seems to be enjoying themselves and (b) the rest of the session catches on as well, and the leader of the original set gets to feel like they’ve been a part of something cool.

The one thing that I think I’d add which I think hasn’t been said is, it’s important to make the transition seamless. It sounds kind of lame if the beat is lost - in my opinion, anyway.

Re: “Picking up”/“Jumping in”/Finishing someone else’s set

Getting philosophical, If you assume sessions are social gatherings, then I think there’s no right or wrong in the specific action, it comes down to (as most things in life) what someone’s “intent” was (which is famously difficult to guess).

To take the example above (beginner playing banshee + merry blacksmith) then someone follows up with the rendition, what was the intent.

Was it to celebrate the beginners set, introduce them to a new tune that may work well and bring the tempo and energy back into the session, or was it a show-off move to subtly admonish a “boring” set and show the players skills/be the hero of recovering the session.
