Tuition in Noodling

I’ve been looking at the June worksops for The Edinburgh ALP Scots Music Group and one of the courses seems to be little more than teaching one how to “noodle”.

The course is “Accompaniment” and its description is as follows

"Ever been at a session where there’s too many tunes and you don’t know where to start when it comes to joining in? Ever wanted to just play along with people but were worried you might create havoc? These workshops will pass on some skills you can use in just such situations. The workshops will be looking at the chord structures of tunes and using these as a basis for finding notes you can use to play when you’re not that familiar with the tunes themselves. The class is aimed at people who don’t know very much music theory.
Levels: Any level accepted "

Now, normally I have a lot of respect for the work of the SMG but I’m not sure if teaching people how to take short cuts and “get away with not knowing the tune” is a good idea. I know we’re all guilty of it at times but, as discussed here before, the best approach is to learn the actual tune (even if you only intend to accompany) and if you don’t know it…Listen, listen, listen.

I know it can be frustrating not to be able to play. All players(even the best) find themselves in these situations from time to time but it’s surely a case of listening, learning, practising at home and/or finding a session at your own level rather than looking for short cuts.

Apologies if I’ve misrepresented this course but my opinions are formed from the wording of the course description.

See
http://www.alpscotsmusic.org/wkshp.html

for details of this and other more “orthodox” courses. :-)

Comments on my observations, please?

Some sessions accept noodling and some even welcome it. Depends on the situation. And some people are better than others at picking up tunes on the fly, and some tunes better lend themselves to that.

For situations where it’s “legal” to noodle or pick up tunes on the fly, it can help if you understand the chord and scale structure of this music. It’s not necessary--lots of people can anticipate where a tune is going without even knowing the names of the notes, let alone a dash of music theory. But learning how to recognize keys, modes, chord patters, and scale patterns doesn’t hurt.

Unless you unleash a noodler on a session where noodling is taboo….

Re: Tuition in Noodling

Most of the excellent players I know sit out on tunes they don’t know rather than noodling along. People who know most of the tunes don’t share the same compulsion to noodle that seems more prevalent among people who are learning. If you know you’ll be playing most of the tunes it’s easier to sit and enjoy listening to one you don’t know, but if you’re spending a majority of time listening you start to wonder why you sat down at the table and got your instrument out… and you get this overwhelming impulse to noodle.

The choice seems to be: 1) sit with your instrument in your lap and listen, 2) noodle because you haven’t learned the tune, or 3) pack up the instrument and sit nearby and enjoy listening.

If you choose # 2 you’re in danger of annoying the people trying to play the tune as well as the folks trying to enjoy listening. We used to have a whistle noodler that would sit off to the side hoping to keep from annoying the people playing, but the punters who came for the music became very annoyed. If you play fiddle or mandolin and you can play silently… what’s the point? If you can pick up the tunes on the fly -- congratulations… you’re one of the lucky few. But keep in mind that the only people that will believe you have this skill might be the one “picking up tunes on the fly.”

If you don’t know many tunes, and sitting without playing and just enjoying listening to the music isn’t an option -- find one of those sessions where people are learning tunes and playing parts again slowly etc…. that sort of thing. There are plenty of these sorts of sessions around, I think, according to what’s been written in this forum, but make sure it’s one of those sorts of sessions before you put your pot on to boil.

Re: Tuition in Noodling

Most of the excellent players I know can and do pick up tunes on the fly. In fact, I’ve seen some fairly modestly talented musicians pick up tunes on the fly, and quickly at that. They’re good at it because, like any other musical skill, they’ve practiced it, and most have the sort of understanding hinted at in the SMG course. It helps to practice by picking up tunes from recordings at full speed, and from another player in the privacy of your home. And as we’ve said before, it’s easier to do on some instruments (notably fiddle and mandolin) than others.

But it’s certainly possible to do this without creating Celtic spaghettti or annoying other discerning players. It requires a quick and accurate recognition of pitch intervals, a solid understanding of the building blocks in this music, and reflexive knowing of where to find the notes on your instrument. Most experienced session players I’ve run into have these abilities. When someone learns a tune on the fly at a session, it adds tremendously to the lift, craic, and sense of community around the circle. Most of the oldsters I’ve been around give high regard and even voice an expectation for picking up tunes on the fly, encouraging others to join in--“give it a go, you’ll have it by the third time round.”

And any accompanist worth his or her salt can suss out appropriate chords to most tunes by the second or third time round. Again, it’s a matter of understanding the genre, and knowing your instrument well enough.
