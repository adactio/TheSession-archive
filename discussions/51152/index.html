We are all composers. Each session, set of tunes, & interpretation of those tunes @ a given session are like compositions.

In the header is one method for approaching sessions based on interpretation, never playing parts the same each time through and a modicum of variation. Teasing an anticipation of spontaneity the result is a style of composition with commonly familiar tunes, very close listening to other session players, and good agility for responding to what we hear as best we can in real time.

I don’t want to make too much of tune sets in sessions being compositions since this might come off as arrogant. Not my intention. To me it is more about the organic nature of session participation.

If you have any response to my latest stream of consciousness *What is a session thread?* please say whatever you like. Love it, hate it, just don’t care… Thank you, in advance, everyone!

Re: We are all composers. Each session, set of tunes, & interpretation of those tunes @ a given session are like compositions.

I think I get what you’re going for here, Ben. It isn’t our job to regurgitate notes like a midi player (or the way that the guy standing up, waving the stick around wants them to come out). It’s our job is to express the music in an eloquent way and to put our own personality into that expression.

While that may not really live up to the definition of being a composer, we are putting our own unique spin on the melodies, hopefully in a way that grabs the ears and minds of others. This is definitely a creative process that shares a lot in common with composing melodies!

When I started trying to compose tunes, I learned a lot about the music, and it really opened my eyes to the infinite possibilities there are in tunes. And then I realized that composing really helped me treat melodies in a more fluid way. It certainly helped me with my ability to use variation in my expression of tunes, to the point where I actively encourage my students to compose tunes. Not to become the next Broderick, Reavy, Fahey, or Lennon, but instead to gain a more intimate knowledge of how tunes are put together. You can start to figure out why certain phrases really capture your imagination, how composers combine familiarity and surprise in creating masterpieces, and learn to tell stories with your playing…

I’m not saying I’m a great composer, although a few of my tunes are starting to spread out into the world beyond my local scene… But what I’ve really taken away from the composing is a much improved ability to more eloquently express other people’s compositions…

↳ Posted by trish santer on July 5th, 2026.

Re: We are all composers. Each session, set of tunes, & interpretation of those tunes @ a given session are like compositions.

Though people often think of it as a binary thing, “classical” music having everyone play in strict unison and “jazz” having everyone play anything they want, the real picture is more complicated and nuanced.

There’s a middle ground between homophonic music (everyone playing the same line in unison) and polyphonic music (everybody playing independent lines) which is heterophonic music (simply put, everyone playing slightly different versions of the same line) which exists in many types of traditional music around the world, and is what most ITM session playing is.

https://www.perennialmusicandarts.com/post/four-types-of-texture-in-music

I’ve mentioned before how I was trying to transcribe a Bothy Band reel, and was bamboozled by a brief passage which just sounded like mud.

Playing it at half-speed (back in the day, using a tape recorder capable of that) where the timbres of the fiddle, flute, and pipes were much more clearly distinguishable than at full speed, I could hear that at the moment in question one guy was playing G, one A, and one B simultaneously.

Homophonic it ain’t.

The really interesting thing about this thread is that all the answers above are, presumably, correct, in that they describe how each writer and player approaches an ITM session. By listening to each other, a musical whole is created that works … that’s the theory, at least!

There’s no conductor, but etiquette suggests that the person who starts a set should determine not just the tunes, but also the rhythm and speed (take note, you dreaded speeder-uppers!)
Most classical composers have used folk tunes in their compositions. No-one calls them “just interpreters”. So why are we different?

↳ Posted by halfwaythere on July 10th, 2026.

Re: We are all composers. Each session, set of tunes, & interpretation of those tunes @ a given session are like compositions.

I agree with the “We are all composers” concept, but I apply it to the learning of tunes. I’m sure there are many ways to compose, but one method is to hear a melody in your head and then find the notes on your instrument and/or jot them down on paper. Presumably this would be the process used to compose an original melody, but what if the melody in question matches the sound of the tune known as Rose in the Heather? I think it was Leo Kottke who said each time he woke up from a sleep humming a new tune, he would excitedly go to play it on his guitar only to realize that it was Cripple Creek, over and over again!

That’s sort of my creative assignment for this week…to get the sound known as Rose in the Heather in my head, and then “compose” a tune that sounds as much like Rose in the Heather as possible. If I didn’t have a session to go to then I might simply use Rose in the Heather as an inspiration or starting point and then intentionally veer from it to compose something more original. However, since my intention is to be able to play Rose in the Heather at a session in unison with other people also playing Rose in the Heather, then in this case the composition needs to sound like the pre-existing tune Rose in the Heather.

Sometimes I might hear a television jingle or a South American folk melody (something not Irish at all), and then since this will never be played in a session I can be more creative with the note choices, hopefully disguising the source recording to such an extent that could it could pass for an original melody.
