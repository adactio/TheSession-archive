2flynn, take the notes of the mode in a list, A to A and build chords from them using only the notes found in the mode (or parent Major scale) simple triad chords, no extensions (you can worry about harmonizing them later). notes for the chords I III & V.

In A dorian the first note is A the third is C, but as there is no C# available it’ll have to be a C now add the 5th from the available notes E, A Cnat & E which makes it A minor. Now spell the rest from the available notes.

Now you have all the basic chords for the A dorian mode.

Modal theory is a useful guideline, but that’s all. If a tune has mostly the notes of the D major scale, and it resolves to E, it’s worth using the set of chords that are filed in your head under E dorian as a sort of “first-call” set of changes. That doesn’t mean the tune won’t have a C chord in it, though, and it doesn’t mean that the fiddler won’t play a c natural, and those two things can happen independently of each other. (just because you hear a c in the melody doesn’t mean you have to play a c, or an Am, or an F, or a D7, or anything else that contains a c, unless it happens to be the right chord)

I had the misfortune to be exposed to a few accompanists when I was first learning who thought that tunes came in three modes--major, minor and modal. By modal they meant mixolydian. “Minor” tunes all got the same treatment, with I, VII and VI chords. I did notice that some ‘minor’ tunes accomodated that VI chord well, while in others it didn’t work as well. But then I ran into someone wise, who explained that, despite starting with the same minor chord, there were different types of tunes that were branded as ‘minor’, including tunes whose melodies contained one more sharp than the scale does in the classical minor scale, melodies in the Dorian mode. And in those tunes, that VI chord would not fit. And then I realized some tunes avoided that sixth tone of the scale entirely, and could be pulled in the direction of either classical minor or dorian accompaniment schemes. It was one of those ‘aha’ moments. I am the sort who can hear something isn’t quite right, but until I understand how that is occurring, I don’t do well figuring out how to fix it.
As folks have stated above, there is some of that ambiguity in Star of Munster, especially in the A part. Even if someone plays an F# here and there, they are not so prominent a part of the melody that they clash with an F chord in the accompaniment. The same person who showed me the difference between minor and dorian taught me to play a chord pattern that included F chords under the A part of Star of Munster, and I have done so ever since (oh, and right near the end of the B part, a D chord, with an F# in it, sounds quite nice, also).

The problem with that is that “why it works or why it doesn’t work” is purely subjective. The tradition is of single line melodies that often contradict standard western harmonic theory. For example, the difference between an accidental and a modulation in standard western harmonic theory is clear, but because it is rarely clear with diddley tunes, it renders both terms meaningless. There are many more examples, not least the concept of rootless tunes … And this is before we’ve even considered the use of notes in between notes, for example, all that priceless space in between Cnat and Csharp that is so extensively used.

So strumming to Irish diddley tunes with a fretted instrument is an exercise in shoehorning one traditional harmonic system onto another which is largely alien to it. And this is why there’s so much disagreement. It’s a work-in-progress.

And, of course, don’t just think of it as compromising standard western harmonic theory to fit with diddley tunes. For it to work at all, the diddley tunes have to be compromised also.

As I said at the start, you’ve got a lot of listening to do … because there ain’t no rule book for you to study.

Here’s an easier way to get drop 2 chords, or at least another way, which is easier for me.

Take a four-voice chord - any seventh or sixth chord, basically - in any inversion. So you might have an Am7 (ACEG), or an E6 (EG#BC#), or a D7, first inversion (F#ACD), or woteffah.
Take the second note from the bottom and move it up an octave. (AEGc, EBC#g#, F#CDa) The result is a voicing that will work on a standard-tuned guitar, if you put the bass note on the E, A, or D string. The notes will line up (sometimes awkwardly) on four adjacent strings. This means that if you’re reading a jazz chart (say, Autumn Leaves:

||:Cm7 | F7| Bbmaj7|Eb maj7|Am7 b5|D7 | G | |
|Cm7 | F7| Bbmaj7|Eb maj7|Am7 b5|D7 | G | |
Am7 b5|D7 | G | |Cm7 | F7| Bbmaj7|Eb maj7|
Am7 b5|D7 | Gm7 |F7 Bb7 |Ebmaj7 |Am7b5 D7|G |G :||

)

you can find good voicings for all of those chords by a very simple process. And not only that, since you can invert each of these chords four different ways, and place each of those inversions in any of three places on the neck (one for each of the three bottom strings) you end up with a lot of possible variations, without having to refer to big “chord dictionaries” (ick). This means you can walk around the neck like the real jazz players do, because you know where all the inversions of that Ebmaj7 are, and you can grab the one that’s going to work right now.

Now applying this to trad seems difficult, but I think there’s something to apply. If nothing else, it gives you access to a lot of voicings that you’re not going to come up with on your own, and it makes a very visceral link between some related chords.

As I say, I don’t really use any of this directly in playing trad today, but I wouldn’t mind figuring out how to use some of it. Listening to PJ Hernon play the box right now, I hear in my head how some more closed-form, choppy accompaniment would suit his style nicely. Might be good on hornpipes, polkas. I don’t know, it’s just a notion I’m taken with.

The chord is just the collection of note, and we should be able to agree that it takes three different note NAMES to make up a chord (that is to say octaves don’t count). Inversion describes which note of the chord is on the bottom. Probably we all agree on those. Voicing refers to your choices of the order of tones from the bottom note to the top note. Drop 2 seems to me to fit into this as well as into inversion issues. Spacing is more commonly used in piano voicings or in orchestration to describe the distance between the notes of the chord. Common vocal voicing, for example says a 6th to a 10th between the bass and tenor parts, and about a 6th or less between tenor and alto. The alto to soprano spacing should be the smallest. Jon, I think your drop 2 is upside down.
To quote Tony Marcus (Acoustic Guitar, May 2007):

In a drop-2 chord form, whatever the top note of the chord you skip the next chord tone down and play the next two notes of the chord; then you take that skipped note and use it as the bass note of the chord. In a drop-3 chord form, you do the same process but displace the third note from the top, adding it back an octave below.

Whatever you call the forms doesn’t really matter. I mean, when I’m playing I’m not thinking to myself, “Mmm? I’ll play that as a drop-3.” it’s purely a case of whether you have a damped string between the bass note of a chord and the next note, or not.
