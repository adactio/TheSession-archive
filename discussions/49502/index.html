Re: Playing the recorder in sessions?

It really depends on the session. Generally speaking “Irish trad” sessions would prefer you brought a tin whistle instead. Part of the fun in the hobby is to try and get comfortable on a new instrument and to get a tone that mimics your favourite players (and get your own sound of course). You can get a cheap generation in D and play most of the tunes that will come up, best that you practice it a little before you head a long and come prepared with some popular tunes you might be able to join in on.

That said, I’ve been to many sessions in which you’d be welcome with a recorder as long as you show an open mind to picking up new tunes and perhaps with an eye to switch instrument to a more genre appropriate one in time. Especially in England where the recorders not uncommon. These days there’s many types of session and some of them might have all ranges of instruments. I’ve seen clarinets, cellos and violas heartedly welcomed in the right setting.

Best thing to do is go to your local session, maybe without an instrument, and just check out what the social rules seem to be. Chat to some of them about what kind of music they play/how they would feel about a recorder joining in. Listen and see if you recognise any tunes, or ask them for a few recommended tunes they often play to go away and learn before you come back.

I think it’s best that the trad community be welcome to you and encourage you to make a start in what ever way is best for you. However, the point is to get stuck into the sub cultures and to explore the tradition, so start learning to the tunes and I would say make getting a tin whistle (or perhaps eventually a flute?) your next step.

Re: Playing the recorder in sessions?

Welcome Amira,

These days, we have a wide variety of “sessions” and arrangements where musicians get together. For good or ill!
So, there will be plenty of situations where a recorder will be welcomed or, at least, tolerated as will almost any other instrument including the human voice.

Some of these are set up specifically as “learning” or training environments for novice and intermediate musicians to hone their skills. Others are more of a “free for all” where anything goes. There are many possibilities and extremes.

However, the more traditional style of Irish, Scottish etc session which has prevailed through the years and the type of arrangement which, I imagine, most of the members on this site would prefer tends to have a more conservative(with a small “c” ) view of what works best in a session situation.

So, it is very important to determine what the preferences are in each session you visit. The same applies to all of us. It’s polite to ask if you can “sit in” if you aren’t a regular, for instance, and wise not to join in at all if you are very unsure of the repertoire or your level of capabality require for that session. Also, too many instruments of any kind could prove to be disruptive or unwelcome although fiddles, flutes etc are usually fine with larger numbers.

Having said that, I’m sure a recorder would be welcome enough in many situations but, if in doubt, just ask. As I keep on saying in many other threads “It all depends on the session”.

Re: Playing the recorder in sessions?

They’re not common in my experience but there’s no reason why you should not, in my view. They are capable of the same ornamentations as the whistle, and are more easily played in different keys. The tone is slightly different to a whistle, although the cheaper plastic models are often closer in tone than wooden ones, and stay in tune during a long session.

A more important question is how proficient you are on the instrument. If you are a relative beginner with only memories of some school recorder lessons you might do better to switch to whistle. If you are a proficient player then it will just be a matter of adapting your technique to suit the genre. For example, whistle players tend to tongue less than recorder players.

Some may object that recorder is not a traditional Irish instrument, unlike the fiddle (Italian), banjo (Afro-American), bouzouki (Greek), concertina (Anglo-German), uilleann pipes (probably Scottish or English), whistle (Chinese, but English in the 6-hole form used today) or harp (ancient Egypt). Removing my tongue from my cheek, there are some sessions where that will matter, but in most sessions it should not. It will really be up to your fellow musicians to decide whether you are welcome.

Re: Playing the recorder in sessions?

It’s not the recorder. It’s the recorder players!

For some people who’ve had some classical training on recorder (often from a German repertoire) it’s difficult to unlearn those idioms of timing, articulation, and ornamentation and sound “correct” in the Irish trad context. They frequently sound like Germans trying to play Irish music (and in my experience, often are LITERALLY Germans trying to play Irish music!).

There’s no reason you can’t play Irish trad on the recorder, and play it well. Whether you’re German or not. It’s a matter of being aware of the different stylistic idioms, and blending with folks around you and the traditions of players who came before.

Any resistance you might encounter isn’t your fault, nor the recorder’s, but the result of bad experiences with other players who couldn’t unlearn their classical German training, or didn’t realize what was happening.

I think maybe the most common issue is overarticulating with the tonguing while playing tunes, and not grasping the “swing” in Irish music. Or at all.

There are some fingering and timbre differences, especially in higher registers, which the whistle/recorder players can point out. But those can be dealt with.

If you’re interested in Irish trad music, I would recommend you “embrace the horror,” and buy a few whistles in appropriate keys (C, D, G, and F). You’ll be glad you did! And you can go a long way without the G and F, if you like.

More important is that you listen to tons of great whistle players like Mary Bergin, Vin Kilduff, Micho Russell, and Colin Farrell and try to emulate that more relaxed sound regardless of whether you’re on recorder or whistle.

Well, Russell wasn’t all that legato. But you get the idea!

Re: Playing the recorder in sessions?

amirahiggs, you say you’re relatively new to recorders. Do you play some other instrument(s)? Perhaps an instrument more typical within the Irish tradition? And do you already Irish trad music?

I ask only because the few recorders I’ve heard at Irish sessions were tootled by long-experienced recorder players who had recently developed an interest in Irish trad. In almost every instance, it seemed to me that their passion for recorder was stronger than their (perhaps) passing fancy in trad music. That said, I do know two recorder players who dove head first into Irish trad. Both of them soon switched to whistle for this music, though one of them still played recorder for other genres. Both grew to be excellent trad whistle players.

If you want to immerse in Irish trad music and play regularly at sessions, it will be less of an uphill battle if you play the music on a trad instrument, that is, one that the tunes and repertoire have evolved on. An instrument whose tonal and other qualities are already part of the tradition.

On the other hand, if recorder is really your passion, and you’d like to dabble in Irish music and sessions, then by all means learn recorder. You can pick up trad tunes and go to sessions as far as your local scene will welcome you. Just be aware that not all sessions will offer you a seat.

FWIW, Irish sessions generally fall into one of two broad camps: (1) jams, open to a wider range of instruments and perhaps even musical abilities, and tunes from other genres; and (2) sessions with a tighter focus on Irish traditional tunes and norms, usually including instrumentation. (Obviously, there’s a range of approaches between these two ends of the spectrum, and any given session may run the gamut from one week to the next.)

I’ve enjoyed playing in both and am happy that there’s room in the world for both to thrive. For that to happen, however, it helps if people from one camp don’t try to crash the other camp’s gatherings. When we visit a session, it’s on us to fit in, whatever the local vibe is, rather than try to morph it to our own liking.

The instruments commonly used in a namable musical context coalesce as part of the process in which the music, itself, develops. ITM is just one example and the question can (and has) been asked about why the recorder never found its way into the classical woodwind quintet.

Andy Irvine often tells the story of how the bouzouki entered the ensemble we’re squabbling about here, for example, in this video snippet: https://youtu.be/s7sv396lLS8

He blames Johnny Moynihan who comments on his own perception of the upshot: https://youtu.be/dQWLA2GHfO8

The outcome might be similar for the recorder — or any other of the many instruments that have been put forward as candidates for acceptance — if a similarly influential member of the ITM community were to campaign toward that end. The same result would likely also result if a wide consensus were to emerge among session leaders toward regularly admitting an instrument that is currently regarded as a more or less welcome occasional guest.

Just to chime in here (3 days after this discussion has ended, apparently), there is absolutely no logical reason why a recorder wouldn’t make a good session instrument. I’ve been to sessions with recorder players who used Irish ornamentation, and it sounded good. If anything, the recorder is MORE capable of doing Irish ornaments than the whistle is. Re: Playing the recorder in sessions?

As a recorder player, I’m used to being frowned on, except by fellow recorder players and a few people who know better ;-)

My main advice is, don’t try a new genre and a new instrument at the same time. When I started playing Irish trad five years ago, I played nothing but recorders (tenor mostly). We had separate learner and slow sessions in Hamburg back then, where basically all levels and all instruments (except brass) were welcome.

At that time, I had two cheap high D tin whistles (Generation and Feadóg), which I never used because I just hated the squeaky sound and the botched tuning.

I’m not a professional recorder player, but a serious amateur experienced in lots of genres. When I started with Irish music, the main challenge and motivation for me was to play from memory without any sheet music. It felt a bit uncomfortable at first, but very liberating and enriching. After four or five months, I started going to our regular pub sessions as a listener, soon I was invited by some of the players who knew me from the slow session to bring my recorder next time and join in.

As I said, I usually played the tenor which has almost the same range as an Irish flute or a low D whistle, so you’re much less exposed than on a soprano. My tenor is rather loud for a recorder, but even that is easily topped by any flute and most low whistles, so in a session with about ten instruments including pipes and fiddles, it just adds some timbre and blends in very nicely.

There were a few raised eyebrows, but nobody ever complained about my recorder not being authentic, or maybe people were just too polite.

Anyway, during Covid lockdowns I had lots of time to expand my repertoire and to improve my technique. I bought my first low D whistle and had enough time to get familiar with it before pub sessions resumed.

Currently, I mainly play an MK Low D and a Wild High D from McNeela. I don’t think I would have landed there if I hadn’t had the chance to get started in Irish sessions with my recorders. The main reason why I now prefer the MK to a tenor recoder is simply volume: this may sound trivial but can quite an issue when you want to start a set and make yourself heard in pub environment.

Regarding ornamentation and tonguing, that’s mainly a question of the player’s technique and a not of the recorder compared to a whistle. You just shouldn’t play an Irish jig like a Bach gigue.

Some ornaments are easier on the whistle for lack of cross-fingerings. On the other hand, cross-fingerings give you a fully chromatic range of two octaves plus a bit on the recorder, so there’s no issue with the occasional F natural, G# or D#, and you even have a low C# and C.

“It might be pretty messy playing tunes at the proper pitch with the proper ornamentation on an alto if you’re transposing so that the tunes play at the proper key in a session.”: I wouldn’t say it’s “messy,” no. Difficult? Sure. Playing in D major on an alto whistle is certainly more difficult than playing D major on a high D whistle. Many notes have to be cross-fingered. But it’s still very doable. If you watch the video I posted, the first tune was 250 to Vigo in B minor (same notes as D major), and I was able to play that with a little practice. I have no doubt that someone more adept than me at the alto recorder could play faster tunes in D major on one.

Also, to the extent that it’s difficult, nothing is difficult about the ornamentation. Yes, there are some cross-fingered notes that need ornaments, but none of them are hard to ornament. And unlike on whistle, there are no annoying notes like C and C sharp that are difficult to ornament. Pretty much every note is easy to ornament, whether it’s cross-fingered or not.

Hopefully this serves as a good example of how flexible the recorder is. Even an F recorder can play in D without too much difficult, and with proper ornamentation. And of course, it can also play in F, G, and C fairly easily.
