“crazy” tunes

Hey everyone, just back from the Catskills Irish Week (I had to come home mid-week, sadly), but I left Conán, Bridie, Zina, Jason, Joyce & others having a GREAT time at Furlong’s etc! As per our tradition, we played tunes on Sunday night until well after sunrise Monday morning, then sat out on the lawn near the river drinking beer til at least 9 or 10am! :)

OK so this was Bridie’s first trip to the States, & I had just visited her & Dow in March & seen the sessions down in Sydney. Bridie (bb) is mad for what she calls ‘crazy’ tunes, ie Flook, McGoldrick, the newer Solas, etc. Like when she heard Zina play Exile of Erin, she yelled, “Now that’s a sexy tune, Zina Lee!”

She also went on to lament the apparent absence of Crazy Tune Sessions (hereafter referred as CTS’s) at the Catskills, noting that she knew all the styles of all the teachers & none of them were likely to be hosting anything other than more ‘old school’ sessions, including Liz Carroll (who also incidentally denies that she is a nurse btw :) ). Beebs talked about how sessions in London are either CTS’s or old school, but never both, & it’s my very tentative belief that sessions in the States veer more to the old school with occasional Crazy Tunes thrown in for a laugh, but with very few (if any) devoted simply to CTs. The other Yanks with whom we were discussing this decided it was probably b/c we aren’t Irish, & want to stay as close to the tradition as possible. I mean, I played White Petticoat one time (which is in O’Neill’s) & my session leader, highly respected, Co Meath etc, said he’d never play it, it just didn’t sound “Irish enough.” & he writes great new tunes all the time, but they just aren’t “crazy.”

A. What is a Crazy Tune? I mean, just b/c a tune is new doesn’t make it crazy. I think it has more to do with accidentals, unpredictable rhythms etc, like Guns of the Magnificent Seven, etc. Dow, can you help us out here?

B. Do you know of any CTS’s in your neighborhood, or is your local a CTS? Wot’s it like? Fill us in, would you?

C. Do you, or can you LOL, mix crazy tunes into sets with more traditional tunes? If so, were there harsh penalties inflicted by the Trad Police?

D. Personally, I am not into playing CT’s quite yet, sort of like how Picasso had to learn how to sketch the human figure before he could move on to Cubism, you know?

Thanks very much.

PS Dow, dolls says she loves you very much & misses you terribly, & that’s not an exaggeration. :)

Re: “crazy” tunes

Crazy tunes or crooked tunes are ones that are missing rhythmic beats. Typically I think that they have nine bars per section instead of the usual eight. One that I learned (old-time tune called Old Black Cat) was crooked in that the last measure in each section was shorted two beats.

They also can be in interesting modalities, or switch modes.

Most of the jam’s (I’ve got to train myself to use the term session, but it’s so unnatural) that I go to throw in one or two (or more) crooked tunes, only if enough people know them. They are some of the hardest dang tunes to catch hold of aurally in a session type situation.

Re: “crazy” tunes

Almost all folk music was composed and played long before it was written down - sometimes a very long time before. It’s when you try to force tunes into the strait-jacket of standard classical notation with bar lines that some of them can look “crazy”.

A case in point is in Bartok’s Concerto for Orchestra (not the easiest of works to play or to listen to - I know, we performed it only last week) where in the slow movement there is a haunting and most beguiling Hungarian folk tune for the violas. As written it is mostly 2/4 alternating with 5/8, with an occasional 7/8 thrown in for good measure. So it looks frightening to play - you’ll see some players desperately counting quavers silently - but if you strip out the bar lines it becomes the most natural thing in the world to play.

This is one reason why Irish slow airs are next to impossible to notate classically. It is much better just to put a series of tailless dots on the music paper, don’t use bar lines, and let the sense of the music tell you what to play. All you need is a marker or two to show you where the main sections are. You’ll probably end up with a very efficient notation reminiscent of mediaeval plain chant.

Trevor

Re: “crazy” tunes

Crazy is something different, out of the ordinary unexpected, and that will be different for all of us. For me it can be an unusual chord inversion or reharmonisation of a familiar tune. I loved the switch to Amaj in the A part of Farewell to Connaught. It can be a subtle shift in the emphasis of rythym in the middle of a tune. Try shifting one beat in Foxhunters to turn a slip jig into a Daichovo hora. It can be the shift of rythym across bar lines ot make transcription hell. I had real troubles with Pamela’s Ruchinitsa because of this. I think the modern Scots piping tradition has been exploring this at length (Andy Renwick’s Ferret). It can be crooked tunes. Someone else on this site pointed out the Smeseno horo means crooked dance.
An dthen there is the novelty that comes from the mixing of two traditions. If it works and is repeated long enough, it becomes new tradition. Really ‘out there’ for me in this regard is Mark Sauls latest celtic pipe album.. well celtic/funk/house/various
http://www.marksaul.tv/
In short, if the crazy stuff gets played and played, it either becomes boring and is relegated to ‘pot-boiler’ status or it becomes part of the tradition and is no longer crazy. So amongst the crazy stuff is a lot of dross and also some signposts to the future. Mostly you can’t tell at first.
