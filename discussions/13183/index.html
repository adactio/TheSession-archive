Inn-tolerance

The tall dark stranger strode into the bar, with two odd packages under his arm & after buying a beer, headed for our table …………………………

I’m posting this thread because of the lack of tolerance that some musicians here seem to display at sessions, & this topic actually cropped up in a recent thread here.

Anyway, back to the dude. It turned out that what he had under his arm was a music stand & a silver flute!
So, always eager to welcome passing musical strangers, we did our best to make him feel welcome & as the session got going, every now & then, when there was a lull, he would launch into a song air. He was a fairly quiet bloke & he played song airs mostly, oh & one jig, at a very relaxed pace & was clearly not used to going to ITM sessions. He didn’t annoy us at all though, well except perhaps for our piper, who kept tripping over his stand each time he headed for a pint or a pish!

Anyway, I have seen it written here, so many times now, how some musicians can be very blinkered & selfish when it comes to *their* precious session time, so I wondered how many sessioneers here would have encouraged him, as we did. How many would have tried to make him feel uncomfortabe, in the hope that he would just go away & or at least never come back & how many would have told him to pack up & feck off, cause this was *your* session, not a fecking orchestra rehearsal!

The strange thing is the public really enjoyed hearing him play, whether because his slow tunes were a welcome break from our diddley jigs & reels or whether it was because they could actually recognize the tunes, I don’t know. But I do know that a pub audience does usually appreciate it if you can play something they know every now & then, in amongst all the diddle & the dee!

So pray tell, how would your session have handled *the stranger*?

Re: Inn-tolerance

We recently had a guy with a recorder and a 3-ring binder full of O’Carolan tunes show up at our session. Although our session is not “pure drop” by any standard, I did remark that that the recorder sounded “English” to me--not that I’m any sort of expert, in either genre. It was an honest reaction to something different at our session--we have never had a recorder before. I suppose I hoped it would encourage the fellow--who was not a bad player--to switch to whistle. And maybe he will.

It’s an interesting conundrum. Do you discourage willing participants, in order to preserve a more “Irish” (the same “not an expert” disclaimer applies) sound--or do you allow a more eclectic mix, in order to eventually make more friends for Irish music?

I, for one, was completely clueless when I first ventured into the Irish-session world. Some people were very tolerant, others less so. In my case, I was comforted by the more tolerant sorts, challenged to do better by the others, and today I still persevere. (Whether this is a good thing for ITM, has yet to be determined.)

In short: If somebody is interested enough to show up and give it an honest try, encourage them. Irish music is no different from anything else--it can use all the friends it can get. IMHO.

Re: Inn-tolerance

Some years ago, a woman showed up at our session with a recorder. She spoke with a heavy Quebecois accent, but knew a couple of Irish tunes, and obviously had serious music talent. It didn’t take her long to switch to whistle, with very little prodding from us, because she could hear that the recorder sounded “too clean” for this music, and the whistle’s chiff was more appropriate.

She now plays with us once or twice a week, has a stupendous ear, often picks up complex tunes on the fly and has them nailed a week later, and adds a lot to the craic at our session. She’s also learned tunes from recordings and brought them to us, introducing fresh tunes to our repertoire.

On the other side of the coin, a young violinist took an interest in our session at one point. She played from the dots and regularly complained that we were excluding her by playing “too fast.” I invited her to our tune learning session, and I gave her a couple of free one-on-one lessons. But it never clicked. It was near impossible for her to trust her ear, and she took the dots literally, resisting any explanation of how to put pulse into jigs, reels, or hornpipes. Needless to say, she didn’t last long--by her own choice.

We always give people the benefit of the doubt, and that welcome has rarely been abused. In 10 years or more now at this local session, I can think of only two times one of us had to step in and ask a person to limit their participation or leave.

Hey, morning star, those plastic recorders have an edge on them often, and plastic can kind of catch anyway. Some plastic whistles have the same kind of catch when you try to slur a note. A good wooden recorder is often shaped so the finger can easily sweep across the holes for slurs, or for half-holing, as cross-fingered isn’t the be-all and end-all…

I think someone else hit it well, the Rennaisance designs and tones would be much better blends in ITM. That said, if you want old, the early recorders, flutes and bone and wood whistles were once the woodwinds of choices. If you want an amazing pure tone, I’ve played one bone whistle (tibia) and one ivory, and WOW! It might not fit to some folks ears, but to me they were beautiful to see, touch, play and hear…

I would never not welcome someone because of what they carried, but how they carried themselves might cause me to take note, especially if it infringed on the established community. Even then, despite my examples and earlier spouting off, I never would approach someone to exclude them, but to try to work them in, if they were able. I leave the choice of ‘exclusion’ up to the individual. My first hope is that they will feel welcomed and will join us and add to the craic with their spirit and character, that the community present is what they are hoping for…

Like the Puddy Tat, over years I think I’ve only thrice been pushed to the point of asking someone to leave, and as said, I hate that, it made me feel ill… Thinking hard, it was their intolerance and disrespect that lead to ‘our’ needing to ask them to move on.
