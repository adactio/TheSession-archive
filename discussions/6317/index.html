A significant number of tunes played in sessions in Ireland and accepted as part of the Irish tradition are English in origin, or at least, are or have been common to many parts of the British Isles including Ireland and England. The hornpipe, as a tune and dance form, is generally agreed to be English in origin and, although many hornpipes have been composed in Ireland since the arrival of the form there, many of the common hornpipes are English or have English variants.

Re: English Traditional

England still does have many similarities to the Irish tradition! In fact, the Irish session music tradition was imported from England by immigrants to Ireland mainly in the 17th century. The Jig was invented in England along with the hornpipe, and the Reel was developed from the French quadrille. There are many fantastic English tunes- modal and fast, slow airs, happ-clappy ones and more!

There are a few main reasons why most people think of English music as a bit boring:

England has always been more suceptable to European influence than the further reaches of Ireland. The gentry were always the leaders of fashion, and as the ballroom culture came in, Polkas, waltzes and schottisches were played more often than the native Jigs, Reels and Hornpipes.

The style of tunes preserved in areas is connected to the type of dancing. In my native East Anglia, hornpipes and step Polkas are in abundance because the traditional dancing of East Anglia is Step Dancing. Yes, the fast footwork you see in Ireland, except you generally have your hands out in front instead of behind.

Many popular tunes come from England. Take these for example:

Haste to the Wedding
The Irish Washerwoman
Mooncoin Jig
Drops of Brandy…

There are loads of superb English tunes. I play Irish, Scottish, Shetland and English tunes all together on the mandolin and they will all sit very well next to one another. Don’t reject it cos you don’t know it or because it’s English… you’ll be missing out.
I met a lovely girl from Bray in Dublin outside a Shane MacGowan gig and she asked me what trad. English music was like. Very much the same as Irish but a bit more ‘bouncy’ was the general answer. ‘In fact’, I said, ‘you stole loads of our tunes’, ‘fair enough’, she said, ‘you stole our island!’ Can’t argue with that, eh?
Cheers for giving me room to rant.

Tommy is absolutely right about there being loads of English tunes in O’Neill’s. If you go back a couple of hundred years there was quite a large common repertoire in England, Ireland and Scotland. Here is a link to a scan of a North Yorkshire fiddler’s tunebook from about 1803 (hope it works!)-

Cromwell and music

I don’t think he was a bad ruler in England (Ireland’s another story). His government banned elaborate church music, but people could freely play and sing in their homes and in tavern clubs - the latter, acc. one authoritative dictionary I’ve read, being the ancestors of the concert hall in England. (And the folk club and session also, I imagine.) Composers Gay (“the Beggars’ Opera”) and Purcell were probably practising their scales under Cromwell, as they flourished not so long after.

Re: English Traditional

From the Reformation English music was strong on songs, tunes, hymns and short/incidental pieces. The Anglican Church didn’t really showcase elaborate choral music again till c.19. In the c.18-19 the court (with German roots anyway) was obsessed with Continental Classical musicians
and composers, and why not, they were the true greats - helped along by the fact that in Germany, e.g., any number of courts vied to host new Baroque/Classical music, while in England there was only the one. So there was never going to be an English Bach, Mozart, Schubert or Beethoven. Meanwhile Handel went down a storm over here - and De Danann’s 1980’s version of “The Entry Of The Queen Of Sheba ” is indeed a treat. I’ll mention another composer below.
