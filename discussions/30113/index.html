Your posting has me pondering two questions.
(only two? - HAH! That’ll be the day…)

So:
1. What is it you are not getting from participating at this session, ie. what are your expectations? What do you really want from them? Define what you REALLY want.
[I know what I want from my “dream” session - tunes I know, people I know, and a few of each that I didn’t know yesterday :-)]

2. Are you willing to invest more to gain what you want? would it be worth it?

What I mean is, if what you want is acceptance in a rather closed, elitist musicial group, your situation is obvious. Do what they do and you may be accepted, at least as a musician. If the group is socially oriented and structured, however, you have another set of challenges.

I tend to prefer sociable groups, but with most of them playing at a at least an intermediate level - the point is to have fun and make a grand noise, right?
One session I followed for awhile was very embracing, initially, to pretty much anyone of good intent, but I noted that novices who chose to remain novice were not well accepted. If one wanted to be a chronic hack/dabbler, you were setting a pace and level that was no fun fot the rest. One might be marked as a drag on the group’s experience.
Another session I recall was rather a mish-mosh musically, but quite the social phenom - more chit-chat than music, sometimes. Drops-ins could spout their sea-chanteys and C&W repertoire at will, to the punters applause. It seemed much more about making friends than making music (although one can wish for both at a session). Not bad, just different.

Both sessions were what the members made them.
Both were happy and vital for awhile.
Both have disappeared with time and circumstances.
And I miss both of them equally.

Is there a session near you that fits you better?
Could you start one of your own for you and you peers?

Good Luck.

Re: Not a defence of mediocrity, but…

I know countless excellent players who ‘have a family and work and a house to maintain and all the other commitments life throws at us.’

So those commitments are not an excuse for not being a great player. It’s probably more to do with the fact that you mightn’t have the same natural ability as others. Not your fault, everyone isn’t born with the same skills.

You sound to me a bit like an Irish midfield player who bemoans the fact that he’s not in the Spanish team! If the Spanish football team had the Irish midfield, the rest of the team would get mightily frustrated because the midfield would keep losing the ball!

The Irish midfielder works hard but no matter what he does he’ll never have the ability of the Spanish because he wasn’t trained in their system as a young boy. Knowiing his shortcomings he settles for the fact that he’ll never be that good and so he doesn’t work as hard as his Spanish counterparts. He blames other life issues for his failings, forgetting the fact that many of his Spanish counterparts have the same life issues as him!

If you find it hard to keep up with the standard at the sessions you usually attend perhaps try going to other sessions where players are either

(a) More at your level
(b) at a mixed level and the better players are happy to play with players with less ability (most sessions are like this from my experience)

I get the sense that the players you are talking about treat trad VERY seriously and don’t like it when inferior players bring their enjoyment down. That’s more their problem than yours, however I wouldn’t take offense at sessions like this,

I completely understand this mentality also. I’ve been in sessions where one mediocre player ruins the whole thing by insisting on ‘leading’ too many sets. This brings the quality down too much and frustrates the other players and the listeners. It’s particularly frustrating for the other players of less abillity who have enough respect to sit patiently, listen and LEARN!

So, the usual session ettiquette if you want to play a set of tunes is to wait until asked by those leading the session, presuming someone is leading the session. Usually if it’s a friendly session you will be asked at some stage in the night to play something. This is an old tradition that is being lost more and more due to pushy players of a mediocre level who insist on taking over sessions without asking.

People used to visit Willie Clancy and listen to him play, then he’d ask his guests to play something. There’s a great sense of respect in that kind of scenario that beats the hell out of 30 people banging out tunes all night without listening to each other!

Pushy players outside of the main circle of top players are not usually appreciated by anyone. So accept your limitations, find a place to play where your limitations aren’t a handicap to others. If you find yourself in a situation where the players you are playing with are way above your level, instead of bringing them down by insisting they come down to your level, sit back, listen and learn!

One thing I’ve come to realise is that you can get an idea of what to expect of a session from when it takes place.

Sessions on a Sunday afternoon or Monday evening when the pub is quiet or deserted tend to be relaxed and tolerant - the musicians are playing mainly for themselves and will probably be quite happy to accommodate newcomers of any ability. >>“ Why should the perfect music be made specifically to impress listeners in the bar? Why can’t it be made for the pleasure of the musicians as they play it?”

Well yes. Unless you are getting paid a shed load of money for it, I think it is safe to say everyone plays for the enjoyment of playing.

But that isn’t really the point. The point is that everyone gets more self conscious about their playing when they know people are listening. In private practice you try things and take risks that you wouldn’t do in front of an audience. And the same thing happens to sessions. If the session is just the musicians playing amongst themselves, they will be more tolerant of bad playing, and people pushing their personal capabilities to the point where they stuff up occasionally. If there is an audience, the musicians may still be playing for their own enjoyment, but they also want to make the evening enjoyable for those listening, so become less tolerant of bad playing.

Anyone who says ‘We just play for ourselves, and don’t care what the other people in the pub think’ is being just as inconsiderate and rude as someone who says ‘I just want to join the session at my own level, and don’t care what the other sessioneers think’.

Re: Not a defence of mediocrity, but…

I was interested to read the Yeats passages. He seems to be saying that “he”, the idler / musician, will one day touch the core of those who may have despised him when they were all much younger with music that evokes in *them* something of profound joy and recognition: he will give each of his hearers a personal gift.

I think the other possibility, that he’ll be admired specifically for his industry in achieving musical competence and turning out to do spots, e.g., when his music hasn’t actually moved his tin-eared listeners or, maybe, anybody, is dismissed as a sad one: indeed, the admiration may not be forthcoming at all.

Mind, the poem can’t cover every base, or pronounce upon the value of the life or deeds of every individual. And my own take on it is not particularly rigorous.

Nearly all the people I know who play music (any kind) see it as a decidedly important, motivating thing in their lives. Meanwhile, those who seem to treat it as a rather limited, compartmented thing in their lives tend to use it as an adjunct to mainstream activities, such as teaching, or to drama. It isn’t wasted.
