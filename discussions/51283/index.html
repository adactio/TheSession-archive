Re: Please suggest some very simple tunes to teach an older beginner.

I often start my students on single reels. Most of my students are banjo or mandolin players, and a straight 4/4 time is often easier for complete beginners to grasp than a jig form or the intricacies of the polka rhythm, even though there are certainly some simple tunes to be found in either of those forms.

The single reels are generally not too complex, but they can still give a new player a good idea about the call and response nature of the music, matching resolving phrases between parts, etc. And single reels are often built with repeating parts that are half as long as a regular reel, which makes them easier to learn. And finally, many single reels include a part that has a variation in the melody instead of fully repeating, which gives the learner some skill at being flexible.

Most commonly I will teach a brand new student Rolling In The Ryegrass (https://thesession.org/tunes/87), or Throw Away The Keys (https://thesession.org/tunes/1540) as their first tune.

> Yes, she would progress faster with the whistle. Ideally I’d like her to do both!

What the whistle gives you is an idea of what’s involved. You’d expect the fiddle to be hard, but a whistle? Six-year-olds play them. Until you’re asked to play eight notes in a particular order, at which point you start wondering if you were born with your fingers on back to front.

People have to find out whether they enjoy learning and can cope with times where it feels as if they’ll never get anywhere. Music needs time, persistence, humour, etc, but also a real desire to do it. If you discover you have that, you know you can learn anything.

Re: Please suggest some very simple tunes to teach an older beginner.

Lisnagun Jig in G is one of the easiest whistle tunes in existence.

Arran Boat Song is excellent for learning to manage breathing, with natural places to catch an inhale. And it’s a beautiful melody even at a dead crawl.

Both are repetitive just enough for the phrases to latch on quickly, but with a clear call-and-response structure between the parts.

Whistling Fionn makes a great point—she’ll be playing tunes on whistle in a day. Fiddle takes a great deal more patience. And there’s no harm in learning both as long as she tempers her expected learning pace on fiddle.

Hello. As a whistle player myself (and as Fionn said), when you are a beginner, the high notes can be a little traumatic (maybe with the fiddle too), so I think it’s great to start with tunes that don’t go too high (that is, they don’t reach high A and B). Also, I would favour more linear tunes; I mean, with fewer jumps (as opposed to Cooley’s or Drowsie Maggie). For these reasons, I would suggest:

1. The Foggy Dew: https://thesession.org/tunes/2516 (pretty easy, honestly; a classic for beginners)
2. Jimmy Ward’s: https://thesession.org/tunes/793
3. The Swallowtail: https://thesession.org/tunes/106 (in the key of Dmaj/Edor, as it has fewer high notes and seems more popular).
4. The Blarney Pilgrim: https://thesession.org/tunes/5
5. Off to California: https://thesession.org/tunes/30
6. The Old Favourite: https://thesession.org/tunes/56

Additionally, this one requires some high notes but has a pretty easy/understandable melody:
7. The Britches Full of Stitches: https://thesession.org/tunes/1075

I know I’ve mentioned just a few, but I tried to squeeze my repertoire into the easiest ones.

Finally, I agree with what other users have said: I think that it’s better to learn those two instruments than just one. Maybe it would be great to start with the whistle (as it’s easier and cheaper) and then, once she’s familiar with the genre, start with the fiddle too. Playing multiple instruments gives you more posibilities (and twice as fun!).

Yeah, it’s not the widest known tune by any stretch, but it is known by a bunch of different names, too (surprise, surprise!) I think it has primarily been recorded under the name “Gorman’s”, but that name leads to a fair amount of confusion. I originally learned it off an old live recording album called Maiden Voyage, which was recorded at Pepper’s in Feakle in the 90s. But I know of other recordings of it, including Stockton’s Wing, Kevin Crawford, and a few others.

The reason I like teaching that tune to a beginner is because a lot of the tune works in scalewise motion without too many twists and turns. So if I just taught somebody the D major scale, this is an easy tune for them to grasp…

↳ Posted by WhistlingFionn on August 31st, 2026.

Re: Please suggest some very simple tunes to teach an older beginner.

Some easy-ish tunes for fiddle (not necessarily tested on whistle):

Reels:
Humours of Tulla
https://thesession.org/tunes/141
The version that I learned (quite similar to the first setting given here) is only 4 bars (and a repeat) per part: not too daunting to memorize. It does require a fiddler to alternate between a low 2nd finger and a high 2nd finger, however, which I’ve noticed can be a bit of extra work for some folks just starting off…but maybe a good tune to use for that very purpose.

Buntàta ’s Sgadan (Potatoes and Herring)
See version #6
https://thesession.org/tunes/4389
A great Scottish tune (one of Allan MacDonald’s compositions) rather than an Irish one: not quite as neatly repetitious as “Tulla,” but close.

The Ale is Dear
See Version #1
https://thesession.org/tunes/866
A highland pipe reel that consists of 4 repeating bars per part. It might just be me, but I’ve always felt that minor tunes have an extra level of addiction in them. This one sure does!

Jigs:

Deirdre’s Fancy
Slip Jig
https://thesession.org/tunes/1538
First setting
Four bars that repeat per part. Again, on fiddle, you do have to keep track of low 2nd vs high 2nd finger. It was suggested as a particularly good tune for beginning whistle in this article:
https://blog.mcneelamusic.com/the-best-beginner-tunes-for-irish-trad-tune-flute-and-tin-whistle/

Na Ceannabháin Bhána (The Little Fair Canavans)
https://thesession.org/tunes/612
See version 9
Another slip jig with 4 repeating bars per line. Really fun to play.

Páidín Ó Raifeartaigh (Paddy O’ Rafferty)
See version 1
https://thesession.org/tunes/8983
Rather confusingly shares a title with several other tunes.
A delightful 6/8 jig that, at its most elemental is also 4 repeating bars per part. It’s a fun one to put variations into if you want to, but it plays very nicely as is, also. Patrick Street recorded this one in a set with “Na Ceannabháin Bhána.”

Cailleach A’ Ghobhainn
A highland pipe tune, a puirt a beul, and one of the most splendidly contagious tunes in existence.
https://tunes.gfw.scot/tune.html?tune=445

Dan Collins’ Father’s Jig
See version 11
https://thesession.org/tunes/4389
This tune is also sometimes called “Boys of Ballymote.” A really nice tune, that just happens to be easy as well. It’s a great one for memorizing since the entire second line of the B part is exactly the same as the second line of the A part. All of the settings here have it starting on high D, but to avoid a later 4th finger note for a beginning fiddler, you can transpose it down an octave, where it sits very comfortably and sounds a good deal more mellow.

The Snowy Path
See version #3
https://thesession.org/tunes/104
Slightly less repetitious than a lot of the other tunes on this list, but charming, and still within a reasonable grasp of memorization. Maybe it’s just a local thing, but I’ve never heard it played terribly fast at a session, which also might make it less daunting for a beginner.

Maison de Glace (The Ice House)
See version 3
https://thesession.org/tunes/9002
A French Canadian tune written by the Brunet brothers that has gained a toehold in several of the local sessions. Not quite as structurally repetitious as most of the other tunes I’ve suggested, but very melodic and sits very comfortably on the fiddle.


Other:
Hunting the Squirrel
https://thesession.org/tunes/3333
See version #4
According to past posts on The Session this is an English dance tune. I love the way The Gloaming recorded it, as a very slow march, which I assume is rather slower than standard, but which is just plain beautiful. If I was teaching it to a beginner, though, I’d opt for putting it in A or D, rather than their Bb!

ELCBK, did you learn to sing scales before Row, Row, Row Your Boat?

Three year olds learn violin (Suzuki method) in part because no one tells them, “This is difficult, this instrument is harder than others.”

Mars Man’s situation may be different because his friend is on in years. Whistle is mechanically simpler to get a tune out of than a fiddle. But if this person wants to play fiddle, I say go for it. And start with simple tunes—no point in wasting time!

Re: Please suggest some very simple tunes to teach an older beginner.

Well, if you are playing form the dots or want to do so, some basic knowledge of scales is useful so that you know where the notes are.

Of course, there’s no need to endlessly practice them and/or do various exercises. You can work them out for yourself too just by singing “doh ray me ” etc. Before I taught myself to read music, I had learned to find where all the sounds were on the instrument and it was a case of locating the notes as and when I heard the tunes or if they were already in my head.

Having said that, I see no harm in being able to read music and even learn tunes that way too. It’s a useful tool but I usually also refer to what I actually hear..e.g. from other players or recordings or interpret them in my own way.

Clarke Sweetones are very easy to play, though I find the overlapped seam a bit annoying. Clarke originals have a lovely tone but need more air than most other high D whistles. Not sure it’d be my first choice for a beginner.
