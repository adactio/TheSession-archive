Re: Reading music during a session

I’d say it very much depends on what you mean by an “Irish session”. For the only kind of session I was aware of for my first 40 years of playing this music, I would say it was not so much tacky as totally contrary to the spirit of a session. The idea is to play together, to share your knowledge of and joy in the music, and that means knowing the music. If you are following sheet music or a setting on your phone or tablet, you are not truly participating, and you are not able to blend in with whatever version of the tune is being played by those you are with (rather than whatever version you have in front of you) - it’s a form of gross disrespect in my view.

In recent years however lots of varieties of sessions have sprung up around the globe, including those advertised as “slow sessions”, “learner’s sessions”, etc. In these types of gathering playing from sheet music may not be frowned upon or even encouraged.

I would say strive to free yourself from sheet music as quickly as possible - and whatever you do, don’t make the mistake of attempting to sit in at a real (high-level) session with sheets or a screen. Cheers!

This is a hot topic. I have a split view on this, as it’s from a place of “grace” more than tradition. Firstly, I absolutely think that one should never rely on sheet music or tablets in a traditional setting if at all possible. A slow (learning) session would be a reasonable exception, one that would allow people to get up to speed on a tune so that all could play that set of tunes together well. I want to see people looking around and enjoying the craic. To me- tablets distract from that.

All that said, I have recently come to understand that although I am very blessed to be a quick “learn by ear” musician, many of my friends and acquaintances are not, and they are visual learners when it comes to music, and the dots allow them to be involved. I did a bit of research on this and apparently, there definitely is a spectrum of learning styles within music and it helps explain how I can “feel” where music or a tune is going, but someone such as my brother in law is unable- that is just different for him and I suspect that others really struggle with that in a way that until recently was impossible for me to appreciate. I am glad that dots exists to help people learn the music when needed.

Re: Reading music during a session

Whether it’s “tacky” or “permissible” depends on the session, but I would say it’s not a great way to enjoy yourself or to learn the tunes. I know it seems like it’ll be helpful to have the dots in front of you, but from my experience I’d say it’s a lot better to trust your ears and trust the other players, and risk making some mistakes. You’ll learn a lot more that way, and you’ll be a lot more at home with the other players. If you know two tunes well enough that you can get through them reliably at home, that’s enough to go down and introduce yourself to the players at your local. In general, folks will be happy to play your two tunes with you and more likely than not your biggest issue will be that they’ll overwhelm you with advice and encouragement.

Basic advice for your first time playing at a session:
- tell people what tunes you’re intending to play, since there are lots of tunes that sound similar to each other.
- start slow, and keep a steady rhythm - the pulse is much more important than the pace.
- if you’re nervous (it’s okay if you are) then think your way through the A part, in time, of the tune you’re going to start with, as a way to get your bearings.
- if you make a mistake, that’s okay, just find your place again and keep going

Re: Reading music during a session

I’m of the opinion that sheet music is a wonderful tool for discovering, learning, and archiving tunes .. but it has no place in a full-on session. (Learners events, beginners sessions, introduction to session skills, certain types of workshops, different rules apply).

One of the primary skills in full participation in sessions is playing by ear.

Maybe the person who starts a tune is playing a different version to the one on your phone? It’s their tune, their version, and if you’re ploughing on with your version you’re not listening to them, recognising the variant, and not fully participating.

If someone starts a tune, how are you going to find out what it’s called, to then search for the music, and only then be ready to join in just as the tune changes and you’re back at the start of the cycle?

Even if it’s an established set (eg Tune B is always played after Tune A), are you going to stop playing at the change, find the dots of the new tune, and only then be ready to jump back in?

And if your head is buried in your phone / tablet / piles of sheet music, you’re missing a lot of the looks and nods and winks and subtle grins and the whole person to person interaction of playing *together*, rather than playing (versions of) the same tune in parallel.

Learners events, beginners sessions, introduction to session skills, certain types of workshops, different rules apply. But if you’re in a genuine full-on session, for the sake of your own playing and everyone else’s, put the sheet music away!

(Full disclosure - I do have a small list of sets on my phone. But that’s purely the tune titles of sets I might fancy starting, but might not think of without that prompt in the heat of the moment.)

Re: Reading music during a session

Many of the comments in this thread talk about “memorizing” the tunes, but to my mind that’s not exactly how this music works, at least not in traditional sessions.

Instead, learning tunes is like going through a life experience and then retelling it as a story. This is easier than mere rote memorization, and it leaves the tune more malleable. So when you go to a session and someone else is telling the same story but in a slightly different way, you can still join in.

It’s less a matter of learning a prescribed series of notes and more about having this story in your head, and you know it’s overall plot and arc, and the key themes and pieces of it, so it unfolds naturally, rather than just being regurgitated.

This turns out to be far easier than rote memorization. And it evolves over time, like any good oral story.

For me, letting the tunes change and develop over the years I play them is one of the great joys of playing this music. Adhering to the dots would hinder that experience.

Being able to play without music is liberating and allows a richer participation in a session

However, I have also experienced a downside from some players who probably think they are playing ‘by ear’ but really are simply playing from memory.

By this I mean they have memorised a single version of a tune. So far so good. But the problem is that as soon as they think they have identified the tune being played, they simply play their memorised version, irrespective of what is actually being played by anybody else. They do not actually appear to be listening (i.e. actually playing ‘by ear’) simply reciting. It tends to be also that they play the tune exactly the same way all three times through with zero variation. These can also be the people who exhibit the greatest hostility to sheet music being produced at a session (even if quite often they have memorised their version at home from notation rather than the Holy Grail of having learnt it by listening)

The same difficulty can arise when more than one person is seeking to provide chords as accompaniment. One guitarist might be playing an almost incompatible set of chords and strumming patterns to another with both continuing regardless

So my conclusion is that whether you rely on notation or on memory (or both), the real skill to develop is being able to listen and respond to what is actually going on. It’s much greater fun to interact than to recite. Improvision is king. That’s the real ‘Jimi Hendrix’

A quick note on the idea that sheet music hurts “the sense of flexibility in the playing of tunes”: This is a perspective I’ve heard a great deal, and I personally disagree with it. It’s true that relying on sheet music CAN be correlated with inflexible playing. But this isn’t inevitable. If you use the sheet music as a rough guideline, it can allow you to pick up the gist of the tune faster. It doesn’t prevent you from adapting to how other people are playing it.

Placed in this light, I don’t see how using sheet music is fundamentally different from relying on memory, at least as far as “flexibility” is concerned. While it’s true that “what is written down in the sheet music may not exactly represent what is being played,” it’s equally true that what you REMEMBER by heart might not exactly represent what’s being played. If you remember a tune and decide to play along when you hear it, there’s always the possibility that you’ll misremember it or remember a different version of it than other people. If you lack a flexible mentality, what you play may end up clashing with what other people are playing. You have to adapt on the fly - the exact same way you need to if you’re using sheet music.

The point is, flexibility is a mindset, and it’s one you can have (or not have) regardless of whether you’re using sheet music or relying on memory.

“Looking up the music whether from a book or a phone may or not be distracting but it is not helpful for yourself in the long run”: This is another perspective I hear a lot, and it is both completely contrary to my personal experience and (in my opinion) unsupported. There is nothing magical about Irish trad that would make it less conducive to being learned with sheet music than any other type of music. Learning by ear vs. learning by sheet music isn’t fundamentally any different. In both cases, you’re learning something note-for-note the way you perceive it, then adding your own variations/embellishments once you know the tune by heart. As long as you’re not wedded to playing the tune exactly how you see it/hear it, you’re going to learn it just fine for ITM purposes.

I often tell naysayers that I’ve learned hundreds of tunes in a very short period of time using sheet music, and they typically respond “well, I’ve never read music in my life, and I know hundreds/thousands of tunes myself!” Well, yes, I’m sure you do, because you’ve been playing for 20+ years. I’ve been playing in sessions for only 4 years - if I’d tried to learn everything purely by ear at sessions, I wouldn’t know nearly as many tunes as I currently know. “If all the actors were reading their lines from a script, would that be as engaging an experience?”: Of course not. But I’d argue you’re making the wrong comparison here. You’re trying to compare “reading music off the sheet” vs. “having the music down by heart so solidly that you don’t mess up anything and all play it perfectly.” Obviously, between those two choices, the latter is preferable.

Also, FWIW, I use “the dots” not dismissively but as a term of affection. Not because I’m smitten with written notation, but because I find it remarkable that glancing at ink on a page or pixels on a screen can spark music in my brain. And I’ve learnt and played 99% by ear for nearly all my life.
