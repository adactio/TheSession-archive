Why Can’t We Just Play Some Tunes?

Why do people care about session rules? Why do they spend their days arguing on the Internet instead of practicing or playing? Isn’t this all about MUSIC and playing it?

Anyone who has stuck their head into either of the following threads knows that the age-old battle between old-school melody players and new-school accompanists is still raging.
https://thesession.org/discussions/7157#comment152915
or
https://thesession.org/discussions/7181

I mean no disrespect to anyone, I’m just very passionate about the role my instrument of choice plays in ITM. Very passionate indeed. And I understand that there are differing opinions, and not everyon’e view of an ITM session is the same. But to say that certain trains of though are “incorrect” or “inappropriate” in ITM is elitist, exclusive, and does not help to further this music that we all love and enjoy.

For me, ITM is the greatest expression of emotion in the world. It can be ecstatic and euphoric; it can be mournful and melancholy. And a session should be a place to channel these forces, not a clubhouse for soloists or a stage for performers. People should know if they’re good enough to play, and when they are ready, there should be no problem with everyone doing absolutely whatever. No hoops to jump through, no hype, just music. That’s what it’s all about, isn’t it?

So everybody, let’s just get all our feelings out on the table and hash this one out. I may fight like a pitbull on this site, but I’d be glad to have tune with anyone. This means you, High-Strung!

Hoping this will end this battle and clear my name as a rabble-rouser,

Sean Earnest
Camp Hill, PA

Re: Why Can’t We Just Play Some Tunes?

I don’t play ITM to follow or impose rules, but many things in life, including social music and dancing, have to have some rules, or let’s call them guidelines if you prefer. For instance, it’s clearly bad form for someone to get into the middle of a good session and wang away brutally on a loud guitar, especially if they don’t know the tune. It’s bad form to make someone uncomfortable, or to impose one’s view of ITM on others via a musical instrument. That’s not beautiful self-expression--it’s incredibly selfish and inconsiderate. It’s social music, and so requires a modicum of social graces and rules. (Maybe it’s a bit of a southern thing too--we American southerners tend to be a bit more polite than our Yankee friends.)

That doesn’t mean that you have to have a degree in music or ethnomusicology or have to sit off to the side for a six-month probationary period, but there has to be some kind of etiquette, or you have chaos. No one likes a martinet, least of all me, but etiquette exists for a reason. I’ve played sessions in Ireland as a visiting American, and I would have been thrown out of the pub if I’d behaved poorly. Because I asked to play my guitar, and played with as much consideration and sensitivity and taste as I could muster, I had a very good reception and a great time. A little humility goes a long way, particular when you’re traveling in foreign countries.

This is the last thing I’ll say:

I think melody players and accompanists are complementary pieces of the whole. Without the melody players, there would be no music to accompany, and without the acoompanists, the “session” would be little more than a recital with everyone playing the same thing. So we need each other. When a flute player does a sweet roll on a note I like, I get excited. When a fiddle player drags and saws through a rollicking tune, I get excited. It’s the same kind of excitement I get when I come up with a cool chord or interesting progression. I hope this feeling of excitement is reciprocated by my melody counterparts.

I feel, and justifiably so, that zook players, guitaritsts, mandola players, and all kinds of accompanists have not been given the credit that is due to them. I LOVE good melodies and melody players! Can we accompanists “get a little love”?

What a world it would be if melody players shared in the little glories of the backers. What we do isn’t “optional” or “unnecessary”. We do it for a reason, and that reason (for me at least) is the same love of traditional Irish music that flows from the fingers of a box player, that leaps like lightning from fiddlers’ strings, that pounds skin on a wooden frame; the same love that squeezes a bellows of the pipes and that jangles from a resonator. It is the love that swirls around you at a fine session that makes you forget who you are, where you are, what time it is; forget everything except the music.

I’ve said some things that were harsh and insensitive. I’ve implied things that I should not have. I’ve made claims that may or may not be true. I receive the same treatment. It’s obvious that I’m not the only one who loves this music with fiery, passionate intensity. Methinks we all do. And I think we all love the music so much that when we perceive an affront to our vision of the music, we lash out.

I can’t stand listening to a single instrument play for an extended period of time. Especially myself! It’s not complete. I need others, and others need me. I think that’s what sessions are all about. They are, essentially, places to forget the troubles of our world and our daily lives and to congregate among people of like interest and share in a communion of sorts; a collective masterpiece that shouts thunderous praise to the Music Gods. Let us not allow petty disagreement and ill will infiltrate and pervert this inner sanctum, this Final Redoubt of ours.

Apologies extended and gladly accepted,

Sean Earnest

Re: Why Can’t We Just Play Some Tunes?

As part of my evolution and exposure to ITM, I decided to take some fiddle lessons in order to learn how some of these tunes are structured, so it would make me a better guitar player. That was 14 years ago, and I’ve branched out into mandolin, bouzouki, and tenor banjo. (Maybe I’ll get back on the fiddle one day soon when my daughter gets a bit older.) As a result, I have a much better understanding of the tune structures, the scales, and how to be a better accompanist (e.g, playing modally, the use of drones, chord substitutions, etc.). I also know a handful of melodies on the guitar, and have a better sense of when not to play.

As in any serious study of traditional music, there’s often a lot more than meets the eye. Jack’s advice is good. Listen to your elders. We all do that to the musicians we know by their recordings. Find the best players, and watch them closely. John Doyle, for instance, is a really kind person, and is happy to share tips and advice. Robin Bullock is the same way, and most of the best players are. We’re fortunate to have a relatively easy session to join. There’s been some hurt feelings from a few in the past about etiquette issues, but a complete lack of etiquette would have driven away the better players years ago. It’s a long road, but a worthy one.

Re: Why Can’t We Just Play Some Tunes?

I found Zina Lee’s post very refreshing. No, really. And please read my whole post….this is as sincere as I get:

I’m not trying to force anyone over to my way of thinking. I have no hatred of older players and older albums; I just find a chronic lack of intensity in these older albums that I find in great abundance on any early Solas album, in the live playing of Lunasa, or in the arrangements and style of Dervish. I can listen to a slow, melancholy set of Martin Hayes w/ Dennis Cahill and still “feel the magic” because for me, accompaniment sets the mood.

I greatly enjoy hearing an old air, set piece, or march played by Martin Hayes and hear him tell where he got it from: Michael Coleman, Paddy Fahey, etc. I like old tunes, I just don’t care for the sound of solo performances. I think Martin & Dennis capture this concept perfectly: a tune can have its own character and can be very emotive, but good accompaniment makes it all the better. To my ears, accompanied music sounds better. This is clearly not the case for everyone, and this difference of opinion doesn’t mean I need to force others to play my way, and conversely, I don’t need to be forced to conform to others’ interpretations.

I was not, am not, and never will be angry at those who don’t think the same way I do. I call such individuals “conservatives” and there’s no way I’m getting labeled as such! The people I get angry at are those that feel that their way is the ONLY way. Like Irish music hasn’t changed at all over the years.

I would liken the situation to (and I know this is a controversial issue…) the issue of gay/lesbian marriage. I’m all for it, by the way. But going to a Catholic highschool and having a Catholic bishop for a cousin means I keep hearing the same thing over and over: “the institution of marriage has its own definition and cannot be changed to accomodate the union of homosexuals.” I ask, “why can’t marriage mean a general concept whose exact definitions are dependant on the individual?”

The same thing I think applies to sessions; many people believe that: “A session has its own definition and cannot be changed to accomodate the influx of new musical ideas, role equalization between melody players and accompanists, and fusion with other genres of music.” By “fusion” I don’t mean that I endorse electric guitars and synthesizers in sessions (although Capercaillie has a nice sound….), I simple mean that a samba rhthym or a bossa nova line never hurt anyone. A “jazz” chord substituted for one of the 3 main chords isn’t going to change the music altogether! Irish music is a subdivision of MUSIC, so there’s no harm in borrowing techniques and nuances from other genres to make musical statements.

I’m not out to ruin anyone’s session experience by playing in a lively fashion with a lively set. I could see how you’d be angry if I kept strumming away right through a slow piece, but if the set is lively, I am too! I’m not trying to create pandemonium by altering orthodox accompaniment styles. And as far as volume is concerned, I think even the loudest guitar has to compete to be heard at all. A guitar is an instrument too, and I’m not going to back off my attack to the point where you can barely hear the guitar or even to the point where the melody is drastically louder than the accompaniment. It’s hard to believe that some people are so instrument-biased that they can’t bear to hear a well-played guitar at or just below the volume of a fiddle or another melody instrument. It’s outrageous.

What I said about “not needing a mentor” was not meant to be twisted into “I’m so good that there’s no room for improvement.” Far from it. I pride myself in good accompaniment skills, but I’d never put myself on the same level as the greats: Zan, Arty, John Doyle, Ed Boyd, Randall Bayes, Steve Cooney, Daithi Sproule, Ciaran Curran, Donal Clancy, Manus Lunny, Donogh Hennessy, Seamus O‘Dowd, Niall O’Callanain, Donal & Andy, MIchael Holmes and Brian McDonagh, and all the rest. BTW, the idea for my screen name was conceived with purely admirational purposes in mind! The intended message of the quote was this: “I don’t need a mentor or years of further study to confirm what I already know: that I enjoy Irish traditional music played a certain way: with prominent and interesting accompaniment.” That’s not so bad, is it?

I admit I can be a stubborn 17 year old at times. About certain things. I know I’m stubborn about things that mean a lot to me. But you don’t have to be forty-something to have developed a musical taste! I’m very stubborn about everyone’s views being equally and accurately represented. I’m stubborn about fairness and when in the rare case I think a situation has one “right” course of action, I’ll stick by it. Luckily, this is not one of those cases! I’ll listen to anybody, but for God’s sake, I hate being spoken down to! I can honestly say I never bashed someone on this forum for having a differing opinion. I bashed people for having the opinion that other opintions are “wrong.”

I guess I’ll have a refresher course in session etiquette starting tomorrow at Augusta Irish Week. As has been predicted in earlier posts, I may receive a rude awakening. But I don’t really think that will happen. I’m too nice a person in real life to make others feel angry or intimidated!

Sean Earnest
Camp Hill, PA
