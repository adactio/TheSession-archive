Aha Laitch got in first. Here’s what I was writing while (s)he posted:

OK Zazz, fair enough. How on earth do you figure out chords on the guitar btw? You need to read up on a bit of music theory and harmony but here’s a crash course for you.

A triad is a three-note or full chord consisting of degrees 1,3 and 5 of the scale. The 3 can be major or minor. Playing a triad as a “broken chord” (one note after the other) gives you an arpeggio. Irish tunes are full of bits and pieces of arpeggios, so it’s useful to learn or figure the notes involved in the arpeggios of every common key at least.

The peculiarities of the halfstep box layout mean that you can only get a few triads with the right hand.

On your C row, you can easily get a full triad of C major. On the press, hold down three consecutive buttons starting with C as the lowest. Bingo - CEG or C-major triad. Play them one after the other - arpeggio of C major. Any consecutive three buttons on the press will give you different permutations or inversions of this chord. In fact any random (hehe) choice of more than one button on the press will give you some form of C chord. (An inversion means you have the same notes but not necessarily in the same order - for example, not having C as your lowest or root note.)

On the draw, hold down the same first three buttons of the scale and you get D-F-nat-A, or a triad of D minor. You can get lots of permutations inversions of this chord too but you have to be careful not to play any Bs in there.

It would be really useful to be able to get a triad of D major, but you are snafu’d by the fact that your F# on the other row is on the push. So you have to make do with - da-da! - two-note chords for D-major, using just D and A.

Using the same logic, on the B row you can get B major on the press (B-D#-F#) and C# minor (C#-E-G#) on the draw. Not the most useful triads for ITM but there. For a B-minor chord, which is useful, you can just miss out the D#.

Here endeth the first lesson. If you can get your head around this I’ll be happy to tell you a few more chords you can get. But I think you ought to learn all this stuff on the guitar first. Everything will seem a lot less random :-)

Steve

Re: B/C Right-Hand Chords

Write down the scale in the key you’re playing in:
GABCDEF#G or DEF#GABC#D. A third in that key is two notes of the scale that have one note between them, wrapping around when you get to the end. So GB and F#A are thirds in both keys, G and D. Some thirds are major and some are minor, but you don’t have to worry about that if the music is in one of these (diatonic) scales. AC is a third in the key of G but not in the key of D. AC# is a third in the key of D but not in the key of G. There are only seven thirds in any key.

When you place two thirds back to back you get a triad. GBD and ACE and EGB are some triads in the key of G. DF#A, EGB, and F#AC are some triads in the key of D. There are only seven triads in any key (and one of them sounds strange).

A triad without the middle note is an open fifth. (The fifth corresponding to the strange sounding triad is not really a fifth, it’s called an augmented fifth.)

For any triad and any two-row half-step accordion, at least two of the three notes are available using the same direction of the bellows. These two notes may be a third or they may be an open fifth. A few triads have all three notes available using the same bellows direction, but not many.

On the B/C, the only triads available in either key are EGB and AC#E. Other than that, you’ll have to settle for thirds and fifths. All of the available thirds involve either the note E or the note B, with the exception of AC#.

Re: B/C Right-Hand Chords

If it’s chords you want, here is a list of some of the chords that are possible on a B/C keyboard.

On the push:

B major (BD#F#)
B sus4 (BEF#)
C major (CEG)
C maj7 (CEGB)
E minor (EGB)
Em+7 (EGBD#)
G augmented (GBD#)

On the pull:

A major (A C# E)
A maj7 (A C# E G#)
A sus4 (A D F)
Bb major (Bb D F)*
Bb7 (Bb D F Ab)*
B diminished (B D F)
B dim7 (B D F Ab)
C# major (C# E# G#)*
C#6 (C# E# G# A#)*
C#7 (C# E# G# B)*
C# minor (C# E G#)
C#m6 (C# E# G# A#)*
C#m7 (C# E# G# B)*
D minor (D F A)
Dm6 (D F A B)
Dm +7 (D F A C#)
E major (E G# B)
E6 (E G# B C#)
E7 (E G# B D)
F augmented (F A C#)
G# diminished (G# B D)
G# dim7 (G# B D F)

* E# is the same as F, it just makes more sense in the context of the chord to call it E#. The same applies to A#/Bb.

There are more possibilities - some of which I wouldn’t know the names of. Personally, I can’t imagine many of the above chords being of any use in traditional music. However, there are always those musicians who seem to be able to make the most unlikely chords make perfect sense - for all I know, Zazz, you could be one of the gifted few. Also, bear in mind that I am not a box player, I just have a rudimentary knowledge of the layout of a button accordion keyboard. All these chords are *possible*, but I don’t know how practical they are, as regards reaching all the keys at once - they are just derived theoretically from the notes available..

Bringing in the basses opens up other possibilities as well - for example, adding a G bassnote to a B diminished chord gives a G7. Partial chords, as explained by Steve and Gary, are probably far more useful than any of the chords I have listed above.

Re: B/C Right-Hand Chords

Gary,

You imply that B/C boxes are only ever played in keys with one or two sharps. But people do play tunes in C major (often but not always D tunes transposed down) and D minor, so you have the full tonic chords in those keys.

F is a pretty easy key on the B/C too, giving you the chance to use, theoretically, not only a full C major chord but B-flat major too. (B-flat minor is also available…)

On a C#/D the available triads are probably more useful and (D major, E minor and C major in particular).

Culchie, I think just about every box player uses r-h chords, at least partial ones, to some extent. Often for percussive effect, like a dab on the regulators, or to give a little off-beat pulse in the middle of a long note. And of course on the last note of a tune.

It may be that I don’t listen to enough B/C players but I suspect this kind of thing is more prevalent among people playing on a D-row (C#D or one-row players), just because it falls more naturally under the hand.

Reinforcing a note with a note an octave above or below is also very effective and very common, and even playing entire passages in octaves, particularly in (yes) waltzes and the like.

One player who uses partial chords for the off-beat pulse thing to deadly effect is Andrew Mac Namara. But he’s not the only one.
