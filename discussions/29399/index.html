That sounds about right, although not necessarily in that order of frequency. B minor (aeolian) is also not uncommon, usually modulating to (or from) D major (ionian).

There is a certain amount of regional variation within Ireland: A major is more common in the northern counties, whilst you are more likely to hear tunes in C, F, D dorian, G dorian and G mixolydian in the E. Clare/E. Galway area.

Re: What are the most common modes?

“However, I happen to be the worst ear player I can think of…so I’m looking for any way I can to improve”

I’m not convinced that your approach is going to be effective. Go ahead and give it a try if you think it’s going to work for you, but thinking in terms of modes and such is simply not the way the tunes think. Some of them do mimic the modal thought, but you’re going to be hopelessly lost on the more interesting ones, and you’re going to lock yourself into boring stereotyped accompaniment on the uninteresting ones.

If you want to work on your ear, there are a few things you could do. One would be to find someone else who’s in the same situation and sit back to back and play follow-my-leader. You play a note, they match it. They play a note, you match it. When you can do that, try with two notes. When you can hear a note and interval, try more complicated phrases.

Another thing you could do would be to play a ground note and try to sing a particular note above it. Play a low D and see if you can sing a good B or c above it. It sounds like you’re a guitarist, so you should be able to check yourself easily on this. If you can produce a note given a particular drone, you should be able to recognize that note against that drone - whether the drone is there or not. That is, if you can hear an implied fundamental, you should be able to hear where the tune is.

“Never quite sure of what the tonal center (key) is on a tune I’m not acquainted with and before I can figure it out the group has moved on to another tune in the set”

This is a problem. Lilting the tunes often helps me find their center. Try that.

Re: What are the most common modes?

Patrick, it’s all pretty simple. You know how we have the keys of C Major and A Minor? Well, those are modes of the key of C. The thing about a mode, is that you play in the notation of a certain but start and end on a different note. An example, A minor. Has all the same notes of C Major, but has a different feel because the note starts and end on A instead of C. Because of this all of the notes aurally relate differently to each other.

Re: What are the most common modes?

accidental is a bad name for it no matter what music you are talking about. C.P.P composers mostly use them to modulate between keys in ways that are very deliberate. In Traditional music it is less often used for a full scale modulation, but when you see them they usually follow the order of circle of fifths.

I mean you are not likely to see a G# accidental unless you are already in a key with at least C# or C# is also accidentally noted.

when you see a G# accidental there are a couple of possibilities. Either the G note had not been previously used in the tune, (if it’s hexatonic) in which case it would probably be best to write it in the key signature of both parts anyway, so it’s not so much an accidental but poor notation.

Then there is the case where G natural had been used previously in the tune. Some of these cases use G natural at some point in one part; at a different point in the part shift to G# in either one or multiple instances.

When a tune shifts to one certain accidental for multiple instances, if even for a small bit of one part, I would consider it modulation, whether the composer/performer realizes it or not.

Other tunes use strictly G natural in one part and G# in another part. I think the latter case is best thought of as a modulation, and the accidental should be written into the key signature. The change in signature should be at the end of the line before the change occurs for clarity.

Re: What are the most common modes?

In the classical musical world, a change of mode is not what’s normally meant by a modulation. When you modulate, you are changing tonal centre. But, in any case, there’s no change of mode in the first part of Beare Island. That early G# is just an inflected note. It’s the way modes work - there has always been the possiblility of ‘inflection’, which is the moving up or down of a note by a semitone. Not just any note, mind. But certain notes can move. It’s not a change of mode when that happens with just one, inflected, note.

Re: What are the most common modes?

Chinese music theory has a name for those unobtrusive gap-filling notes - “pien”. Kodaly adopted the word for describing Hungarian basically-pentatonic folk tunes, A.L. Lloyd got it from him for describing English music, and it’s now commonly used by ethnomusicologists for all kinds of music.

Those mixolydian sevenths coming out of nowhere could be labelled in several different ways. I often prefer to see them as a modulation from a gapped mode to one that fills the gap, but I don’t think that’s standard terminology. (One of the neatest examples I know is the epithalamium for Eric of Orkney and Margaret of Norway, in my modes tutorial - that goes from dorian/aeolian hexatonic to dorian).

Re: What are the most common modes?

Breandan Breathnach, who knew a thing or two about Irish music, gives the following percentages:

Major (Ionian) over 60%
Mixolydian 15%
Dorian (minor but with a raised 6th) over 10%
Minor (Aeolian) “the least numerous”

He states further

“It is of interest to note that English folk music, by and large, falls into these same four divisions and the proportions of airs in each division is suprisingly close to the Irish figures.”

"The scales or modes referred to above are Heptatonic, that is, having seven notes within the span of the octave. Two other systems of scales are of importance in Irish music:

Pentatonic (having five notes) the first Pentatonic scale corresponds with Major except that it lacks a fourth and seventh.

Hexatonic (having six notes) corresponds with Major except that it lacks a seventh.

One hears from time to time, admittedly in vague terms, that pentatonic melodies are a marked feature of Gaelic (Irish and Scots) music. An examination of the Irish material does not confirm these assertions, as such melodies constitute only a relatively small proportion of the national repertory."

If Richard was quoting the same passage of Breathnach that I read (and it seems the same, I read it some 20 years ago), he didn’t actually give any evidence there. There must have been some genuine calculation involved, but I don’t recall any pointers to the data set so we could check how he figured that.

Just had a look at a couple of examples I happened to have windows open for anyway, two different “Toss the Feathers”:

https://thesession.org/tunes/113
https://thesession.org/tunes/138

The first one would be labelled as E minor if you ignored the issue of pien notes. In fact it’s pentatonic - the c’s and the g only occur in second-quaver-of-a-beat position, and if you just held the note preceding them, you would not lose anything essential to the tune. (Means you could play it on either a G or an A moothie with position shifts, or on a harp tuned to one to three sharps with no lever flips). So which way would Breathnach have counted that? I’ve no idea, he didn’t say, and I don’t think Richard Cook knows either. But the fact that Breathnach doesn’t mention the pien issue suggests to me that he would have counted it as E minor.

The other “Toss the Feathers” is a good example of modal modulation - mixolydian into major and back. It’s a lovely dramatic effect.

Re: What are the most common modes?

I recently did an analysis on this. Breathnach is about right, or at least his numbers roughly match what I get out of the tunes on TheSession.org

Most tunes are major (~65%), then Dorian, then Aeolian (Minor), then Mixolydian. I didn’t control for tunes with multiple settings, though, so the answer you get depends on the data used.

Anyway, for those who want to know more, see page 8 (Figure 3) here:
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.12596

One issue with the analysis here is that it uses the key (G, D, etc.) provided by the person who deposited the tune. This website doesn’t allow users to put down any key - it’s limited to the most common ones - so an F# tune like Farewell to Whalley Range is never labelled F# unfortunately.
