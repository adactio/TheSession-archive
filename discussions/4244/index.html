Re: Classical Composers Stole Our Tunes

It was general practice in those good ol’ days, before the copyright suits got in on the act, for composers to “borrow” each other’s tunes (or even their own if they’d forgotten they’d wrote it). It was, of course, what they did with the tunes that really mattered, just as two authors may take the same basic plot (didn’t someone once say that there are only 7 possible plots? anyway, I don’t believe it) and one writes a real cracker with film and tv rights and spin-offs, and the other author’s book gets pulped at the end of the season.

As far back as music has been composed, “serious” composers have gone to folk-music, if not to lift the tunes note-for-note but for inspiration. Beethoven and Dvorak did it. More recently, Bartok, Kodaly and Ralph Vaughan Williams (as a small modern sample) based a large part of their output on folk-music. I shouldn’t be surprised if some Gregorian chant and some of the later Renaissance church music were found to be based on popular songs of the day.

I’ve got no problem with “serious” composers getting inspiration from folk-music; the music of the Western world be a lot poorer otherwise.

Trevor

Re: Classical Composers Stole Our Tunes

Many classical composers used folk tunes, and sometimes adapted “folk-like” melodies into their works. A few examples:

Beethoven - Sym No 6, 3rd Mvt
Mahler - Sym No 1, 3rd Mvt
Tchaikovsky - Capriccio Italien
Tchaikovsky - Mache Slav
Dvorak - “New World” Symphony
Mendelssohn - Hebrides Overture
Manuel de Falla - Three Cornered Hat Suite
Copland - Appalachian Spring; Lincoln Portrait
Greig - Peer Gynt Suite
Sibelius - Finlandia
Ives - Three Places in New England; Central Park in the Dark
Bartok - Mikrokosmos
Brahms - Hungarian Rhapsodies
Lizst - Hungarian Rhapsodies
Grainger - Irish Tune from County Derry

There has sometimes occurred a sort-of reverse effect in that music written by a classical composer has found its way into a populist mainstream to where it might be considered a sort-of folk music. Examples of this:

Verdi - “Va, pensiero” (Chrous of the Hebrew Slaves from the opera Nabucco)
Holst - Andante theme in Jupiter from The Planets

Examples of composers “borrowing” from their own music include:

JS Bach - Mass in B-Minor, various mvts
Mozart - Oboe Concerto, copied almost note for note from his Flute Concerto
Copland - “Fanfare for the Common Man” lifted from the Finale of his 3rd Symphony
Barber - “Adagio for Strings” lifted from his String Quartet, Opus 11

Re: Classical Composers Stole Our Tunes

Of course Classical composers used folk tunes, but to talk about stealing them is to misunderstand the process, which was a 2-way one. Baroque composers wrote suites of elaborated dance tunes, they had to come from somewhere. Playford found tunes from the same sources and transcribed them. For some reason, people talk about ‘The Hole In The Wall’, as notated in Playford, as being a ‘traditional setting’, but the same tune as orchestrated by Purcell in, I think, the Abdelazer Suite (It might be somewhere else!), as being some sort of Classical corruption. The fact is, they are both interpretations of a common tune. Listen to much Purcell, and you’ll hear lots of half familiar hornpipes and jigs. Bach doesn’t sound so familiar because the tunes were German. It’s only in the twentieth Century that the lifting of ‘folk tunes’ by the likes of Delius, Grainger, Vaughan Williams, Smetana, Bartok and Janecek became a self trumpeted conceit, as though Composers were somehow giving the people’s music a boost by inflating it into orchestral works. And in the opposite direction, if you look at Michael Turner’s Sussex Tunebook (recreated by Martin Carthy), a, collection of dance tunes noted down by a musician in the early nineteenth century; you’ll find lots of tunes from European sources, some ‘classical’ mixed up with ’traditional’ tunes, with no regard for provenance - just tunes, on their way into the common repertoire! The effect of the cultural mixing caused by the Napoleonic wars foreshadows the effect of emigration and mechanical music reproduction on Irish regional music.
Also, ‘people’s music’ and Classical music have of course been influenced by the same popular fashions, with ‘moorish’ and ‘Turkish’ themes and motifs, for example, evident in both early classical music and many English folk tunes.
I think it was Geminiani who was the biggest influence on O‘Carolan - him being in Ireland at the time, though I’m sure O’Carolan would have heard stuff by Handel….

Re: Classical Composers Stole Our Tunes

> Mozart - Oboe Concerto, copied almost note for note from his Flute Concerto

Err…other way round. Sometime post-war it was discovered that the Oboe concerto was written first. Later on Mozart was commissioned to write a Flute concerto and, being short of time, resorted to the expedient of re-badging the Oboe concerto. Perhaps not surprising, given his coolness towards the flute as an instrument.

There are plenty of other such instances of composers making more money off their works by rearranging them (e.g. Beethoven Op.1 C minor Piano Trio --> String Quintet); or indeed allowing others to do so (e.g. Joachim’s conversion of the Brahms Clarinet Sonatas into Viola Sonatas; Tertis doing something similar to the Elgar Cello Concerto).

And then there are those instances where one composer borrows another’s theme for the purpose of mockery or parody. Examples would include Mahler’s (ab)use of the famous theme from Brahms‘ 1st symphony in his own third. Or Bartok making plain his disdain for Shostakovich’s Invasion theme in the latter’s ’Leningrad’ symphony, in the form of huge raspberries from the Brass section in the Concerto for Orchestra.

Re: Classical Composers Stole Our Tunes

Ottery (and Jim), rest assured; I don’t think we’re going to run out of tunes any time soon.

Putting on my geek’s hat, if we imagine a very simple short tune consisting of 8 measures with just 4 notes in each measure (making a total of 32 notes in the tune), and we restrict the choice of pitches to just 8 (i.e. the notes of a single octave scale or mode) then the total number of “tunes” that can be written with this limited choice is about 8E28 (in scientific notation), or 8 with 28 zeroes after it. It would take rather longer than the lifetime of the universe to play them all, even by Frankie Gavin in a hurry :)

If we then re-do the calculation for a typical 16-measure tune with 8 notes in each measure (a reel), and allow, say, a choice of notes from 2 octaves, then the number of possible “tunes” shoots up to about 3E150 (3 with 150 zeroes).

And I haven’t even considered variations in rhythm, changes of key or mode, or ornaments.

Even if 99.99999999% of these “tunes” are utter rubbish, as they surely will be, I reckon there will still be enough “tunes” to keep us going for at least a few millenia, or even until the Sun goes nova.

“Shepherd’s Hey is a one-movement work with a performance time of two and one-quarter minutes.  The work includes parts for the standard wind ensemble.  The percussion in written with the xylophone (a recent, intriguing addition to the wind ensemble that was explored during the piece), bells, and timpani as the most prominently featured.  There are several cues and many doublings of parts.  Nevertheless, competent soloists are needed on alto sax, bassoon, oboe, and trumpet.  Strong tubas, euphoniums, and trombones are also required.  Technically, the piece is somewhat demanding of the players, particularly toward the end during the accelerando.  Musically the players have to have a good sense of beat because of the separated, quick style.  They also need a good understanding of shape and how their instrument fits into the overall sound of the group (the pyramid shape with tubas on the bottom and piccolo on the top).”

Come all ye Morris-ons and Morris-ettes! There must be someone out there willing to defend the the honour of this magnificent specimen of English tunesmanship that Dow and I constantly fling ordure at…..
Come on then!

If anyone wants a flavour of the authentic Milton Babbit here’s what the great man himself says in part in his program notes for a performance of his “Dual”, a piece for cello and piano.

“The title of this one-movement work is intended to intimate, well beyond the peripheral pertinence of its obvious homophone (in the sense of ‘dueling banjos‘ and the more gentlemanly, if sanguinary, art) and of the traditional ’duet‘ and ’duo,’ the central and pervasive musical expressions of a duality relation, interpreted variously inter- and intradimensionally….[I]t is just the progression from the local to the global in relational implications which should provide the listener with the means of achieving that cognition of cumulative containment and successive subsumption which human memory in general, and musical memory in particular, requires for a musical work to be entified, eventually, as a unified, closed totality -- as an all of a piece of music.”

So now we know how to review submitted tunes and recordings.

I doubt if I’d care for Grainger’s setting of “Shepherds Hey”, but it does indicate something about southern English TM - all manner of instruments can be used to play it, including oboes, xylophones and brass that don’t (normally) do ITM. Good English TM bands are making effective use of this space for variety.

Vaughan Williams did a lovely extended piece based on “Dives and Lazarus”, aka “The Star of the County Down”; his probably more famous piece “The Lark Ascending”, which I like even more, isn’t based to my knowledge on any particular folk tune but seems to have come out of the
feeling he had acquired for them, and taken a quite original form.

(An English composer, early c20, I forget who, did a setting of the English folk-song “Brigg Fair” which is good enough to deserve mention. )
