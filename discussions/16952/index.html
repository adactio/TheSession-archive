Cast off those opressive sheets of music

What is the difference between playing by ear,
playing from memory, & sight-reading?

Here is an analogy I thought of today while I was out walking.
This past week it seems I have been driving more than walking or biking. Finally I removed myself from the old Toyota. It was a bit frightening at 1st. Just a change of pace really. But when you walk everyone is right there beside you & you can look each other in the eye.
After the 1st block or so I found a comfortable stride & was happy to be out of the vehicle.
So that is it ~ the analogy.

Reading music is like driving .. .
You just follow the signs. You can always keep a handy map in the glovebox.
& you should be able to get places more efficiently.

Walking is like playing by ear . . .
You are free to hop, skip, dance, run, or walk. You can really see the other people who are out walking. And you can look a person right in the eye.
So if you cannot give up the car at least give up the sheet.
Cheers!

Re: Cast off those opressive sheets of music

I think that it might be worth clarifying the difference between playing by ear and learning be ear. For example , there is nothing stranger than to see someone arrive into a session, whip out sheet music and start to play from it. I personally do not think that it is possible or ideal to play this music in that fashion.

However learning by ear whilst ideal for “developing your ear”, is not neccessary in order that you become a good trad player.

If it was true that using dots or ABCs somehow hindered or impeded your progress and ability to play this music well, then the large percentage of trad players in Ireland are doomed because that is how they learnt, learn and were and are taught. And as you can see on the Comhaltas site and hear all around Ireland, these players that have and are being taught by dots/ABCs are doing just fine thank you and indeed the very players who learnt by dots/ABCs are now passing on the tradition just fine !

However, it is also worth mentioning that by and large dots and more relevantly ABCs( and when I say ABCs I mean the system used in Ireland years before the computer version) are not used so much to give precise guidance as to beat length, rests , strength of note etc as they would in classical music but more so just to give the bare notes and it after this part of the learning that the player puts his/her stamp on the tune by lengthening or shortening notes as he/she sees fit.
Many a tune was passed on the back of a cigarette pack and sometimes by the time you got home, the packet was bent out of all shape in your pocket but it was still enough to learn the tune.

So whilst I don’t think there is any doubt of how futile it is to be continually playing this music only from dots and indeed ABCs, it is important to define exactly was is meant by “learning from sheet music” because for years this music has been learnt in lots of different ways in Ireland and there is no evidence that it has harmed the players today?

Re: Cast off those opressive sheets of music

I’m going to venture to share my own experience as a beginner, which maybe will help those who are hesitating to learn to pick up tunes by ear because they’re worried it will be too slow or too frustrating:

I’ve only been playing traditional music for about 4 months now, coming from a classical background. I started picking up tunes using the dots, but forced myself to memorize tunes as quickly as possible (it was like pulling teeth, but eventually it would stick), and never played from sheet music at a session. I also listened incessantly to recordings of the tunes, trying to get them in my head. When not listening to music I would attempt to hum the tunes out loud (amazing how difficult I found that).

I noticed after a month or two that it took fewer and fewer times reading through the dots before the tunes stuck, and that it got much easier to hum the tunes - and that this humming was instrumental to learning them. I discovered a decent sense of relative pitch, and that I was starting to pick up tunes by ear from recordings and in sessions, almost without trying. While before I would have had trouble playing “Happy Birthday” without the dots, I now can play just about anything that I can hum. These newly developed abilities has really increased my enjoyment of this music (and probably my playing level as well). I still find sheet music useful for nailing down tricky parts, or for hearing tunes that I don’t have recordings of.

At this point I still have a long ways to go before I’m learning by ear at the level of some of the posters above, and it’s entirely possible that I would be much better at this point had I given up sheet music entirely from the get-go. But hopefully my example illustrates that it is possible to quickly develop an ability to play by ear, even without giving up sheet music entirely.

Re: Cast off those opressive sheets of music

Firstly, you cannot learn this music from the dots. There is universal agreement here for this and for that I am truly thankful.

Secondly, anyone who thinks they can learn a tune from the dots is tragically deluded. The dots carry only a miniscule of the information that is a diddley tune. A recording is better, but still woefully inadequate. Even a very good recording by a good player is only three times through on a particular day in a particular mood. To know a tune is to have many many years of playing and listening to it, and understanding and knowing its relations.

Thirdly, this diddley music has a lot of notes in it, most of which are not represented in your average dots. Your average dots are often described as being the skeleton of the tune that the musician can then put the flesh onto. I profoundly disagree with this analogy, I think it’s much more accurate to take a more holistic view where every bit of the sound is equally important and flexible. However, I think it’s reasonably straight forward that this, so called, skeleton bit is the easy bit, compared to the twiddly diddley bits, not to mention the so called variations. So the killer point is, if you have trouble getting the easy bit by ear alone, you haven’t a feckin cat in hell’s chance of getting the rest of it.

Re: Cast off those opressive sheets of music

Music is a language, and you can’t learn how to speak a language properly by reading it. You can speak a language properly by reading words off a page, once you are fluent in the language. The great danger is reading without knowing how the language is supposed to sound, or all of its nuances, etc. The only solution when suffering from a lack of knowing “how it is supposed to sound” is total immersion, just like learning a language. Same rules apply.

But the words and the dots are just words and dots.

“I stubbed my toe and boy, does it hurt!” Imagine someone, a non-native speaker, who was learning English reading that like a robot: “I…stubbed…my…toe…and…boy…uh…does…it…hurt?”

We’re all English speakers for the most part, so all of us read it, know how to speak English properly, and see the exclamation point, so we all read it as “I STUBBED MY TOE AND BOY, DOES IT HURT!!!” with the proper inflection, tone, pauses, anger, frustration, etc.

I’ll admit it. I go to the dots sometimes, and all it does for me is tell me where to put my fingers when I’m too daft or lazy to properly figure it out. I’ll play it through, all foreign speaker robot style, once or twice, and then I can take it and make it a real tune, and have it happily jive with what’s in my head from a friend playing it or a recording, etc. I could probably do it without the dots helping my brain figure out what sound/finger goes where, but you know what? F it. I’m lazy. I’m getting old. I’m a busy guy. I’m a single Dad with two kids, I have to go to work and make money, and then post nonsensical things on the internet while I’m working during my lunch break, like right now, etc. etc.

I think I’ve been hamstrung as a child with intensive classical music training and all its dot-dependencies, but I’m curing myself slowly.

Moral of the story? Immerse thyself. You can’t speak a language properly without immersing yourself in it, and music is a language, so…

Re: Cast off those opressive sheets of music

These discussions about dots always come back to a few key points. Namely:

- You can learn tunes from the dots only if you already know what this music is supposed to sound like because you’ve listened to it (a lot).

- For someone who already knows what the music is supposed to sound like, the dots are a useful memory aid and a simple way to share a tune or two with another person who already knows what the music is supposed to sound like.

- Trying to learn this music from the dots alone, without listening to actual people playing this music, would be like trying to learn to pronounce and speak Chinese from the written symbols without ever hearing it spoken.

- Once you know how this music works and sounds, through aural immersion in it, the dots can be useful. With practice, you can learn to “sight hear” from sheet music or abc notation, so the ink on the page becomes yet another form of aural transmission of tunes. But this requires not just a fluency with the notation, but more importantly a high level of intimacy with the music.

Re: Cast off those opressive sheets of music

Of course Ailin’s language isn’t as careful as I’d aim for.

I’d argue that someone could indeed learn the lines of a play (at least the role of their character) by listening. It was once common for storytellers in many cultures to learn and recite whole odes and sagas aurally, with no written source. Our motivation (and perhaps our knack) for doing this atrophies when we have ink and paper at hand.

Another obvious difference between learning, say, all of Hamlet’s lines versus learning a bunch of 32-bar tunes is that the tunes are distinct, discrete wholes unto themselves. In other words, the tunes are short, and don’t have a fixed sequence or semantic relationship between them. They can be learned piecemeal, and even played back that way. But for the play to work, Hamlet’s lines must run from front to back--they can’t be randomly shuffled and still make sense. The narrative nature of the play may actually help someone trying to memorize the lines, but a written reference is clearly a significant help in learning the play.

Because tunes are short and discrete, a written reference (though perhaps helpful to some) is extraneous.

Re: Cast off those opressive sheets of music

Here’s my two cents’ worth!

-Music is organised sound.

-Sounds are perceived by the ears - hearing the tune.
These perceptions are stored in the brain as *aural* patterns. - remembering the tune as sound.
-The patterns are reproduced by one’s instrument -playing the tune.

- Musical notation or ‘the dots’ are symbols on paper perceived by the eyes - reading the tune.
- the symbols are stored in the brain as ’visual* patterns - seeing the tune as images.

- The patterns are translated from visual to aural in the brain and reproduced by one’s instrument - playing the tune.

- The translation process is an extra step - good for fine tuning details but unecessary work for the most part.

-Learning by ear = No need to translate!

JNW, that’s the problem with the way too many people use the dots--they “sight read” (a bit redundant, eh?). A much more effective way to use the dots is to “sight hear” them--to allow the ink spots to be sounds in your mind’s ear, so they become aural patterns, not visual ones. You can skip the visual storage--so no “translation” is needed.

When I learn a tune from the page, I don’t memorize the visual dots, just the aural pitches, and I can do this with or without a fiddle in my hands.

Many singers a quite adept at this, but it seems a shame not as many instrumentalists master this skill. The easiest way to do it is to lilt tunes from the dots.

Re: Cast off those opressive sheets of music

My head’s still sore from reading what concertinaplayer said above: “Yes, you do learn how to speak by listening but you learn whats on the page by reading it.”

This relates to comparisons people here have been making with tunes and plays. I don’t think tunes and plays written on the page compare very well at all. Yes, if an actor wants to learn his/her lines, they read the script, and then on the day they can use their acting skills to make it all sound right. The difference is that with tunes, there’d be more variation or ad-libbing. The general structure and meaning of the lines in the script would be conveyed, but possibly through different words altogether.

So rather than a “play” analogy, I’d like to suggest that tunes are more like an oral tradition of story-telling, like you get in indigenous communities in America and Australia. Story-telling like this is an art. You can’t learn how to tell these stories from what’s written in a book. They really have to be brought alive. The story-teller has to remember what happens in the story, and then somehow communicate the whole thing in an interesting and engaging way. That is what a muso has to do with tunes. Some of these stories have been published in books of course, and sometimes you see animated versions on the TV. You could learn a story off by heart from a book or the TV if you wanted, and maybe add in all the emotion and appropriate intonation, and then you could tell it to your kids and make it sound almost exactly like what you read or saw on the TV. But you couldn’t call yourself a “story-teller”.

Re: Cast off those opressive sheets of music

The cook/recipe analogy was mentioned above too. This is the same thing: a cook/chef knows recipes in their head, and has been cooking for long enough to know the specific properties of each ingredient and what they add to a dish in terms of flavour. You can make delicious food using a good recipe book, and you’ll impress your friends at dinner parties, but you can’t call yourself a “cook” or a “chef”. You’re just someone who’s good at reading and following instructions. This is what annoys me about a lot of younger players today who learn how to play their Lunasa tunes very fast and work up their technique so that they make them sound decent, but then you test them with a few session standards and all you get is a blank stare. You want to say to them, “go away and do your homework, and then come back and make that Lunasa tune your own, if you still find yourself wanting to play it”.

A good cook, on the other hand, will have made thousands of dishes and experimented with each of them over the years until they’ve got them exactly how they want them. These are the people who come up with things like “you might want to add a little bit of nutmeg at this stage” or “you don’t need to bother sieving the flour - it’s a waste of time with this dish”. This isn’t because the recipe told them so, it’s because they’ve experimented with the flavours of cinnamon and cloves and nutmeg each time they’ve made the dish, and found nutmeg to add a flavour that they like.

So there are no short cuts to getting tunes, just like there are no shortcuts for getting stories or good dishes. You have to put in years of playing, and you have to keep playing each tune or it never evolves. I have tunes I “learnt” years ago off the dots, and with a bit of prompting, I can still pick out which order the notes come in, and probably play along in a session, but I can’t say I “know” the tune. If I try to join in the tune in a session, it’s not “music”, what I’m playing, it’s just me putting my fingers in the right places, which is pointless. If it’s not music, why bother? You might just as well get everyone in the session to print out a whole load of sheetmusic at home before they come out, and then put it all in the middle of the table and compare dots.

Another thing to consider here is that visual cues may be extremely important for your friend. So sheet music feeds her comfort level with a tune.

I know the feeling--I’m strongly visual, so much so that I can see in great detail and accuracy pages from a manuscript I’m writing or editing. (An aside, just to illustrate how visual a person can be: Once, when working on a 400 page report for a government agency, I was asked about a specific paragraph. Without opening the document, I recited the passage and mentioned that it was on a left-hand page, page 218 to be exact, and it was the second paragraph from the top. The person who had challenged me on the wording then flipped to the page and stared at me like I was a clump of moss that had just sung Jumpin Jack Flash).

Even with music, I can usually bring up a detailed and accurate visual image of the sheet music or abc of a tune I’m playing. I’ve used this on occasion, when my aural memory of a tune is fuzzy, to “see” a phrase ahead of time to help me recall it and play it instead of trainwrecking.

But that’s a really odd experience, even for me. It feels like the picture is intruding on the aural stream of consciousness. I prefer to wallow in the sound. It took me some years to learn to pull the drapes on the visual side and let the aural side fill the space in my head. For me, it was a bit like sitting in a movie theater and learning to darken the screen to black while bringing up the volume on the audio track.

Playing music is *all* about the audio, and even strongly visiual or tactile people can learn to let sounds take over.

“The Listening Book” by W.A. Mathieu (Shambhala Press, 1991) has some great ideas about how to infuse your mind with sound and really *listen.* It’s good practice for anyone with ears and functioning cochlea, especially if they habitually favor other senses.
