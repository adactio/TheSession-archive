↳ Posted by Greg the Piano Tuner on June 1st, 2008.

Re: Making fingering charts, am I correct?

I don’t want this thread to descend to a p*ssing contest either. The thing that worries me, though, is that LasseNC says “[I] thought I’d make life a bit easier for myself by making fingering charts”. The fact is, doing that does not make it easier to play the music. Using a fingering chart as a guide to get the basics is one thing, but LasseNC is using it against sheetmusic in order to learn a tune. To learn a tune, you have to use your ears, because the whole point of the tune is the rhythm of it. If you use finger charts to learn a tune, there’s a danger that you’ll get too accustomed to doing things that way, and things like swing and accent in the rhythm, good roll articulation, tonguing, breathing, phrasing, variations in the melody, and all those other things will always be a mystery to you. Learn by using your ears from the outset, and your brain will learn to connect a particular fingering with a certain sound, and particular fingering combinations will connect with common melodic units, and it all becomes automatic as it should be.

Re: Making fingering charts, am I correct?

Greg, it seems (to me anyway) that your charts are efficient for learning the whistle, but difficult for learning tunes.
I always think there is a difference between the two “jobs” to be done - learning the instrument and learning the tune(s).

I suppose the ultimate goal is that when one hears a note that you can then very quickly, almost immediately reproduce that note on your instrument by an unconscious knowledge of the position required on the instrument. (You don’t have to think “oh, that’s a D, now where is that on the instrument” you just go there.) Thence to a series of notes (i.e. a tune), and you can reproduce that with some practice without resorting to the dots. That’s what I think the ‘learn by ear’ people are saying.
But it is a high level skill and takes a long time.

Being a teacher myself (of machine shorthand - a ‘word instrument’ I think of it), I can say that all students of that skill want a finger chart in the early stages of learning the keyboard. They think they will never learn the machine without that chart.
Within weeks of thorough practice though, the students don’t want to know about the chart, regarding it as a hindrance, because it is an extra step in the process they are trying to achieve. Once they become conversant with the keyboard they then can apply that knowledge to what they hear and reproduce it in turn on the keyboard. There are no visuals involved, importantly. If the students were to become reliant on visuals that would seriously hamper their skill development later.
Not a direct analogy I know, but maybe helpful.

Having said that, I learned the dots for ITM, but find that while handy, learning a tune that way seems to me to rely more on visual memory and therefore puts an extra step in the process which slows you down when trying to play that tune at a session.

As with most music teaching though, the convention apparently is that one learns from the charts and from the dots.
With ITM though, if you want to play at advanced session speed, it could be a problem in the long run, but for beginning students it would certainly, I think, build confidence and enthusiasm on the basis of early success rather than frustration and failure.
Hope that helps.

Learning by ear has to be self motivated. There aren’t ticky boxes and stages and hurdles etc. The best way to teach someone to learn by ear is to find out a tune they know in their head, usually something as simple as three blind mice, and send them away to figure it out for themselves. How quickly or successful they are at it will vary, but what won’t vary is that they’ll get better at it as they carry on.

“Doe a deer, a female deer, Ray a spot of golden sun ….” is a great one. (a lot easier on a whistle or simple system flute.) If the learner is bright, there is a heck of a lot of useful stuff in that tune about scales and intervals. If a beginner can work that tune out for themselves, they will be chuffed to bits, and a whole rake of important stuff will be internalised for ever.

Re: Making fingering charts, am I correct?

One thing that some people have to learn is how to estimate how how many things they are looking at without having time to count them. Field biologists estimating bird colonies do it. One way is to have set of drawings in a book, laid out like a table with different numbers of black dots in random patterns. Under each is the number. Learning that is not very effective, expecially if they are in numerical order. Better to get someone to test you by covering up the number and making you ‘guess’.

Far better, and demonstrated to me by an industrial psychologist, is to have a set of cards with nothing on them show one for a couple of seconds, make an estimate (without voicing it) then get told the answer. With even a couple of minutes of this the effect on ability is dramatic. Trial and error with feedback is very powerful.

I was reminded of this when reading a discussion similar to this on another forum. An accordian teacher had recommeded ‘hunt and peck’ to find a tune in your head being a good way of internalising where the notes are. Inevitably the fingering chart enthusiasts did not agree.

If you really must learn your tunes from looking at little pictures of whistles, there are plenty of sites like this: http://sniff.numachi.com/aidx/tidxA.html

They’ve already drawn in the littles whistles for you, so no need for you to spend hours using photoshop. Just search for “whistle tablature” or “whistle notation”. Theres lots of programs too for creating this type of notation.

When I started off playing whistle I too learnt my first few tunes using this type of notation. I got some sheet music and pasted in the little whistles under each and every note. I quickly realised what a complete and utter waste of time this was and I started just jotting down the note letters instead DEFG etc. After having translated a few tunes of sheet music in this way I was then able to skip that too and just read the normal sheet music. Writing down the note letters was just helping me to learn to read the sheet music faster and faster. I knew what each dot was but I couldn’t call them quick enough. Now I can play a new tune from the sheet music directly at a reasonable pace. For a simple type tune I just need to play it through once or twice and then it’s in memory so I can put the music away. I will listen to a new tune a good few times before trying to play it. Playing it for the very first time with the help of sheet music I now often find myself not really reading the dots at all, it’s like my fingers already know where to go next. But I keep going till the end of the tune just to make sure I’m playing the right notes. I think at this point my subconscious knows how to play by ear but I don’t fully trust it, so I do read the sheet music of a new tune at least once.

I think the problem with expecting a complete beginner to learn straight away by ear is that it would take them far too long to work out a tune and they would just get bored to death as their progress would seem far too slow. They need to start playing tunes immediately otherwise they’ll feel like they’re getting nowhere. If you get tin whistle lessons here in Ireland, the teacher will usually write the tune out for you in ABC format (not the Internet ABC which is more complicated than sheet music) and they reckon this is best step to take towards learning by ear. Even complete beginners will learn 1 tune per 1 hour class. Whilst the individual notes are learnt from ABC, the timing, length of each note, which ones are tongued/cut, ornamentation and rhythm are all learnt by ear from the teacher.
