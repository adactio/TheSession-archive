Re: Django style jazz chords to tunes

There’s an awful lot of misinformation in this thread. Anyone who claims the “.5 plectrum, DADGAD and let’s not worry about 3rds or the concept of major/minor” approach is better on the grounds of traditionality is, unless they’ve got an incredibly clever argument, spectacularly wrong. Not that I’m aiming to demean this way of playing (that’s up to personal taste), but it is predated by Willie Johnson’s swing style by around 20 years (somewhat more if certain accounts are accepted).

In response to the question, it’s not that simple. To create a fluid swing style guitar part to a tune requires planning - it’s essential to know if that passing chord is going to create an untimely semitone clash or diminished 5th. As such, it’s hard to do at a session unless you’ve prearranged a tune or have a “set of chords that just about go over any tune in X major” (a seriously dangerous path to tread)

Oddly, in my experience the point isn’t using ever increasingly extended chord voicings, the trick seems to be moving toward 3/7 voicings to create chromatic single string lines, plus a bass note to anchor the whole thing to some kind of practical bass progression that fits the tune. Using some kind of musical notation software is a good way to detach your brain from the standard guitar chord voicings and experiment with non scalar chords and inversions.

It’s a good question and a valid one. Although you spoilt it a bit by putting an apostrophe in Django.

Re: D’jango style jazz chords to tunes

There are two issues here, the ‘boom tschuk’ rhythm and the use of complex chromatic harmony.

The ‘boom tschuk’ doesn’t work because the phrasings of the tunes eschew a one/two one/two pattern. You “can” force the tunes into this pattern, and many do (for example, fiddlers with repetitive bowing patterns - with the back beat always on the down stroke) but the tunes are rhythmically much much better that this. And as a strummer, you should know this if you know the tunes. (that old prerequisite)

The chromatic harmony doesn’t work because the tunes aren’t chromatic. You can force them into behaving this way, by suggesting certain phrases suggest chromatic variation, but it is imposing. One could say that the use of harmony is all about the suggestion of melody direction. A simple example would be when a major tune ends on the tonic, you could harmonise it by ending on the fourth, and so giving the listener an unresolved feeling that wants to drop to the third. But even this simple example is an imposition.

My take on it is that melody and harmony are the same thing, it’s just that one is stacked up and the other flows chronologically. It seems a simple suggestion, but if you use a hell of a lot of notes that are not actually in the tune, you are giving the tune a false identity. To put it plainly, you are disguising it.

Let the tune stand proud as what it is.

- YES, traditiona piano backing is the closest thing to boogie-woogie / hot jazz / Djangoish beat there is

To answer the question a bit further, most guitarists are only using a swing influenced style. The purpose of a guitar in swing is to provide the changes over which the soloist can improvise, and to underpin the solo to the header. In trad, the melody is the constant and the chord structure changes to provide the harmony. Look at a technique called voice leading, at 3/7 chords and finding chromatisism if you wish to impart a more swing sound to your playing. And also look at developing your left and right hand muting. See the “Strumming Patterns” thread for info about that.

What would Django do?

Ps. Django (no apostrophe) style chords center around voicings that he was able to play with his injured fretting hand. His handicap resulted in often placing the 3rd and 4th fingers on adjacent strings the same fret. The thumb then is wrapped around to the top of the neck to play the 6th and 5th strings.

These fingerings favor voicings/inversions in the form of minor 6th, major 6/9, m7b5, dom 9 and diminished chords - great for jazzy tunes with shifting key centers. (Interesting how a disability gave rise to a style of music…) Given the “modal” nature of Irish tunes it would seem “hot club” chords have a very limited use.

Which begs the question - what would Django have done in DADGAD??
