What would you have done?

Sorry this post is incredibly long, but if you bear with me it does relate back to a recent thread eventually.

I’m in Manila, and it’s extremely hot, sticky and polluted. I feel like I’m choking on petrol fumes. No surprise to find that there weren’t any sessions posted here for the Philippines :-) I certainly haven’t found any. Haven’t found any Irish pubs either for that matter. Not that I’d be able to join in a session anyway since I haven’t got any instruments with me. I’m sure that by now they would’ve warped beyond recognition.

I did find a cafe/bar with a London Underground sign outside though, so I popped in for brekkie this morning. Ended up on the beers singing karaoke with The Maids Behind The Bar. I discovered I could actually sing, as long as it didn’t get too high. No really, I wasn’t all that bad. Having said that, the machine didn’t score me very well, but I reckoned that that was because the mic wasn’t working properly, and the machine couldn’t allow for my intricate popsong ornamentation around the tune :-)

I went for a walk in the old walled city and met a toothless man who wanted to take me round in this tricycle thing with a covered sidecar. I couldn’t hide the fact that I really really wanted a go in one, so I agreed, on one condition - that I be allowed to be the driver. So there I was driving round the streets of Manila on this stupid-looking contraption, dodging mad traffic and making passers-by do a double take. What an adrenalin rush! That is, until I decided I wanted to stop. Couldn’t find the brakes could I? I tried dragging my feet along the ground, but the momentum of the side car was too much. Fortunately, just as I was about to get us both killed, the driver pointed to a brake near my right foot.

I walked back the way I’d come, and stopped to have a look in a nice cool, air-conditioned antique shop with art galleries and stuff, and when I came back out it was pouring with rain, and I mean really *pouring* - like being in a gigantic power shower.

Anyway, the point to my story was that when I sat down under the shelter of some corrugated iron roofing outside a diner, a toothless old woman approached me with 3 children. 2 of them were completely naked, and the other was wearing a sort of vest. They waved and said “hello”, and then the woman started asking for money. She’d say “give me money” and the kids would try to copy her with amusing results such as “bi-ni-naani”, “biriyani”, “it-ee-naami”. Another younger woman appeared with some more children and 2 small dogs. Soon I was surrounded by children and animals. One of the dogs - a puppy - sniffed me and then walked round and peed behind my back (luckily not actually on me). The older woman pointed to the diner and said “canteen”, “give me money”, and then pointed to her mouth. I said “no money”. I’d already been asked hundreds of times after all. They soon gave up asking me for money, but they weren’t going anywhere, so I decided to observe them for a while. The kids were all about 2 or 3 years old, apart from one who looked about 4 or 5 maybe. One of the younger kids was holding a small clear plastic bag containing some scraps of meat, and these were being shared out. At one point, the kid dropped some on the dirty ground, and the older woman picked it back up so that it wouldn’t be wasted. Another kid had some sort of rash round its mouth, and the mother would alternate between scolding it and rocking it on her knee. It was crying the whole time - a really distressed crying sound like it was in pain - not the whingey sort of attention-seeking cry you get from Western kids. The older boy had one of those portable computer games which doubled up as something that looked like a mobile phone. They’d take turns to pretend they were saying hello to me on the phone. Another kid was playing with a sheet of cellophane, and another had brought over a shallow cardboard box and was teasing the puppy which kept biting back. I asked the woman what its name was. She said it was “Negra”, and that the other dog - a cream-coloured chihuahua type thing - was called “Sara”. One of the kids went over to the “canteen” and a man gave her a plastic bag containing ice, which they all started to eat. Soon they gave up and drifted off, only to return later to ask me for money again. Later still they disappeared again round the corner and I took the opportunity to take out my wallet and get some notes out. (I think it was because I didn’t want them to see how much I had in my wallet out of respect for them, or something) Anyway, I found the woman and gave her the notes, and said “canteen money”. She said “thankyou” and beamed at me. I walked off feeling really depressed. Why had I taken so long to give her money when all she wanted was something to eat? Perhaps I thought that they’d only keep pestering for more, or maybe more families would come and pester me. But that didn’t happen. About half an hour later I passed them again walking in the other direction towards my hostel, and they shouted “thankyou my friend” across the road.

I guess the point of the story is, well, I dunno what the point of it is. But I remember there being a thread recently about the pros and cons of charity, and I thought about it at the time. I’m sure some people here will slate me for giving them money, saying that it’s pointless and doesn’t help them to become self-sufficient. I wondered whether I felt feelings of guilt, and decided that I did, but that it wasn’t because of a difference in wealth, but more because I had had to think about it before giving them money. It should have simply been a case of “you’re telling me you really need food, you can’t pay for it, I can, and anyway I’ve already eaten”. On my way back to my hostel I was contemplating how much money I spend on Guinness during a session, not to mention the cab fare home. The amount I gave them was only a tiny fraction of the money I’d spend on a night out getting drunk and playing tunes.

What would you have done?

Re: What would you have done?

It’s a very difficult quandry and one I have thought about a few times. Vin Garbutt sums it up well in his song “One legged begger”.

I think that it would be impractical to give money to every begger you pass and each time. Even in Edinburgh, you would end up quite poor yourself--Then, again, if everybody helped that problem wouldn’t arise. However, I have frequently given money to apparently deserving cases over the years and I don’t see anything wrong with that either. It depends on the circumstances. The most generous way is to give is freely and without any great deal of thought and not through feelings of fear or guilt.

Ideally, we should all subscribe to the various charities etc instead so that these people can be helped indirectly. We all intend to do this but never seem to get around to it--at least, I know that I don’t contribute as much as I should.

As regards your scenario Dow, I would probably have done the same in your position but there is no simple answer.

Greetings: I think you used good judgment. I used to be an office manager in a homeless-placement office in San Francisco. The amount of need and desperation I saw on my way to work was terrible. I could have taken every cent Ted and I didn’t need to simply exist and given it away just to the people I saw, and it would never have been enough. I got used to the idea that I could not fix these people and focused on what I could do. I brought sandwiches in with me and handed them out, purchased fast-food deals and gave them to people, stayed on top of the politics and communicated with my elected representatives about the issues of poverty and homelessness. I’ve long since moved on from that job and actually moved on to a new career (court reporter and captioner), but I maintain those habits. Sadly, when I do go into SF, it’s not at all difficult to find someone who needs to be fed.

I’m personally a lot more comfortable actually passing out food, though I have given money on occasion. At least in SF, there is a staggering amount of substance abuse among the homeless. I hate the thought of my money going to that, so I’d rather just give them the food.

A tough situation. And that’s how I make my rather rocky peace with it. I think a certain amount of unease around this is inevitable, and perhaps even a sign of mental and moral health.

Blessings, Cathryn

Re: What would you have done?

Hey Dow, too bad you didn’t have any instruments, I bet they would have enjoyed your music as well. Seriously.

OK, I have a lot to say on the topic, but shall try to keep it to a minimum. I have lots of experience with the poor all over the world. Urban USA, Asian slums, Indian reservations. I find that what guides my level of giving is my gut. On the mean streets of the city, you soon become wise to who is asking for money to shoot up or get drunk or high, which is practically everybody, so I usually will buy them dinner if they are seriously hungry. It’s very easy to turn a hard heart against that kind of panhandling, just make no eye contact, walk on by. However, it’s been a long time since I’ve been on a city street, being exposed to that kind of poverty & homelessness again might just pull my heartstrings.

But the Navajo is a whole different can of worms. When I first moved here, I studied the language, took culture classes, cursed my white ancestors (a good chunk Irish) for the raping of the native tribes. We have one little shopping center & it’s usually full of Indians asking for money, despite signs on the lightpoles saying ‘Please help our community & do not give to panhandlers.’ But when a little old grandma in traditional dress, withered skin, hunched back approaches you at your car which you are loading up with groceries with her hands extended & no English to speak of, how can you say no? My god, she was probably alive for the Long Walk….(er not really, it was around the time of the US Civil War.)

For better or worse, I am mostly over my guilt. I always carry dog food & treats in my car for the ridiculous abundance of strays I come across, but what I will NOT tolerate, is when some drunken Indian man approaches me at my car. It doesn’t matter what story they conjure up, their ride left them, they need to make a phone call, whatever. In a crowded parking lot, he approaches the WHITE girl who is alone, which also scares me. There are gangland style murders out here, & obviously a lingering resentment against white ppl, so if a drunk Indian corners me at my car asking for money, my latest tactic is to say loudly, ‘Why me? Are you approaching me b/c I’m white & therefore you think I have money? What about all those other ppl at their cars, other Navajos, why not ask them? I live here, I am not a tourist, I am here to help the community, & you are scaring me, please turn around & walk away.’ Sounds harsh & maybe it is, but it usually works since they usually expect white folks to cave in at the mere sight of them, & it helps me feel redeemed, empowered & safer.

I think this attitude also comes from other social issues on the rez. All Indians are eligible beneficiaries to receive monthly checks, free health care, etc etc etc, so despite the rampant poverty, they definitely have resources, & I do feel like through my job I am helping out hugely. I have restricted my direct money handouts, though, to virtually nil. However, if a really beautiful rug comes through for sale, or jewelry or something, I often will pay more than the asking price if I feel it is appropriate since I am honoring the artist herself in a direct transaction. I’m also the kind of person who leaves a generous generous tip, often 30% (in any restaurant), so I have no qualms about distributing money directly, & my time in Asia was no different. I always gave out lots of money, esp in Saigon where again, you could see the amputees on skateboards & know it was our bombs that rendered them this way. No, my cash flows fast & free, but it has to be the right circumstances. I prefer direct giving always, the big charities I support (which aren’t many) are often in support of women’s health on an global level.

Wow this is a huge rant, & I apologize, but Dow, never stop giving, & obviously trust your gut. You have the privileges of health, education & earning potential that those that you give to will never have. As for why you withheld initially, it’s b/c in any transaction, you need to feel satisfied. You needed to make sure this woman wasn’t pimping her children, or beating them. You wanted to assess that the situation was one that *could* benefit from your gift. This is a required process to prevent philanthropic burnout. If you feel like you are hemorrhaging money into a pit that cannot be filled, you will stop giving. But you know now that you made a difference, but it was b/c you TOOK THE TIME to get to know the ppl whose lives you were going to touch. Much much better than anonymous bandaids to get you through a cloying crowd, much more meaningful. & much harder work that entails risk & emotional labor, not something you can do all the time, so bravo, good for you. Just remember what your boundaries are & keep your bullsh*t detector on high b/c of the huge amount of ppl who are looking to take advantage of you, but I don’t think you’ll have any problem with that. Love the story about driving the tricycle thing too. Safe travels, keep us in the loop! :)

Not much to add here beyond the wisdom above, but a couple more points:

1) I almost always give. No rationale--I just do.

2) Just read an article in one of the wife’s yoga rags about a person in the same dilemma, who decided that rather than avoid eye contact, feel bad, etc, she would give 1 dollar to the first person who asked her each day. She figured she could hack, at the most, US$31/month

3) Allen Ginsberg, a great poet and (in my opinion) Bodhisattva, lived in the E Village for about 50 ears, a neighborhood deeply saturated with the homeless. Allen decided that, whether he gave or not, he would always make eye contact and smile. He said that transformed the interaction, whether money changed hands or not.

4) I don’t try to figure out what/why somebody wants the money for--that’s not my business.

5) Only time I tend to refuse is when my gut tells me it’s somebody who could earn the money other ways--condition of shoes/teeth is a good idea.

6) (dragging back on-topic): Playing music by, for, and with other people, live in a room, without the intervention of the music business, is itself a revolutionary act--and I mean that as a compliment. It creates positive energy in the world, at a time when multinationals want people to starve and want music to be only a bought-and-sold commodity.

I am moved by emily’s remarks (she and I corresponded and compared experiences a long time back about this) to comment on my own time among the Navajos as a third grade teacher. I was encouraged when I went to the Rez (and it was good advice) to consider who I could help and how. It was hardly ever the men in the parking lot who collected money to buy hairspray and drink it. What I did instead was ask community members I knew how to help out. Once they concluded I was serious, they gave me lots of ideas. Stuff like chopping wood for a grandmother (as they were known) or watching someone’s sheep herd on a Saturday when they were busy with other stuff. Seems like small stuff, but time is a gift too.

I came back to my own community (if I really belong here. I’m not sure) with some new ideas. And I know that, even though I am an unemployed school teacher, I am better off than a lot folks I know.

Ken (now in Indiana, U.S.A. One Navajo teen asked me, “Are there Indians in Indiana?” Not many.)
