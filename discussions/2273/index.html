Added by 🎻 Melody Player 🎻 on October 17th, 2003

What is a bad musician?

The answer is not as straightforward as you might think. In starting this discussion, I’m excluding beginners, learners, improvers, those of us who are just having a bad night etc. I’ve been playing for a few years now but would still see myself as “improving” all the time and the learning process is still on-going.

That leaves those who have been playing a long time and are never going to get any better. Some just don’t bother and rest on their laurels, playing the same tunes all the time. Others just can’t seem to “get the fiddle (or other instrument) to sing” yet may know a store of tunes and still be extremely interested in the music. I consider the latter group to be worthwhile, though, as you can learn quite a lot from them.
Finally, and these are the worst kind, are those who don’t have a clue about the tradition, music etc and are unwilling to take advice from others. Some of them might even play quite well but will try to dominate the session, speed up tunes, constantly (Because we all occasionally do it by accident) get all the parts of different tunes mixed up, have their own badly learned versions of tunes---I’m not talking about regional styles or changes due to the tradition, but just general sloppiness.

Each of us has our limitations and we will all reach different peaks. The main thing is that we all do our best.

John

Re: What is a bad musician?

I play at sessions for the fun of it, so I don’t focus on learning something every time out. But I don’t mind when it happens. Of course, you can’t help but learn and improve just by being there, if you’ve got your ears and mind open. Doesn’t have to be a conscious process though--I think we pick things up by osmosis at sessions and it comes back out of us when it’s ready, sometimes that same tune, sometimes later in the evening, sometimes not for weeks or months. Sure, you can speed that up by trying to be cognizant of what it is that you’re learning--dissecting it, labeling it, etc. But I suspect that can impede or at least color the learning. Sometimes it’s better to just relax and let things happen on their own.

Seems like those of us who live farther away from the tradition too often get in a hurry to learn all this stuff (guilty myself at times), but being patient and not over eager is a part of the traditional approach too, at least with the “native trad” players I’ve known.

I also like to approach a session thinking less about what I’m gonna get out of it, and more about what I can contribute to the collective tunes and craic. So I’m not usually thinking, “Great session--I improved a lot,” but, “Great session--everybody had a good time and we played some mighty tunes.”

Concerning Mark’s comment about practicing by classical musicians, a concert pianist from way back when (I’m not sure who - it *may* have been Artur Rubinstein) once remarked that if he missed practice for one day he’d be aware of it at his next concert. Mark, I don’t think anyone is saying we shouldn’t try to improve. But is that the main point of a session? Why not work at improving at home during the rest of the week, and then come to town to enjoy doing what you’ve learned? Gee, a session that’s driven by everyone working hard to improve right then and there doesn’t sound like as much fun as a session that centers on winging through tunes and slinging the craic. I run our local slow or learning session, and we keep it informal and fun, but it’s still work…not the same as the kind of fun and playfulness we have at our regular sesh.

Yes, some (probably most in the early stages) learning has to be a conscious, willful act, but some of it also just happens, whether you realize it or not. And it strikes me that a session is a good place to relax and let things come to you, rather than always trying to force it. I read Michael’s posts on this theme as: “Some people try too hard. Lighten up and you will progress without struggling so much.” And I agree with that. Given than you’ve spent some time really woodshedding on basic technique, you need to cut loose and just play to let that technique happen, and find out where it takes you. My playing does improve at sessions, but mostly out of serendipity--when you let go and just play, things happen that you’d never do sober, and you make great leaps forward.
