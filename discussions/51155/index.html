Advice for my first session

Hello, fellow musicians.

Next month I’ll be traveling to Ireland for 1-2 weeks (turism around the country). Since I love ITM, I would love to attend to a session or two (I’ll use this website to find them hahaha), and, as I love playing, why not playing some tunes?

I’ve been playing the tin whistle for 2-3 years and I already know some common tunes, e.g. Cooley’s, Morrison’s, Drowsie Maggie, Silver Spear, Off to California, Blarnie Pilgrim, Kesh, Old Favourite, Jim Ward’s, and, honestly, many more.

The thing is I’ve learned all of this on my own and I’ve never played in a session and only attended to one abroad (they aren’t common where I live). Last year I played a tin whistle in a shop, while traveling to London, and the shop assistants played guitar and bodhrán meanwhile, but that’s it (it wasn’t my best moment, but they seemed to really have fun).

So, after all this text, my question is, what should I do? Would it be weird to go to a session some random night and ask them to join them and play a tune or two? I would love to first meet the people previous weeks and things like that, but I’ll be in Ireland for ~10 days traveling so there’s no time for that :(, should I simply not play?

In case you guys think I should try to play at some session, could you give me some advice to not disappoint everyone? Maybe some important things you don’t really learn when you learn on your own. Of course I’ll stop playing if being asked to, but I would preffer to not reach that point.

Thanks in advance!

PD: I’m not new to music, I’ve been +6 years playing guitar and harmonica, just kind of “new” to tin whistle, tenor banjo, Irish music (2-3 years playing) and playing with people (I’ve only played with backing tracks).

First and by far the most important; be a nice person :-)
Ask before the session if it is ok to join. Be patient and listen far more than you play. Normally after a while the other participants or the session leaders might ask you if you want to play a set of tunes. Have a few sets that you know well prepared. Excpect the tunes to be played much faster than you are used to and sometimes in different keys as well.. Enjoy the ride.

Maybe you could see if you could email a session leader, in a friendly and appreciative way, to say that you’ll be there, that it is your first session, and even ask if they could play some of the tunes you know.

But in any case, once you get there, spend the first half-hour listening and getting the feel of things. They’ll probably be friendly and have a chat with you & you can see the best way to proceed. I know when we got travellers joining in our ITM session in England, we were always pleased and made them feel welcome.

Don’t be put off if you can’t join in anything much on that particular evening. It’s all good experience to give you confidence and encouragement to seek out sessions nearer home, or on other holiday destinations.

Best wishes: I hope you have a great time, and if you do, please tell us all about it!

Re: Advice for my first session

Yes!

Go to sessions and listen. Work out whether they’re open or invite-only. See if the playing matches your level of skill and speed. See if there are whistlers there already, and if so, think about whether another one will add anything.

If you feel okay about it, ask if you could join them in a tune or two. If you’re invited to start a tune but don’t feel confident enough, just say so. You could change your mind later on if you get more comfortable.

Don’t worry if you make the odd mistake because of nerves or not being used to playing with others, but if you keep struggling, just go back to listening mode.

Some sessions can feel a bit exclusive even though they’re technically open, but most welcome respectful and considerate players. Which you obviously are, as you’ve asked for advice.

Have fun!

Re: Advice for my first session

To be honest, I would suggest that when you’re in Ireland you plan on listening, unless you’re specifically informed that a particular session is open to newcomers - meaning both people new to that session and people new to the music as a whole. I’m guessing you’re most likely to find this outside of the major tourist destinations, and hopefully some locals will chime in with suggestions. If you provide a rough idea of your itinerary, that might help focus those suggestions.

Alternatively, I would suggest that rather than asking whether you can sit in, that you ask one of the musicians if they know of a good session where new players are welcome. That way, they know a few useful things: that you play, that you don’t play much, and that you’d like to play. And that allows them to either invite you to join in, or not, and nobody is put on the spot.

Re: Advice for my first session

The advice so far has been sound.

I’ve not been to Ireland in many years now but when I visited almost all the sessions were very welcoming on a “superficial” basis. By that, I mean if you walked into the bar with an instrument you would be “beckoned over” to join the musicians.

In many ways, this gave one a false sense of security and maybe even an inflated sense of their own worth. So, the advice to “Listen, listen, listen” is very relevant and you should still consider if you are able to contribute something to the proceedings. If it’s quiet enough, of course, there’s no harm in sitting with them if invited etc. Not everyone plays every tune ..not even the well seasoned players… and you will likely be given an opportunity to start something yourself.

Other sessions are not so obviously welcoming. At the two extremes, there are those which cater to please the tourists and those which are a close core of musicians who are used to playing together to please themselves. Nothing wrong as such with either arrangement but they may not be the best ones to join.

The great majority are something in between though and usually welcoming enough. Most will invite you or will be happy enough for you to join them if you are “nice and polite” and there is enough space. However, as I said, it’s not compulsory to join a session even after an invitation. So, it’s up to yourself to judge accordingly.

“Here in Scotland”, there are now many session arrangements specifically for more intermediate and improving players which we didn’t have in the past. I don’t know if such things happen in Ireland. In the olden days, even learners and improving players just went to whatever session was happening and “did what they could”. As long as they were respectful and didn’t disrupt the proceedings that was OK. Nowadays, things are much more fragmented, for good or ill.

Re: Advice for my first session

All this is good advice. I do think being honest with any session leaders or groups about your level is good (which starts with yourself).

For me, as someone who spends all of my time practicing / playing alone, I struggled with speed and “tightness”. So I’d suggest take your top ten tunes and make sure you can play them really tightly at a speed you’d expect in a session.

Reading the room / getting a sense of the vibe and just making sure to pay attention is key.

A fiddler friend recommended to always leave your instrument in the car and check out the session with out, chatting with people. You can always go get it. The other day I went to a session, it was smaller, three or four people, and I chatted with them and explained I was an early intermediate player with not a huge selection of common tunes. They were super kind but recommended a different session in that town as more my level. Over the course of the night it became clear they had played together a LOT and didn’t have a lot of overlap with what I play.

We ended up having a great conversation afterwards, I requested they play a polka and they were happy to and played some polkas from a Scottish group.

Had I had showed up with my fiddle they would’ve let me join, but honestly it would’ve been a bit off. That would’ve been a bit frustrating for them, and probably less fun for me. Instead my approach allowed some great connections!

I’m sure all learners have a few stories like this.

Also, don’t get discouraged if it’s harder to play with everyone than you think! I know it has been for me.

Re: Advice for my first session

All of the above

Try to get there early introduce yourself to whoever is already there explaining that you are new to sessions and just want to sit in and learn. Most of the really good players that I have encountered are very encouraging to people and will engineer an opening for you at some point. If you are very unlucky you will hit a bunch of egos and show-offs. If this does happen, don’t be put off.

Listen carefully to what is played and how it is played. If you think you know the tune, don’t assume they will play it exactly as you have learnt it. Understanding variation is the real beginning of getting to grips with this music. It’s ok to listen without playing. But if you feel comfortable, take a risk.

↳ Posted by Eamon Ó Máirtín on July 5th, 2026.

Re: Advice for my first session

on the technical level, be ready on the technical level.

be sure you can play against a metronome (or strum machine, or etc. real musicians are only less precise and are only harder to hear). be sure you have 5-10 bpm headroom (you can play slightly faster than the expected session tempo. there is no hard numbers, reels at 100 bpm, jigs at 130 bpm could be a good target, but some reels sound really good at 90 bpm, even 80 bpm). if you can play along against recordings and youtube videos of your favourite tunes and musicians, you will do just fine at a session.

on the practical level, to start a set, you only need to know the first A part of a tune, by the end of the second A part, everybody else will join in and you will never play the B part alone, unless nobody else knows the tune. but, the big but. the first 2 bars of the A part must come out super strong, super loud and super crisp. that takes a bit to practice and time investment.

as for leaving instruments in a car, I used to live in the New York area, after you park your car, you leave the doors unlocked, roll down the windows, leave the glove compartment open, with a note “no money, no radio”, those were the days, true story.

more seriously, I play mandolins, they must be protected against heat (glue will soften and creep or unglue) (also protected against cold). inside of a parked car can get very hot even in the shade, to illustrate, lots of photos of heat melted things after the recent heat wave. also I now have a Delrin flute, and this material has an unexpectedly low melting point (below 100C). take your instruments with you, leave nothing in the car.

Yes, I’m sure there will be plenty of places where you, Alejandro, would fit in nicely.

However, always proceed with caution and respect. Even the very players won’t find every session suitable or welcoming. Of course, there’s also many that they just wouldn’t want to join.

Learner/slow sessions didn’t exist to the same extent in the old days. So, we just looked for sessions that were welcoming and, usually, just played the tunes we knew. Most friendly musicians don’t mind as long as you don’t take up space from regular players and/or disrupt things. That’s the best scenario, I think.

Many improving players want to play all night, of course, and so are happy to visit more basic and slower sessions with well known tunes. That’s OK as well but you can learn much from the really good sessions too if they are happy to welcome you.

Re: Advice for my first session

There’s a lot of great advice here, so I don’t have much to add. One thing that you’ll find is that no matter how much you practice and work on tempos and such, things are different when you’re playing with other people. There’s social pressure that you don’t experience at home. The first time I played at sessions in Ireland, I thought I was ready, and as soon as I was playing in a session, it was a mess. Much of it was just my nerves getting in the way, but the music felt too fast for me. I actually clocked some of the tempos, and they were within the range that I could play when I was sitting at home. But the rhythms were a bit different than I was used to practicing, and combined with my nerves, I didn’t do as well as I had hoped.

There are some ways around that, by learning some calming techniques, etc. But if you have communicated with the players that you’re new to this, they will likely be very supportive. If they ask you to start a set, then maybe name your tunes first. You can offload some of the tasks of leading a set by asking them to help you… They will likely ask you to set the tempo, but then you can rely on them to help keep the tempo steady, and make the transitions between tunes cleanly, etc.

I think you’ll find that if you’re fun to hang out and chat with, that matters as much as how many tunes you can play or how fast you can play.

But most of all, just soak it in, and enjoy your trip!

Re: Advice for my first session

My approach as a newcomer to a session has been:

Go into the pub, have my fiddle but don’t get it out of the case. Find a spot as close as possible to the music and ask “is it OK if I sit here?” and then listen. If it’s a session that’s welcoming of new people which I’ve found to be the case most of the time, they’ll see the fiddle and invite me to get it out and likely invite me to play something. If they don’t I’ll assume they want to do their own thing and don’t really want new people coming and playing - that’s happened a handful of times.

My first time at a new session or among musicians I don’t know well, I’ll generally only start a tune when asked. Over time I can get a sense of how much to play. If there are better players than me there which is generally the case, I don’t need to start tunes much at all. I’m there to add the quality of the session.

When I do play at a new session, I generally play a set I know very well, and make sure the last two tunes are ones others there will likely know, while the first could be less well known as long as I can play it myself with steady rhythm. If I couldn’t keep a steady rhythm myself I wouldn’t try to lead a tune - if I’m offered to play a tune I might ask “can we play ___” and let someone else lead it.

It helps to enjoy listening to the music for its own sake. Even now that I know a couple hundred tunes, it’s quite likely in a given session that I’ll know less than 5% of what gets played - so I have to be there to listen primarily anyway. I recommend being an active listener. I’ll be tapping my feet and fingers and lilting the tune to myself silently as it’s played, and practicing relaxing my body while doing all these things. This way the time I’m sitting in a session “not playing” I’m still practicing much of what is important for being able to play the music - which is having the tune in my body and mind in detail, and moving my body to the tune in rhythm, and staying relaxed - and there’s always more detail and nuance of the rhythm that one can incorporate into the lilting and foot and finger tapping. And it’s fun.

When I’m playing with friends I know well, or especially in private, then I feel more free to start more tunes and start tunes I can’t necessarily play smoothly. Still I err on the side of starting less and listening more.

Hmm, I don’t remember saying anything like that, but I can’t necessarily vouch for my younger, stupider self… ;-)

I actually believe that there is a lot to be learned by going to sessions, even if you never play a tune and end up listening all night. I suggest to my students to get out to sessions early in the learning process. The more they experience it, the less intimidating it will be. One of the sessions I anchor will often have a student (either a banjo student of mine, but more often just people that attend my weekly tune learning session) who only has a couple of tunes under their belt. We always offer for those people to start a set, or at least call a set that other people lead (without pressuring them)…

I find that, more often than not, this leads to those players being inspired and thankful for being included. The real way to get good at playing this music is to get obsessed, and participate as much as possible, hopefully with people that are happy to share and encourage you. And it really helps if you have someone who is willing to help you set reasonable expectations about how much you will be able to participate at different stages of your development… But no matter how much you try to prepare, you will likely never feel fully “ready” for your first session…
