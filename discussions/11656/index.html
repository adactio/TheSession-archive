“the tradition of writing tunes in Scotland has been much much stronger. This has had the notable effect of Scottish music being much more rigid than Irish music both in terms of rhythm, decoration and variation”

I’m not convinced that the one is the cause of the other. The cultural conditions in Scotland were for a long time very different than they were in Ireland---there was much more association between the music of “the people” and the music of the upper classes---wealthy personnages would often hire local musicians to play for them, and you can see the proof of this in the tune names---how many tunes are there like “Miss MacLeod’s return to Kinlochan” or some such thing. In Edinburgh, at least, where the classes lived literally on top of one another, there was much mixing of the classes and their entertainments, and each was familiar with many styles. And then there was the significant Continental influence, which you didn’t really have in Ireland either.

I think all these things exposed Scottish musicians to other styles of music (especially the formal classical) and that probably is what made the music sound the way it does. It also made it more likely that they would be writing things down, but I think that’s more of a byproduct of the history of the place than a shaping element of the musical style.

Re: Class Tune

Juliette’s (mis)reading of history doesn’t square with the written collections of tunes dating from the 18th and 19th centuries, nor the habit of many old trad musicians of writing tunes down (often in their own invented notation or tablature), nor of the more recent and not uncommon phenomenon of recording artists listing written sources for their tunes in their liner notes.

As for Michael’s rejection of the dots as a “primary source”--for many older tunes, the dots (Bunting, Hudson, O’Farrell) are the only source we have. In fact, some of the older tunes that you’ve learned to play by ear were no doubt first resurrected from the grave by players who found them in old tune books and manuscripts. Just because you’re learning a tune aurally from another player doesn’t mean that they didn’t learn it from the dots alone.

Putting tunes to paper is subject to the same shortcomings as learning a tune off another player--you get what you get. Learning a tune aurally doesn’t prevent you from picking up the source’s mistakes, misunderstandings, and intentional changes to the tune.

You could even argue that some people are more convinced a version is the “right” one when they learn it by ear--even if it’s a half-arsed version. My guess is that Michael would be rolling his eyes (or worse) if someone showed up at his session playing Paul Ha-penny as a slow air (a la Martin Hayes) instead of the hornpipe it is. If that ‘someone’ has heard only Hayes’s playing of the tune, chances are they won’t even recognize Angela Carberry’s dance tempo playing of it as the same tune. And they probably won’t like it, either.

In contrast, most trad musicians take the dots with a grain of salt. They’re a starting place, not the be-all, end-all setting from their favorite recording star or local session demi-god. With the dots, tempo is rarely indicated, let alone firmly planted in your aural memory. Same with degree of swing and pulse. So it’s much easier to instill your own understanding of the music as you hear the tune come off the page in your mind’s ear. Which is what trad musicians have been doing for at least the last 200 years.
