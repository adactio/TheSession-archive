The one true way?

Recently I came across someone making a comment to a notation on site that was taken note from note from a popularly available printed source. This person complimented the post saying something to the effect that it was nice to have the unadulterated ‘correct’ version of the tune, minus any ornamentation or variation from the person making the post. I’ve seen quite a lot of copying happening on site, sometimes from printed sources, sometimes direct cut-and-paste from other ABC files, but this was the first time I’d come across someone giving another praise for it. I might have let that slide, but the suggestion that it was ‘CORRECT’ and ‘UNADULTERATED’ was just a step too far. By who’s determination? By what criteria?

When I submit a tune on site here I do my damnedest to give something ‘basic’ and ‘clear’ or to at least include a ‘basic’ version of anything I might later adulterate, but mostly I stick with the basics. Sometimes I transcribe the music from field recordings, but more often I check it against my notes and other sources and give something along the lines of how I know it, or one of the ways I know it. At other times I’ll give more than one ‘way’ with it. I also try to list any alternate keys, and if I write it out in an alternate key I do not usually do it as a direct copy of what is already posted, including some ‘variation’ or other ‘ways’ with it. Part of my reasoning, though in good part from the ‘heart’ and ‘experience’, is that THERE IS NO ‘CORRECT’ & ‘ONLY’ WAY… Besides, for me, and I would expect for most of us, every repeat embodying the basic melody is similar, agrees in part, but is also distinct and different from any other. Maybe this is me reacting to the ones I’ve known ~ small fry, big fry, famous and infamous… I would guess most of you have known the sort. I’m not going to list any names.

What is funny about these tyrants/pedants is that when you actually catch them out and can produce something ‘older’, which by their reasoning ‘should’ be ‘CORRECT’, if it doesn’t meet their pre-conceptions of what is ‘CORRECT’, they find fault with your source, whatever it is, whoever, whenever… Maybe the transcription was ‘English’ or ‘American’, always suspect. ;-) Maybe the recording you got from so-and-so, who’d learned it from her da, who learned it from his da, who learned it from his ma ~ etc… ~ maybe something went wrong down the line, they ‘screwed up’ in the process? Hey, I know that is an unquestionable part of the process. Just think of those tunes, some great, that are the first part of one and the second of another, or are turned completely around, the B-part followed by the A, and they work, sometimes… Or the ‘famous’ who were playing a set for dancers, started off with an A-part, couldn’t remember the B-part, and improvised another part on the fly ~ and liked it so much it became a part of their repertoire…and that of others…

As another example, I’ve collected the same tune from a dozen sources in a circumference of roughly 10 miles, and no two were alike, even between two like instruments, say fiddles, played by brothers who often played the thing together…

I understand wanting things to be concrete, stable, reliable, but our music is a bit like ‘Chinese Whispers’, why ‘Chinese’, let’s say, for the sake of focus ~ ‘Celtic Whispers’ ~ (come on, ‘K’ not ‘Anglo’ ‘S’) ~ in each hand it undergoes a personalization, it changes, sometimes slightly, sometimes drastically, sometimes just by nature of the instrument in hand… To get in a froth over it seems like the worst thing to have in a session ~ a rabid sessioneer… Even there, sessions can knock bits off of a tune in the melee. I’m cringing here, because, after saying all this, I too hate seeing things steam rolled flat by the speed and sloppiness of some session playing/players.

And then there’s what I’m calling ‘fascism’. Is it just because it doesn’t agree with me? I took on a lot of unusual tunes, tune types and versions from the many folk I’d grown to meet, but when I showed up back in say Dublin at a session there, folks took my take as a mistake and soon played things as they’d always knew them, mostly from books and recordings and local luminaries, and played over whatever I might have tried to introduce. Hey, it took a lot for me to get that far. I wasn’t and still am not one to ‘introduce’, preferring to honour a session as it is and maybe after a year offering something different from the usual repertoire. Maybe all this is about guilt?

Do any of you think there is a ‘one and only’ as far as tunes and sets, a ‘correct’ way? I remember one ‘composer’ who was chuffed to find one of her pieces had mutated, not so much we couldn’t recognize it. It had also changed key… She, like I, found that strange radiation of evolutionary forces exciting ~ a buzz… But, more ‘buts’, like crabs scurring everywhere ~ I also dread some of the awful excesses of exhibition, where it is not a ‘natural’ evolution but someone kicking an old favourite into something completely unrecognizeable… And yet some folks do that very well…but don’t pretend that it is ‘in the tradition’…they are just messin’ about… Others use it like a bully, to intimidate, like those that show up at a session and mutate things until they’re the only one playing.

I guess I appreciate ‘balance’, or maybe I’m just being hypocritical? There are times I want to shout out “I got that from ~ ”, trying to claim some ‘authority’, wanting some of that ‘power’ myself, as if I knew the one and true way… But I’m glad to say most of my drive is toward keeping things open, hospitable, the way it was, mostly, offered up to me, and to work against things elitist and cliquish… If I was given the choice music and dance wise of knowing a spell or having a magic wand (my copy of Harry Potter arrived yesterday) it would be to have one that fosters that humanity in us all… (Hey, it’s Sunday and my wife is listening to hymn singing…)

Re: The one true way?

When I first started playing ITM one of the things that frustrated me the most was the variation in tune settings. I would struggle to learn and memorize a tune only to find out it wasn’t the same setting everyone else seemed to be playing -- and in a different key sometimes.

For a while I would determine that the composer’s setting overruled any other setting to emerge. But that ended when I realized that the popular version was often an improvement. I also realized that sticking to the composer’s version might place me in the same proximity of my aforementioned frustration.

But there’s always exceptions the rules. For example: Charlie Lennon’s tune, The Smiling Bride. I love his setting and many of the people I play with know the same setting. The Mulcahy Family recording has gained local attention, and their version of this tune leaves out that really lovely second ending on the A part. When someone who learned it from that recording starts it -- it’s a train wreck. Another train wreck can happen when the Humours of Trim comes up. If they learned it off of Kevin Crawford’s CD they call it the Rolling Waves and play all C naturals against our C sharps. Which one is right? Hell if I know.

Then there’s the repeating parts problem. As far as I’m concerned this issue is up in the air for half the tunes.

Re: The one true way?

There are so many different versions of so many different tunes that it would be silly to berate someone for playing a tune differently to how you play it, ITM is not like classical music whereby what the composer has written down is ‘gospel’, tunes are meant to be played around with, in good taste of course, but to give out to someone in a seisiún because they play a tune differently to how you learnt it is just plain stupid. Just because you got your version in O‘Neills doesn’t mean it is more valid than the version someone else learnt from Tommy Peoples. The first version of Sporting Paddy I learnt is completely different to the typical seisiún setting, but I don’t give out to people for playing the typical setting, when I start it off as I first learnt it. My favourite tune is probably Lord Gordon’s Reel, but there are so many different versions that whenever I play it in a seisiún anyone who plays it with me almost always has a different version, but playing two different versions together can often sound great. So instead of getting precious about playing the ’correct‘ version of the tune, just listen to how other people play it and then come up with your own version, after all this is what most of the great players do, you rarely hear the top musicians playing well known tunes in their exact famous seisiún setting. Finally, just listen to Frankie Gavin and Mike McGoldrick playing on the ’Tunes’ album, they rarely play the exact tune, they tend to go off on one and improvise bits here and there, that is what makes a great ITM soloist. If you just play every tune exactly the same way three times around every time, you are just boring. Variation is a major part of ITM.

Re: The one true way?

The One True Way is the “primrose path”. The only thing that matters is: “Does it fit?” If yr “version” doesn’t fit in with the sesh, it’s time to change. Knowing yr instument and knowing the tune is to say the least helpful.

Anybody claiming "correct & unadulterated, should be ignored. They are either completely ignorant or it’s a wind-up.
The counter windup is “anything on the page is wrong”.
The best transcriptions aim to give only a sense of the tune, and the user can fill in the blanks. Kind of like a coloring book.

People tend to approach folk music,with the assumption that the tune is a specific linear series of notes. You could learn a version and play it that way for the rest of your life. You will get bored eventually, won’t want to play it, call it dirty names, &C.

There’s an Irsh proverb: “Every story has 2 sides and every song has 7 versions”. Listening to the early recordings reveals a great deal of variation, and similarity, but not identicality to the earlier publications. There is a story that Jas O’Neill when transcribing for Capt Francis (no relation) became extremely frustrated with the process. Classically trained, he was hired tonote down the tunes. After completion, he would play back what he’d written. Frequently the museos would naysay and then play something completely different.
It’s unclear whether this was due to an unconscious process or sheer cussedness. The point is that variation is a VITAL part of the living tradition.

Donough touches on it with the “critical note” comment. I spent a couple of years “dumbing down” tunes to see which notes and phases are the critical ones. Now I’m rebuilding them with a better idea of how much room I have to gambol in. I’ll admit that my variations are evolutionary, and I’m not above lifting variations from the masters (or even the journeymen (and women)).

Re: The one true way?

In answer to your question, Michael, I don’t think you can call such small snippets different “versions” - that seems like overkill. “Variation” sounds odd as well, because it implies that there’s a standard that it’s deviating from. I’d say the 2 are simply different, but neither is wrong, just one sounds better than the other. For me, the gbeg sounds better because it links more smoothly with the previous notes, and like you say it avoids repetition of the shape of afdf.

However, there is a line which you cross where a phrase does become “wrong”, like if the melody goes in the wrong direction so that it doesn’t link up properly with the next part of the tune, or you go into part of another tune by accident, or you outline the notes of a different chord that doesn’t fit, e.g. afdf ^gbeg would give you an Emaj sound and it’d start sounding a bit lydian and freaky :-) My point is that determining at what point something changes from being a mere difference in version or variation to being “wrong” or “not how the tune goes” is a subjective matter, but it’s more easily judged the better you know the music. Say for example if you only learnt one tune in your whole life, and that tune was the Salamanca, you wouldn’t be in a position to argue about whether someone else had the tune right or wrong. To know that, you have to have exposure to lots of other tunes, so you are familiar with how phrases are treated melodically and rhythmically within the tradition. So we’re all on that scale somewhere. That ability to judge comes with a familiarity acquired through years of playing.

Re: The one true way?

The more I think about it, the more obvious it is that it’s all about experience. A lot of good players past and present basically “recompose” or rework tunes so that they are almost unrecognisable as the same tune unless you are told “that’s just another version of such-and-such”. I have in mind people like John Doherty in particular, and things like regional variants of tunes like Donegal versions. These have to have been “composed” and played out or recorded by someone at some point for them to have become popularised. They’d catch on because they’ve been reworked by great players who have musical influence and already know how to play variations on tunes that work musically without the essence of the tune being lost. A beginner changing a tune to that extent would have less experience in adding interesting twists to tunes and so they wouldn’t make as good a job of it, and would likely be told that they weren’t playing the tune correctly rather than it being “so-and-so’s version”.
