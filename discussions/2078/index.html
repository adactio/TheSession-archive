↳ Posted by b.maloney on September 1st, 2003.

Re: the qualities of great musicians

I’ve read all the posts here and there are some pretty good observations. What makes a great player? IMO,
it’s down to these specifics -

1 - (near) faultless technique
2 - a deep understanding of the music
3 - an aesthetic sense of good taste
4 - *confidence*.

If a player does not have all of these qualities, then there is something lacking. On excellent technique alone, one can sound reasonably good, but also can sound like a ‘musical monkey’, as the saying goes.

Don’t ever fall for the old adage ‘it’s the feeling that counts’ - no degree of ‘playing with feeling’ will sound good if it’s not underpinned by solid technique.

Playing with good taste, ie to suit the music is *so* important - Sean Maguire has been mentioned a few times in these discussions, and IMO he has been guilty of bad taste at times (using excessive vibrato, unsuitable bowing etc). I make no apology for these comments - remember it is the person’s playing I am commenting on, not the person himself. No one is above criticism! Anyway, for the majority of his recorded output, I think
his good points far outweigh the bad.

Ever been to a folk club and listened to a player who lacks confidence and plays almost apologetically? I’ve heard a few who otherwise would make quite good-sounding players. In contrast, Bjillson mentioned a performance by Johnny Cunningham which was *magical* to the audience. If there’s one thing that shines out there - it’s *confidence*.

Take all these points, add a deep understanding of the music (whatever type of music it may be) - and you have a great
musician.

Finally, there has been much made of the musician’s generosity of spirit, warmth, kindness etc - these are a nice addition
to a great musician but they are not a pre-requisite. I know one or two of the ‘big boys’ (no names here) who
are truly excellent musicians, but who are thoroughly dismissive, self-centred obnoxious gits! Thankfully, though, they
are the exception.

Jim

Re: the qualities of great musicians

Lesl, that’s a great topic. Some people seem born with a lot (maybe an excess) of confidence, some eventually earn it, and some of us are under-confident to a fault.

A band mate of mine once told me “The only difference between us on a good day and the A tier players is audacity.” And there is truth in that. Self doubt will derail you every time, a self-fulfilling prophecy.

I’m not naturally an audacious person, especailly when it comes to music. To me, music isn’t about showing off, so my ego tends to take a back seat. I struggled for a while with how to play with confidence without feeling arrogant or audacious or the center of attention. It can become a vicious circle: self-consciousness leads to self-doubt, which leads to a trainwreck, which heightens the self-consciousness.

What finally worked for me is to focus on the tune, confident that the tune itself will be the worthy center of attention, not how I play it. I play this music because it grabs me by the heart, and I know it grabs other people the same way, so all I have to do is put the music out there. Sessions are an excellent place to do this. Gradually, you’ll learn to live inside the tune instead of in your playing of the tune (or, like a character in a Woody Allen movie, inside the imagined criticisms of what other people think about your playing of the tune, heh), and the tune will come out self-assured and strong.

Some of the most brilliant players go through this, even well into their careers. Tommy Peoples admits that he’s uncomfortable with crowds, but he lets the music do the talking. Mike Rafferty once told an audience, “I’m one of those nervous types,” and then proceeded to crank out 2 hours of jigs and reels with nary a bobble. Such players have learned to acknowledge the stage fright or simple lack of confidence, but not let it interfere with their confidence in the music.

This summer, fiddler and flute player Cait Reed told me that Irish music seems to attract more than its share of relatively shy or at least overly humble people. I agree. I’ve seen countless brilliant players act outright apologetic in front of a an audience, even *before* they’ve played. Among many players, it’s part of the tradition to downplay your self. Which is why the focus lands so fully and appropriately on the music.
