“The Invention of Tradition” - edited by Eric Hobsbawn & Terence Ranger

So, as I pull back the skins to this cave I’ve been hiding in, what’s all this cufuffle (harangue?) going to be about anyway? Well, I regularly shoot the bull with this guy, Jack, who is a general handyman and all around nice guy and friend who is always trying to get me to attend the local ploughing contests. He reminds me of the characters I’ve had the fortune to meet, befriend and share time with who happened to also carry Irish tradition as one or two of the facets to their full lives. I had a tease and a laugh with Jack today and afterwards turned to my wife and said “I wish there were more regular folk like that playing the music and dancing.”

It used to be for ‘tradition’, the greater part of it, that it was mostly made up of ‘regular folk’. So what does that mean you might ask. Aren’t we all ‘regular folk’? Well, there are the farmers and shop keepers we’ve danced with, fisherfolk whose music we’ve danced to, mechanics I’ve learned tunes from, colliers I’ve sung with, etc… For them it was as a ‘hobby’ and ‘pastime’ that they made and make music and/or dance, part of their life, but not the ‘be all and end all’. And, it was mainly about friends and the social ‘craic’, and that seemed the greater value to these activities. Some of these folks happened to achieve such skill that they were and are even internationally known. A few names from the many come to mind - - - Peter and Vincent Broderick and families, Mick Hoy, Josie McDermott, McGorman’s, Russell’s, Gallagher’s, Crehan’s, Quinn’s, Cannon’s, Mooney’s, and the beat goes on… They weren’t obsessed. The consequence was that music and dance wasn’t their main topic of chat or concern, there was a good share of just chit-chat, even about the weather, and some might even have friends and family who weren’t involved in either the music or the dance, and that was OK. There were some damned fine cooks amongst them too, usually good down home basic food and baked goods. You could say that a ‘cuppa’, tea for the uninitiated, and fresh baked bread still warm from the oven, butter melting over it, and conversation, might actually be higher on their list of appreciations and necessities, or at least equal.

So what is it about ‘obsession’? Why are there so damned many folks out there nowadays desperate to call themselves “Irish musician”, or ‘dancer’, or just ‘musician’? There is something unhealthy about that being the most important thing in a person’s life, or so it seems to me. Some of these obsessives are volatile and even poisonous. Some of the elements I’ve come to know and respect about that older root to this tradition we share, like humour, patience, courtesy, character, humanity, intelligence and an interest to know and understand others and that world around us – seems a bit thin with some of these “IRISH MUSICIANS & DANCERS”. The capitals are there as a reflection of how these obsessives seem to see themselves, with emphasis on this content in their lives, too often with neglect to other things.

What is even more scary is that these are the sort of self-appointed ‘experts’ and ‘keepers of the tradition’ that take up the banner and go political in order to secure power for themselves and a more ‘true’ tradition, as they see it. That influence, the unhealthy focus on ‘competition’ and ‘exhibition’ I blame on this ‘obsession’. The ‘constraints’ they impose in an attempt to secure their ‘pure essence’ is suffocating, at least from my experience of it, and counter productive, tending to secure their realm for the select and the elect, rather than opening it up for all, for just ‘regular folk’, including those with two left feet. There can even be the expectation, sometimes even stated, that it isn’t just about the ‘craic’ and the social aspects, it has to be about more than this, some greater ideal and goal!? So what is the outcome? I’ve seen it in the dance and the music, though, thank God, not all are ‘affected’. There are those dances where they are all ‘dancers’ by their own definition, like ‘challenging contras’ in North America, and where, except for maybe a forced condescension, there really isn’t that openness, acceptance, patience and appreciation for beginners and the uninitiated I had grown to love and respect about country house and village dances, village pub session, GAA ceilis, those that were social gatherings first, dance and music second. Sometimes we’d be outside on a nice day doing more talking and laughing than music, sometimes it was in the kitchen with tea and baked goods, and usually not the kind that come in plastic bags from the hyper-market, with real butter, and quite often alcohol free…

So, what is this about – maybe ‘accounts’, of both types and extremes to the issue and the conflicts that can cause, like of ‘interest’. I’ve a huge store of heroes to fit my preferred take on it – like Mick Hoy and his great recipes for cabbage and illegally caught eel, his story telling, his humour, his heart. And I have my demons, like the local bored of Comhaltas. And what about all those damned pretentious organizations scrambling for power and the buck, ours and the government funds, like ‘Comhaltas Ceoltoiri Eireann’ in Eire/Ireland? No, I won’t condemn them all, as there are some mighty fine branches, good fun. But such organizations can just as easily squeeze the life and humour out of the thing they profess to be the keepers of, through rules and regulations, petty bickering, ego-centrism, political-centrism, mis-direction of time, funds and energy - - - and dull and boring, mouldy ‘old ways’, meant in its worst possible sense. “We’ve always done it this way (since the organization was codified post WWII) and we will always do it this way!” They can kick the life out of ‘tradition’ and not realize the stench of the rot until it is too late, unable to see their own guilt or failings because of the size of the shadow of the edifice they’ve built, casting so wide that it puts much that is before them in the dark. It is always someone else’s fault if things go pear-shaped.

Having founded a few ‘organizations’, am I on a guilt trip? Am I exploring my darker sides? Am I fearing that I may be tarred with being ‘obsessive’? I’ve rambled this far, what are your fears and trepidations? What run-ins have you had with obsessives, individuals or organizations? And to balance it out, what about ‘regular characters’, like Geordie Hannah, God bless you Geordie…and thanks for the ‘craic’, it was mighty…like your spade sized deeply grooved hands…

Is there something wrong with calling yourself a musician? It’s so much more fun than calling myself a “customer service representative”. Then again , I have always been one to define people by what they love as opposed to what they do for a living. Although, sometimes the two coincide. When I was a grip, I stopped calling myself a musician for a while and starting calling myself a grip because I loooooooooooooved my job. But, alas, thanks to Governor Terminator down in California the film industry’s tanked up here, so I’m back to barfy jobs I refuse to define myself with.

Maybe it’s just a change in social consciousness - a renaissance kind of thing. We get to have one every couple hundred years, don’t we?

Proud aunties exclaim with delight “Ooh, look, she’s an artist!” when their 3-year-old nieces produce their first wobbly Crayola stick men, but they don’t squeal “Ooh! Look, he’s a construction worker!” when their nephews move dirt around the sandbox making engine noises. Maybe it’s because I have lots of proud aunties in my life still squealing about what a clever little musician I am and I’ve never heard a peep about data entry, but I have been calling myself a musician since it occured to me to call myself anything at all, so good luck changing my mind about the wisdom of this habit.

Warning: soppy sentimentality ahead

“what are your fears and trepidations?”

That, in the end, I won’t have showed the people I love how much I love them, on whatever terms they can understand, which sounds terribly Hallmark, but does happen to be true, I’m afraid.

Two women who have been very dear to me have died from cancer in what should have been their primes, and both were loving, wonderful, and wise people. From one, I learned that giving to those you love is the best form of getting there is, but you have to do it on terms you can deal with. From the other, I learned that if you don’t accept your own terms (and forgive yourself for them as well), you’ll end up with regrets that will make your ability to accept the inevitable (and by this I largely mean our own deaths) harder than it perhaps might be otherwise.

So if I have fears and trepidations, it’s mainly that I won’t have given enough or showed enough…love, as soppy as it sounds.

“What run-ins have you had with obsessives, individuals or organizations?”

One person’s interests are someone else’s obessions -- it’s mainly a matter of definition more than anything else. But I don’t think I can decide or tell someone else that they’re obsessed. That’s their own responsibility and their own choice. (I know people who are obsessed with caring for their kids in a world that makes that a very tough job indeed. Can I fault them for that obsession any more than someone who is so obsessed with music that they neglect their families?) All I can do is to decide whether I want to be around people on a case by case, and sometimes even moment by moment basis.
