Re: Doesn’t Jump at the Sun sound like a klezmer tune?

Pete Cooper gives an example of a Klezmer scale in his Eastern European Fiddle Tunes. He gives it as a guide to the player for the tune “Zemer Atik”.
The scale is:
G Ab Bnat C D Eb Fnat G’
The tune has the three flats for Cmin, but almost all of the time it’s Bnat that is used, the only exception in the tune being a struck chord of Eb-Bb-G’.
He also gives an example of an even stranger scale that is non-Klezmer, from Romania. It is:
A Cnat C# D# E F# Gnat,
but G# is used when it is the leading note to A.
Pete makes the point in the introduction to the book that familiarity with the relevant scale is essential for melodic improvisation.

“Klezmer music doesn’t use the harmonic minor scale (or for that matter, the major, natural minor, Dorian or Mixolydian scales either) - it has its own scales, which are unique to Klezmer…” (Mix O’Lydian)

The only Klezmer scale I know is the one described in the programme about Klezmer recently on UK TV (was it BBC4?). Mix O’Lydian is right to say it is distinct from the other scales, more familiar to us, that he lists. But this particular one is not unique to Klezmer - it is widely used in Greek music, and I am fairly confident it is used there across a variety of musical genres.

An octave in this particular scale - and I don’t know its name - goes, I think, like this:

In D:

D - Eb - F# - G - A - Bb - c# - d

In Bb, which I think is the most usual key in Klezmer:

Bb, - B, - D - Eb - F - F# - A - Bb

(Or: Bb - d - eb - f - f# - a - bb)

This scale is regarded very much as a feature and probably a creation of the cultures from Greece to points south and east - it may be a survival from the Byzantine era. In other words, ‘Eastern…’ I wonder how it became so central to Klezmer, whose players lived a long way up to the North and West of these cultures and among nations (Russia, Poland etc.) whose native music, as far as I know, is not very like this.

Klezmer was the music of Ashkenazi Jews whose language (Yiddish) was mostly German and whose main ties were not with the Mediterranean or the Ottoman Empire, the home ground of the Klezmer scale under discussion. The Jews who *did* live in these areas were Sephardic Jews, who spoke Ladino (derived from Spanish) - but as far as I’m aware, the two large Jewish groups had histories that were largely separate, at least in Eastern Europe. I may be mistaken in this last comment; but if I’m not, it could be the case that gypsies more than anybody else brought Eastern modes etc. up into the Ashkenazi areas. I assume gypsies travelled widely at the time between the various empires and musical / cultural zones.

Re: Doesn’t Jump at the Sun sound like a klezmer tune?

Yes and no.

There aren’t really any jigs in Klezmer. There are horas, usually notated in 6/8 or 3/8, but they are much slower, with a completely different rhythmic emphasis, and sound nothing like jigs. (In fact, the Romanian horas that they are derived from are in a kind of quintuple-ish time, but they’ve had some of the irregularity beaten out of them).

So, rhythmically, it doesn’t sound like Klezmer, but melodically, the 1st part bears more than a passing similarity to one particular tune, Choson Kale Mazel Tov (Congratulations to the Bride and Groom). In fact, if you’re not a Klezmer purist, they make a good tune pairing.

Compare:

T: Choson Kale Mazel Tov
L:1/8
M:4/4
K:Ddor
A,DF | A2A2^G2A2 | F>E D2 z A,DF | ^GAAA ^G2A2 | Bc A2 z AB^c | d2A2^G2A2 | F>E D2 z A,DF | ^GG2F A=GF2 FED2 | D5 :| etc.

T: Jump At The Sun
L:1/8
M:6/8
K:Ddor
DFA ^G2A | DFA ^G2A | dAA dAA | AGF E3 | DFA ^G2A | DFA ^G2A | dAA GAF | FGE D3 :| etc.

It is entirely possible that John Kirkpatrick heard the said klezmer tune at some point and it lodged in his subconscious, resurfacing one night, in an altogether more English form, at a pub in Clun, Shropshire.

Re: Doesn’t Jump at the Sun sound like a klezmer tune?

To expand on my earlier comments here are the four main modes (scales) that are used in Klezmer music:

1) Ahavo Rabboh - Phrygian dominant scale

2) Mi Shebeirach - similar (but not identical to) Dorian mode

3) Adoyn-y Moloch - similar (but not identical to) Mixolydian mode

4) Mogen Ovoys - simliar (but not identical to) Aolian mode (natural minor).

Re: Doesn’t Jump at the Sun sound like a klezmer tune?

@Azle Concertina

If you are playing tunes solo at home - or perhaps with friends - it doesn’t really matter how you choose to set them.

Likewise, if you are playing in a performance band - if the audience doesn’t like your tunes or how you set them, they just won’t pay to come and see you.

But a session is different. It’s a social occasion, and in most sessions you are expected to adhere to the unwritten “rules” of sessions - sometimes referred to as session etiquette.

If you choose to ignore these “rules” , at best you may invite hostile looks or comments from the other musicians - or at worst, you might be thrown out.

For example, if you were visiting a session for the very first time and decided to start the first set of the evening, that would be considered as extemely bad form.

It’s not customary to mix tune metres in one set (6/8 and 4/4 in this case), so that’s why you shouldn’t do it. Likewise its not customary to mix tunes of different genres in one set (in this case, English and Klezmer), so that’s not wise, either.

Very experienced or well-known musicians can occasionally get away with infringing session etiquette, but judging from your comments in this thread, it would appear that you are not in that category.

“I wonder how it became so central to Klezmer, whose players lived a long way up to the North and West of these cultures and among nations (Russia, Poland etc.) whose native music, as far as I know, is not very like this.”

I’m a bit late in on this - my computer’s packed up, so I’m using public computers when I can. I think there are two reasons why these ‘Eastern’ scales ares so central to Klezmer:

1. The Jews came from the Eastern Mediterranean, and there acquired what became the Synagogue Modes. In the early Middle Ages in that region, the Arabs were largely resposible for the teaching of music, so their take on music would have been prevalent among all the peoples in the area. On migration to Europe, the Jews generally lived in a degree of voluntary (or sometimes forced) isolation from the wider community, in particular, maintaining their religious traditions without much influence from outside. So the ‘Eastern’ modes persisted. Much of the Ashkenazic secular music (i.e. Klezmer) has its roots in the liturgical music (for which the names of the modes are evidence - they are all names of prayers that are tradtionally sung in those modes.)

2. The area inhabited by Ashkenazi Jews since about 15th Century (following expulsion from Germany) extended southwards to the Black sea, with large populations in Romania and the Ukraine. The Ottoman Empire, in its heyday, almost completely surrounded the Black Sea - and also included Hungary, which also had a very large Jewish Population. The Islamic influence is evident in the non-Jewsih traditional music of those countries, with augmented 4ths featuring heavily. In addition, some Jewish musicians from Romania and the Ukraine would have travelled southwards to work in major cities like Athens, Constantinople and Smyrna, picking up musical influences there. (The klezmer style of clarinet playing shares much in common with the Greek playing style.)

I hope that goes some way to answering the question.
