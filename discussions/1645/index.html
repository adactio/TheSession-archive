Republican/Nationalist Songs at Sessions

A mate of mine recently got p*ssed off at a session cos some drunken people started singing a couple of Republican anthems (I mean Irish Republican, not US)….(sorry, C!)

Personally, it doesn’t bother me too much as long as it’s just a couple at any one time - and the session doesn’t end up a drunken frenzied rabble (which I’ve been witness to, but didn’t happen on this occasion - and that’s coming from a non-Catholic!

I think it’s part of the deal, if you buy into Irish Music, and similarly with songs of a scottish nationalist nature (note, no Capital Letters, so as not to be confused with the SNP) sung north of the border from here.

I feel if you’re going to play the instrumental music of these countries, you should be prepared to put up with at least a couple of songs regarding the national aspirations of those countries, particularly as they are small countries next to a former imperial power.

Also, occasionally, just played as instrumentals, they can be nice, eg The Galty Mountain Boy(?) as a Waltz, is a fine tune.

Having said that, it is a shame these songs often get associated with groups whose aim is to use violent means to achieve their ends. Maybe people feel that alone is adequate justication to have them censored from sessions, particularly in the West of Scotland or the North of Ireland.

Any views?

Danny.

When I was in Cork City (where my wife comes from) a couple of years ago I wandered into a pub near the docks one Saturday night. It was one of those pubs with ceilings about 5 foot 10 inches, dark woodwork everywhere, a light level that would have given a decent digital videocam a problem, and rooms and corridors that seemed to go on for ever back into the darkness. In one of the side rooms a gig by a banjo and a box was entertaining a packed audience.
As the night wore on the players were joined by a singer who furthered the entertainment with rousing old rebel songs. It was not for nothing that Cork was known as the “rebel county” and in the context of that late-night pub in that Cork dockside location these songs were perfectly natural and in place, but, in today’s political climate, harmless. A good time was had by all, and the beer was pretty good, too. However, in some parts of the Island of Ireland, I think it would be a very brave or foolhardy person who would sing some of those ballads in public.

Well, I’m American born, and relatively new to ITM, but I felt a need to share my recent experience with this topic.

I sent off for a cd that I had purchased with the intent of learning one particular song, just the one. It arrived the day before Easter Sunday of this year, and I only took the time to listen to the one cut I was intent on learning. It wasn’t until the next morning that I decided to hear the rest of the cd as background while I was filling the Easter baskets. The cd only got as far as the second cut, the melody of which so enticed me that I stopped with the candy and went back to replay that second song. Mind you, it was only the melody line that captured me, I wasn’t listening to the lyrics at all.
While the second cut replayed itself I finished the Easter baskets and decided, well, I’m going to learn that song as well, whatever it is.
I took the title (which offered no hint as to the message of the song) and searched online for the lyrics. I was, and still am, totally blown away, to find out that the song that so captured me was written about the Easter uprising of 1916, “The Foggy Dew”. What a coincidence!

I have learned the song (as I knew I HAD to, it spoke to me, even before I knew what it was about). And when I sing it, it’s very emotional, but in a prayerful way. It’s just one of those tragically beautiful songs that makes the hair on my arms stand up.

I think these types of songs belong in my repertoire right next to the lively, tap-your-toes-and-smile reels/jigs/hornpipes that I also love dearly. It’s all ITM to me, it’s what makes it the music I love.

Re: Republican/Nationalist Songs at Sessions

Hmm. This is a meaty issue is it not? I have to say I draw a line between jigs/reels/hornpipes/average-session-songs and rebel songs.

I’ll start by making it quite clear that I am English with an interest in Irish culture, and am very definitely “anti-imperialist” in my political views (I study dying languages of oppressed indigenous peoples).

But to me it’s this simple: dance tunes and non-political songs are all about going out to a session and having a great time, whilst also celebrating a wonderful traditional culture and the emotional (and patriotic!) content of the music in an appropriately neutral way. Rebel songs, on the other hand, bring politics into the equation. People’s views differ on politics. There’s not necessarily a right and wrong by definition - just a huge grey area. So if rebel songs are sung in a session, it’s not about people going out and having a good time and making nice music, it’s about going out, getting drunk and potentially winding people up who might have different political views from your own.

The Paddy’s Day before last, I was playing at an Irish pub in Sydney, and we had this nice little session going in the corner. The mix of musos was probably mostly Aussie, and maybe one or two Irish-born, and then me. Later on in the evening people were at an advanced stage of drunkenness, and the rebel songs started. There was a bunch of “heavies” sitting at a table at the back, pretty much roaring their songs over the top of what we were doing. I later judged from their accents that they were Aussie, maybe (or maybe not) of Irish descent. I found myself thinking “here I am, an English person in Australia, playing the music that I enjoy which belongs to a culture I love and respect, and what do I get for it? I get it thrown straight back in my face with a bunch of heavily anti-English songs. Whilst I sympathise with the historical context behind these songs, I do think there’s a time and a place. Why should I sit there and be on the receiving end of that from a group of people who probably only go to an Irish pub on Paddy’s Day for the sole purpose of sending out a message in their songs that they themselves probably don’t fully understand the nuances of?”

So, I don’t mind that these songs exist, and if people want to sing them because that’s the political message they want to express, then that’s fine by me. However, if people are going to sing them at sessions I attend, then I’ll make sure not to be there.

Re: Republican/Nationalist Songs at Sessions

It’s a bit off-topic, I suppose, but this thread reminded me of a trip to Ireland a couple of years ago. We were staying in Fermanagh, near the Marble Arch caves with a group of cavers, and one day my fifteen year old son Elliott and I escaped for a day’s fishing. The trip to the lake we wanted to fish took us through Ballyconnell, where we saw a sign advertising some sort of festival that very day. We spent the day fruitlessly thrashing the Lough to foam without raising a single fish (I think the Germans had got there first), and decided to stop off and check out the Festival on the way back. We arrived in Ballyconnell in the early evening, but all was quiet, so we stuck our heads in the first pub we came to, ordered a Guinness and asked the landlord whether we might find any music that evening. He got very animated at this and assured us we’d come to the right place, and that his pub was THE place for the music in Ballyconnell, and how in an hours time there was to be a performance on the trailer by his pub, for which he was providing the electricity. He said that would be followed by a Mummers play, and that there would also be some mighty music in his pub, which we must come back and listen to. He wouldn’t be pressed on what this music was though, he just said it was “Everything you could want, all sorts of music..”, which didn’t really sound too promising.
So we went off and enjoyed an Irish/Chinese take away in the steamy environs of our car, and then headed back to the trailer. The street around it was a sea of schoolgirls (steady now..), all dressed up to the nines. They massed under umbrellas in the drizzle and the little group playing on the stage were obviously their classmates, helped out by an old man with a box. It was very pleasant, but after a while the damp started to seep through me and I felt the need of something to wash down the aftertaste of the Chinese. I dragged young Lochinvar away from the Sea Of Earthly Delights (I think all those girls actually frightened him not a little) and in to the pub. The landlord greeted us like long lost friends and insisted we go straight through to ‘the other bar’, where he said the music was about to start. My worries were confirmed when I saw the small stage in the corner, covered with electronic devices, but given that we had popped our heads into every other pub we could find on our way to eat, and no-one had told us of anything else, and that we couldn’t stay that late anyway, not without our caving chums assuming that leviathan had dragged us into the lough (some bloody hope!). So we decided to stay. I sat Elliott down on a bench along the wall and went to get the beer. The whole bar was a sort of Republican shrine. The walls and ceilings were covered with photos of Republican heroes, memorabilia, guns, other weapons, flags (tattered and otherwise), and a bullet-pocked sign stolen from a border crossing post. I sat back down and the music arrived. It arrived in the form of a man with a guitar who sang rebel songs. These songs were augmented by the massive array of special effects the guitar was plugged into, which could add the sound of an accordion (well something like the sound of an accordion), or a rather disconcerting delayed banjo effect, and a whole range af miliataristic drum effects. We listened to this strange noise with appalled fascination, but gradually became aware that instead of the singer, WE appeared to be the main focus of attention in the room. Everytime anyone walked in they would stop and stare at us. I would nod a greeting out of politeness, and they would look a little puzled and go and take their place at the bar. Other people at the bar and other tables kept looking at us as well. I couldn’t understand it. It was not as though it was unusual for their to be strangers in the bars here, half the other bars we’d been into were full of English anglers. Then the penny dropped - we were the only males in the bar with hair longer than a couple of millimetres. Elliott had very long, thick hair which cascaded over his shoulders. We did look a little out of place. So I ignored the looks and listened to the singer. The surreality of his act was compounded by the way he introduced each song in a rather incongruous cabaret style, “And now, specially for Mike at the bar, I hope your wife is out of hospital soon Mike - and I know all the ladies love this one, it’s My Little Armalite”, at which the Snare drum effect and auto-banjo kicked in and he was away, to appreciative murmers from the bar. It was so bad it was actually very entertaining, in a kitsch sort of way, and we got chatting to a nice lady who’s son was in Willesden, so was virtually our next door neighbour (We live half way between Reading and Oxford!). I decided on one more before we left and fought my way to the now crowded bar.
As I returned, drinks in hand, I suddenly saw why they had been staring at us. On the wall, immediately above Elliott’s head, was a framed tribute to the Hunger Strikers. Slap bang in the middle was a large photo of the long haired Bobby Sands, looking the spitting image of the young lad sitting below it. So striking was the resemblance that I nearly dropped my pint, and I burst out laughing. The punters in the pub must have thought they were looking at a ghost.
We stayed for a couple more songs and went out to watch the mummers play - now that WAS weird!

Last Night’s Fun

Carson has a great eye and ear for the weird and wonderful.

My favourite story in the whole book is the one about “The Shadow” … with apologies to Carson, I reprint it here. People who haven’t read Last Night’s Fun can then see why I suggested that Mark’s story had a similar surreal ring …

I never met The Shadow, but I heard these things about him.

No one I have asked is exactly sure why The Shadow was so-called. Some claim he shadowed the fiddle-player Sean McGuire. Others deny this; he was a fiddle-player in his own right. Some attribute it to his long thin height and his dapper dude rancher style of dressing.

The Shadow painted a copy of the Sistine Chapel ceiling on his ceiling.

Some believe The Shadow just appeared, without having gone through the motions of getting there.

The Shadow reputed to be the best hitch-hiker in Ireland. Dressed in cowboy hat and boots, a guitar case slung over one shoulder and a fiddle case slung over the other, he presented an intriguing figure to the passing traffic.

Often it was felt that the session lacked a certain je ne sais quoi without The Shadow’s presence. People looked for him when he wasn’t there.

It put me in mind, somehow, of the late Mickey Golly of Colmcille, who I was told was a great fiddle-player. The only time I met him, in the Sliabh Liag Hotel in Carrick, he was playing “air” or “poteen” fiddle: playing with no fiddle and no bow. But the shapes he made convinced me.

The Shadow, according to one source, was also “a very loud guitar-player and at times could be mistaken for a lightly played piano”.

The Shadow was invited to be present at the only meeting of the great fiddle-player John Doherty and the great box-player Joe Cooley. This took place in O’Beirne’s Hotel in Carrick, and I’m trying to establish if this was, in fact, a former name for the Sliabh Liag. During lulls between tunes, The Shadow would take up his guitar and launch into his repertoire of cowboy songs. Among his favourites were “The Yellow Rose Of Texas” and “The Red River Valley”. John Doherty was reputed to have been much taken by The Shadow’s rendition of the American fiddle tunes “The Orange Blossom Special” and “The Mocking Bird”, and learned them then and there. The trio - Doherty, Cooley and The Shadow - played for three days and three nights, and each night was better than the last. The year was 1952.

The Shadow was the pseudonym of Sean McLoughlin of Armoy, County Antrim.

The Shadow’s last request was to be buried with his boots on, wearing a white tuxedo with a half-bottle of Bushmills in the inside pocket.

Ciaran Carson 1996, from Last Night’s Fun
