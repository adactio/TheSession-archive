Gatekeeping culture in ITM

Hello again sessioners.

I was at a recent festival in a certain west Clare town when I discovered more evidence of a certain trend I have been noticing in the trad community recently. It seems that there is an unwillingness to share certain “limited edition” recordings of great music, that people may have. It feels like a purposeful attempt to gatekeep good music from a wider audience. This comes in the form of older recordings (mainly bootleg) of masters in live performances that only a select few people have access to.

I feel this creates an elite class that shouldn’t exist in the community. A sort of, “ you’re not good enough to hear the good stuff” attitude. It also gives off the impression that it’s all about who you know in the scene (however true this is!)
Now of course I know that this is an oral tradition and sometimes it’s better to put down the phone and just listen to what your hearing in person but I feel this is an issue of attitude towards beginners or the masses. If you take a look at the piping community as an example you’ll find the complete opposite. In my experience every piper is keen on showing you the rare recordings they have if they have some and the vast majority of brilliant piping is right on display for all in the form of NPU archives.

Overall I think this is not a major issue I’m just curious as to what your opinions may be.

Not quite the same form of ‘gatekeeping’ but I think there is a widespread and diffuse tendency to define what ITM is and more importantly what it isn’t and then get very exercised when people don’t recognise the rigid boundaries. Examples might be which tune types, which actual tunes, which composers, which players, which instruments are part of ‘the tradition’ or which ways people should learn, whether and how they should be accompanied and so on. There are probably thousands of different positions of these boundaries sketched out in the minds of many of us. We all generate our own angst. My musical ‘journey’ has been a succession of recognising and letting go of these boundaries in my own mind

Re: Gatekeeping culture in ITM

One or two different points for discussion here re “gate keeping” in general.

I think it’s strange that there should be restrictions on who to should listen to old recordings of the music etc. Sometimes, it’s done for commercial reasons e.g. the “Celtic Music” saga where the owners of same resisted demands to re-release same on CD etc.

Also, some academic institutions have strict rules rules re access to rarer material etc. However, I think things are becoming more relaxed now in that respect and more people get to this material if they are really interested.

On a more informal level, we have always had “gate keepers”… e.g. “this is so and so’s set” etc. I wouldn’t dream of starting certain sets in some sessions so as not to upset some of the regulars. Anywhere else, it might be fair game though.

There are also some tunes and songs we know that we’d normally not start in a session situation for various reasons. possibly because they won’t “fit in” but we also like to keep a few of our favourites for ourselves and other situations. At the really absurd end of my musical world, I even have some favourite ukulele tunes and songs which I wouldn’t wish my local Ukulele group to hear or spoil. :-)

It’s always been the case that musicians and singers have respected the “ownership”(not strictly true as most are traditional) and right to perform certain songs and tunes in different situations as opposed to banning them from performing them altogether. The latter would be impossible. For instance, the late Sheila Stewart would never sing certain songs when her mother Belle was still alive but she did so later.

I think there’s still many similar examples in both the singing and instrumental worlds today. In my opinion, that’s not necessarily a bad thing. It’s all about respect.

Re: Gatekeeping culture in ITM

If the recording was a formerly commercially-available one, or similarly well-distributed but now hard-to-find, then that’s one thing.

But coming from the world of ethnomusicology, there are a whole host of thorny issues involved in private and research recordings that complicate sharing them with anyone who asks. We are very used to people just recording everything and posting it online now, and in a modern sense maybe there’s implied consent whenever you see someone whip out their phone for a video or a picture. But it is still important to have permission to share any recordings you make, and in the case that the subject of the recording is long-dead, it’s incumbent on the person holding the recording to make a judgement as to whether it’s appropriate to share.

There are recordings making the rounds of long-ago sessions where someone threw a tape recorder on the table. Did those players know that whatever they did, mistakes and all, would be plastered all over the internet decades later? What about a teacher who allows a student to record their playing in a lesson as an example, should they be OK with that recording being out under their name? “Gatekeeping” in this context might simply mean being respectful to the person who is being recorded.

Re: Gatekeeping culture in ITM

ITMA’s policy is to make the collection as widely available as possible, while balancing preservation, copyright, and available resources.

This means:

- Free public access on-site: Anyone can visit the archive and consult its collections in person.

- Remote access where possible: ITMA provides online catalogues and has digitised large parts of its collections, although not everything can be made available online because of copyright restrictions and resource limitations.

- Copyright compliance: Even when ITMA holds an item, it cannot necessarily publish it online or distribute copies if copyright is still in force or permissions have not been obtained.

- Preservation first: Access is managed in ways that protect unique archival materials from damage, which is why some items may only be consulted under supervised conditions or in digital form.

If you turn up at Merrion Square, you can essentially view or listen to everything. As I’ve done several times.

Not all their holdings are online, mainly because of funding. They’re acutely aware this disadvantages those without ready access to Dublin. Again it’s my experience that they do what they can to answer specific requests via email and over the phone.

If you’re interested in making a donation here’s the form: https://www.itma.ie/donate/
