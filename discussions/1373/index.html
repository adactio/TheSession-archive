Putting the Shamrock back together.

Please excuse the generalisations within this question.
Has anyone else noticed how the culture of Ireland is seggregated into three different camps? Step dancers won’t dance to the music, singers won’t sing to the music and musicians rarely play for either of the other two. It is a rare occurence that singing (including the telling of stories and legend/history), dancing to music other than piano accordian driven drivel, (no offense - some of my best friends play for feis’s - or is that fishes? :), and purveyors of fine tunes on assorted instruments get together and give each other time and space and respect in the same place at the same time.
We all seem to be “happy” contained within our own fields. Surely this is not the way it always was.
I know a lot of blame can be layed at the doorstep of invasion and the colonialism of the last few centuries and the division and declarations that our culture “must be crushed under the heel of the oppressor’s boot”.
But are we perpetuating the “old divide and rule”?
Should we strive towards a better understanding of our heritage and culture…or just be content to amuse ourselves and each other playing tunes in the corner of dark bars…?

Oh noooooooooooooooooooooooooooooo. I can sense one of those discussions about to happen. I shall hold my tongue, for a while anyway.

Well okay I’ll say something and then hold my tongue. I think that a lot of people who play Irish music mistake their love of the actual music for an understanding of the culture, particularly those who did not grow up in Ireland and “live” it. Coming at Irish music from that perspective is like speaking a foreign language - you might be fluent but people will always be able to recognise you as a foreigner. I think that a lot of people with a genuine love for the music actually just don’t get what it *is* and what it *means*. Music like language is part of your identity, and identity theory is very complex. I’m pretty sure the feeling of euphoria that someone from another culture gets when they play a really good tune is very different from the feeling that someone from that culture gets playing the same tune; both good feelings, just very different, and difficult to put your finger on.

I do think that some people need to strive towards a better understanding of heritage and culture, and that it’s particularly important for musicians who live in the US, Canada, Australia, etc. I’ve noticed how people in Australia view the music they play and have found it really interesting. It’s almost as though people here are privately seeking a purity in the tradition that does not exist, striving to link the music with their own culture and identity because they feel some sort of emotional need to do so. There are some fantastic players here, but I really think it’s much more about “amusing ourselves and each other playing in the corner of dark bars” than gaining a full understanding of the culture as a whole, and I think you’ve noticed that too, Greenwiggle.

I don’t know whether the actual music itself is affected by all of this, i.e. to what extent the music is a “cultural” thing, and to what extent it is a “human” thing. I don’t think the music suffers for it particularly - in fact it might even have the opposite effect because people feel free to pick and choose the bits of culture they like and ignore the rest. So naff tunes tend to get sifted out. That “feeling” has nothing to do with the actual pattern of the notes and probably little to do with dynamics. However, I think there’s a lot to be gained on a personal level from seeking more than that, something that is linked with the music that is personally enriching but is not the same as a love for the music itself - words aren’t explaining very well what I mean.

Re: Putting the Shamrock back together.

May I go back to the original question of why the “culture of Ireland” is segregated into three different camps? It has nothing to do with imperialism, colonic irrigation, globalisation etc. etc. It’s to do with the modern lifestyle. Before the advent of television, and Irish theme pubs, people had to create their own entertainment at home. Sessions didn’t happen in pubs. In rural Ireland people would get together at weddings, funerals, house-dances etc when music, singing, dancing and story telling would take place. My mother would talk about going “on our ceili”. This simply meant going on a visit to a friend or neighbour’s house and is the original meaning of the word. In some parts, certain houses would be well known as “ceili” houses or music houses where a welcome was guaranteed for musicians and singers.

This sort of thing is unfortunately a rarity now. Step dancers are mostly unable to dance to live music as they are accustomed to dancing to the Wurlitzer electronic accordian with built in drum-machine and auto-arranger and some think that River Dance is Irish music. Singers rarely have a chance in a noisy pub environment. But it is not all doom and gloom. I have been to (and thrown) parties where sets were danced, tunes were played, sings were sung, jokes were cracked, tea and buns consumed (OK I made that bit up!) and a good time was had by all! So organise your own session at home!

Re: Putting the Shamrock back together.

I thought I might put my oar in- I think it’s important to differentiate between “step dancers” and people who dance “sets”. Irish step dancing seems more oriented towards competition and the gaining of medals, etc., while the kind of dancing people used to do at house parties, “cedhli dancing” if you will, is very condusive to live music and does’nt require musicians who play for it to stick to an exact speed in beats per minute.
BTW, Tony DiMarco once told me that back in the ’50’s in New York it was quite common to have live music at step dancing practice and competitions, often provided by the likes of Andy McGann and Paddy Reynolds! Back then dancers were not in the habit of waving metronomes at musicians and saying " would you play this at 120 bpm?
Also don’t forget that the barroom sesiun is a relatively recent phenomenon in Irish Music. I think the fact that pubs are more well suited for some aspects of the tradtion (music and to some extent singing) than to others such as storytelling, “lies”, dancings “sets” with groups of dancers rather than solo, etc., has caused some of these wonderful things to get pushed into the background. However they still tend to resurface in the context of house parties.

Oh, we still have live music at the feisianna, Murph, although it’s actually rather hard to find a musician who wants to come and play for classes. Modern Irish stepdancing under An Coimisiun (modern Munster style) is indeed more competition oriented, and a lot of us dancers and teachers (and even some of the ADCRGs) think that’s a real shame to the art form. But the sean-nos and other styles of Irish stepdancing are still more about the dancing than the athleticism, and most stepdancers do enjoy getting up and dancing to whatever music is playing -- the better, more experienced dancers know how to adjust the step to the speed (the faster the music goes, the simpler the step must be in order to dance it well).

Re: Putting the Shamrock back together.

In my experience musicians want to play tunes, dancers want to lep about and singers want to sing.

In a session here you can expect, from time to time, that a dancer will get up and jig about or a singer or two will be invited to sing, with the musicians usually calling order for the singer.

BUT you can hardly expect there to be an even division between the three because we all want to do our own thing.

In my experience singers in particular need to have a completely different venue from the musicians since they will all want to do their numbers and perhaps learn from the rest. You can’t really do that in a noisy pub. A classic example is Johnny Joes in Cushendall where the back room is reserved for the singers [and perhaps one or two musicians to play the odd tune by way of interlude] while the session is in full swing in the front room or the kitchen.

Everybody gets to do their thang without annoying the rest. Seems like a good compromise to me?

Set dancers are another breed entirely - water swilling athletes who will lather up a sweat and dance to the wee hours. Even if there was enough room in the pub for them to dance they would insist on dancing the whole bloody night - no thank you!!!

I go to the session for tunes and the odd song, my wife loves nothing better than a ceili or set ceili, my kids love to take part in competition dancing, and sometimes we put it all together at a house session but there’s no harm in them being in their own separate spaces.
