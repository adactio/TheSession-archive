The Perversion of Quantity ~ in search for an old saying…

I was chatting with a friend, a respected musician and valued character, and he was telling me again, if by mere suggestion, about his massive repertoire ~ all the tunes played in the sessions he’s attended lately, and regularly around his area, and by association ~ all the many, many, many tunes he’s had time with or can pull out of a hat, or at least in some form out of his various instruments… (‘various instruments’? :-/) As a contrast he also mentioned some folks that ‘only’ knew say around 100 tunes, ‘beginners’. And, that has been bugging me ever since, that and the fact I didn’t have any alcohol at the time and he was enjoying a pleasant float brought on by vodka. Worse, he doesn’t even like vodka.

Anyway, WHY? My wife smiled and lilted an old Irish march in time to our stroll, a knowing smile, as we walked the backwoods about here in the snow fall and I told here I wanted this old saying and couldn’t remember it, something along the lines of meaning that too much of a good thing leads to abuse, neglect, to taking things for granted. Is it that ‘plenty’ is a curse ~ something like that? That’s where I got stuck, with ‘A Perversion of Quantity’, but that ain’t it. Maybe one of you will know. But as I was struggling and feeling the usual frustration, trying to shake loose this well known phrase, and I know I know it but just can’t pull it out, I started with “remember~” and that’s when she smiled and said “I know what you’re going to say!”

We had this acquaintance who used to regularly chase me up, including to ask who he should look up when he finally had his holidays in Ireland. The subject was the same thing everytime, no variety, and it was always about what he could get from me, very limited… ;-) He finally went to Ireland and after just two weeks there came back an ‘expert’. His main interest was in songs. I’d given him some folks to look up. After this sojourn he again came looking for me, knocking on our door, to regale us with his exploits and new found expertise. I asked him somewhere when he took a break enough to let me ~ “Did you go see so-and-so?” His response was “Yes, but he only knew 30 songs.” That was that! Things went downhill from there including my interest and I was glad to puncture his bubble and not have him chasing me up as much anymore. Besides, he’d come by and pretty much ignored someone significant to me and my life ~ my wife. He wouldn’t be the first or the last to make that mistake. I haven’t much patience for that kind of shallow single-mindedness. Also irritating was this new found expertise and this attitude that quantity was the great determiner of value. I knew the singer I’d directed him to. I knew his generosity and spirit and how good he was, a brilliant singer with an amazing way with those 30 or so songs. I also knew that he’d sung tons to get to his chosen ‘few’, though I wouldn’t call 30 or so songs ‘few’…

This same thing seems to plague us as musicians. What is most important, to know a few tunes well and intimately, or to know tons in a half-baked session sort of way? I started thinking back on some of the source musicians I’d had the pleasure of knowing, and sorry, no, THEY DID NOT KNOW TONS!!! They had a lovely set of tunes that represented their region and themselves and their history and associations. It wasn’t thousands. In some cases it wasn’t even 100, the core that they valued and kept and played repeatedly… So what is this obsession and obsessing with quantity? I still don’t think that the two Qs go well together ~ quantity and quality. I just can’t accept that ‘more is better’…

So, anyone out there know the old saying / phrase / parable I’m trying to remember, or any similar ones, along the lines that more and having a lot is a curse or some kind of perversion or damnation? And while we’re on this subject ~ ;-)

This probably isn’t the saying you’re looking for, but I think there are parallels. Once, when my life was so full of doing so many things, and good things mind you, I reached a point of exhaustion. A minister and good friend of mine said “Larry, even a good tree needs pruning”.

He really emphasized the basics, and I am grateful he did.

It truly is the issue of keeping the cart behind the horse, imho. I do know what you mean about people bypassing the wife. I don’t think many workers, painters, and contractors have not gotten our business because of that.

Re: The Perversion of Quantity ~ in search for an old saying…

This is what I love to read. I have to admit, as I’d intended to start this, that I realize my own guilt in posing the question. When something affects me as our friend’s sharing did, I become introspective, go looking for the wound it irritated, in the dark corners of my own experience and ways. I haven’t stopped that and more thoughts have come up in my search for this phrase / parable…

How ‘many’ ______ (tunes / twiddles / dances / steps / songs / whatever) can we know?

What do we mean by ‘know’?

Quantity is easily quantifiable, it can be counted.

Quality? ~ not so easy to pin down to a single figure, leat alone a few figures that can be added up in a neat column…

Maybe that is why we choose the easier option, to value our involvement in this tradition or anything by what can be measured easily, rather than by the more difficult to measure ‘skill’ and ‘understanding’ we have with regards to things?

What do we mean by ‘know’?

The scale of knowledge, of knowing and understanding can can be from non-existant to shallow and superficial ~ as Llig might aptly put it, akin to those who know the dots, the notes, like midi knows the notes, like paint-by-numbers… But even in paint-by-numbers there can be subtlety and some measure of skill, if more subjective ~ the brush strokes, differences in quality ~ and there’s the tools in hand, brushes, paint, canvas… But this slippery thing ‘knowledge’ and ‘understanding’ on the other end can deep and intimate and becomes more difficult to measure ~ not just ‘tradition’, but a single tune / melody, and instrument, and the context of or either or both. It is as difficult to concieve of how one would measure humour, something I also think is an integral part of this tradition. Who would we choose to measure it, but what measures?

Quantity is easily measurable whereas quality is not… There are really no variables in the number of tunes, but the variables in our understanding and skill with it all, or with any one melody, is full of crazy variables.

I still haven’t shaken that phrase / parable free yet. I’m sure I’ve seen something similar in both Irish and Welsh too… :-/

I don’t have a copy of Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance any more and I couldn’t begin to put Pirsig’s thought into my own words - so - with a bit of cut and paste and editing, here’s a smidgeon of it from Wikipedia.

Pirsig’s metaphysics of quality
From Wikipedia

“Quality”, or “value” as described by Pirsig, cannot be defined because it empirically precedes any intellectual constructions. It is the “knife-edge” of experience, known to all. Everything (including mind, ideas and matter) is a product and a result of Quality.
Static and Dynamic qualities
- two forms: Static quality patterns (patterned) and Dynamic Quality (unpatterned).
- It is important to note that Pirsig is not proposing a duality: Quality is one, yet manifests itself differently.

Dynamic Quality includes everything not static. Dynamic Quality is the force of change in the universe; when this aspect of Quality becomes habitual or customary it becomes static. Pirsig called Dynamic Quality “the pre-intellectual cutting edge of reality” because it can be recognized before one can think about it. This means that Quality lies in the moment we sense anything during the instantaneous present; with a short delay we give this impression a static form by describing it as emotion, thing, word etc. These static forms, if they have enough good or bad quality, are given names and are interchanged with other people, building the base of knowledge for a culture. It also explains that the Dynamic beauty of a piece of music can be recognized before reading any static analysis explaining why the music is beautiful."

Poor Pirsig later had a nervous breakdown, likely in part due to his intense introspection and thought processes.

We all do recognize quality if we stop to think about it or- even if we don’t think about it.

This is from John Taylor Gatto, about how he learned sensitivity and morality in his boyhood :

"On the way up Third Street hill to Waverly school each morning to discover what song Miss Wible was going to have kids memorize that day, I would pass a shack made of age-blackened hemlock, the kind you see on old barns long gone in disrepair. This shack perched at the edge of an otherwise empty double lot grown wild in burdock, wild hollyhock, and briar. I knew the old woman who lived there as Moll Miner because boys tormented her by shouting that name as they passed in the daily processional headed for school. I never actually saw her until one Saturday morning when, for want of anything better to do, I went to shoot birds.

I had a Red Ryder BB rifle, Moll Miner’s lot had birds, and so lying on my belly as if birds were wild Indians, I shot one. As it flopped around dying, the old woman ran shrieking from her shack to the fallen bird, raised it to bosom and then fled shouting, “I know who you are. You’re the printer’s boy. Why did you kill it? What harm did it do to you?” Then overcome with sobs she disappeared into her shack.

Her wild white hair and old cotton housedress, light grey with faded pink roses, lingered in my vision after I went home. Who could answer such a question at eight or at twenty-eight? But being asked made me ask it of myself. I killed because I wanted to. I killed for fun. Who cared about birds? There were plenty of birds. But then, what did it mean, this crazy old lady taking the downed bird into her home? She said she knew me; how was that possible? It was all very puzzling. I found myself hoping the BB hadn’t really killed the bird but only shocked it. I felt stupid and tried to put the incident out of my mind. A week or so later I got rid of my BB gun, trading it for an entrenching tool and some marbles. I told myself I was tired of it; it wasn’t a real gun anyway. Around Halloween some kids were planning a prank on the old lady. I protested, saying we should pick on someone who could fight back and chase us. “We shouldn’t pick on weak people,” I said. “Anyway, that lady’s not crazy, she’s very kind.”

That winter, without asking, I shoveled the snow around her house. It was a business I usually did for pocket money, and I was good at it, but I didn’t even ask permission. I just shoveled the sidewalk without asking for money. She watched me from her window without saying a word.

Whether she recognized I was the boy who shot the bird, I wish I could tell you, but that’s all there is. Not a sparrow falls, they say. That was the way I learned to care about moral values in Monongahela—by rubbing shoulders with men and women who cared about things other than what money bought, although they cared about money, too. I watched them. They talked to me. Have you noticed nobody talks to children in schools? I mean, nobody. All verbal exchanges in school are instrumental. Person-to-person stuff is contrary to policy. That’s why popular teachers are disliked and fired. They talk to kids. It’s unacceptable."

The theory is that humans have evolved to have a very highly developed ability to think and solve problems. They have an awareness of emotions (‘gut feeling’, fear, sorrow, grief, joy, etc,) but often cannot understand their own feelings or pick up on the feelings of others.

Horses, by contrast, have very little intellectual or problem solving ability, but have a much more highly developed and sophisticated ‘emotional intelligence’. They receive, and accurately assess, the feelings of people and other horses almost instantly, by means which are not entirely clear or well understood, but the word telepathy covers the territory.

The reason for this is that they are highly social herd animals. They have to spend almost the entire 24 hours grazing to get enough to eat, because they cannot store food (like sheep or cattle) so thaat means most of their lives, their head is down in the grass, with their eyes about a foot higher, which is a crappy position to be in if there’s a lion creeping up on you. The advantage they have is that if one of the herd, of say, a hundred, is frightened or attacked, it sends out some sort of emotional or telepathic signal that alerts all others immediately, and they all gallop off without waiting to find out the danger.

Sadly, horses are often abused and mistreated. Linda Kohanov discovered, more or less by accident, that horses can somehow perceive the emotional distress of humans who have been similarly abused. It is like some kind of magic, but I know from my own experience, that it works. I got the first clues from seeing ‘riding for the disabled’. Ponies which were normally very stroppy and difficult would let disabled children be put onto their backs and take the greatest care of them. Quite astonishing.

I’ve seen videos of Linda Kohanov’s methods. They are deeply moving. People break down into tears almost immediately at the catharsis of standing with an animal which understands exactly how you feel, and fills your whole inner being with a healing empathetic vibe.

I guess this might have something to do with sessions, although I havn’t given it any thought, because musical is an emotional, not an intellectual, communication.

Re: The Perversion of Quantity ~ in search for an old saying…

Some more reasons to learn as many tunes as you can:

Our sessions, because they are friendly and welcoming, are attended by all levels of players. My experience, anyway, is that I can keep everyone interested from the masters to the beginners by knowing the tunes that each individual knows and loves. I can start Lucy Farr’s with relish when the beginner looks to be tuning out since I know that beginner knows that one and I know it, too. Yet, I can follow up with Whiskey before Breakfast at a good clip when an accomplished player needs to be challenged. Knowing the tunes that each individual knows helps make a rollicking evening for all.

We can start our session at 5:30pm and play til 2am and not run out of tunes.

When visitors show up, if we know enough tunes, there will be a large intersection of known tunes.

Quantity is VERY important.
