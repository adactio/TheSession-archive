Nothing wrong with percussion as such but it’s a bit more than just “joining in”.

You really need to know or familiarise yourself with the music to make a worthwhile contribution. Some here even suggest that you have to also know each individual tune either by memory or on another instrument before attempting to accompany same. That’s not my view although I believe you should be familiar with its general structure at least.

Also, to do the above, you need to learn to play with other people and familiarise yourself with the music before you start to actually play the percussion bits yourself in a session. So, the “surprise” element would not be so easy whereas with the whistle it will be easier to try things out on your own with the aid of recordings, music sheets, and tutorials of course as you see fit.

Re: Beginners guide

The tin whistle is 100% the best on-boarding route for a beginner. The mechanics of how to play notes is as simple as it gets. This lets you concentrate reasonably quickly on learning some tunes, rhythms (jigs, reels, hornpipes, polkas etc.). It is the path that perhaps as many as 70-80% of beginner Irish traditional musicians take. A basic whistle costs small money <20 £$€ or so. And a good whistle is probably no more than €100. But the basic whistle will get you comfortably through a lot of your early learning.

It is all about the time - your effort level, commitment, practice. And, I think also, getting a teacher who will guide you through the initial stages. That could be in-person (best) or online. But it definitely helps to have some level of directed learning.

You will progress slowly with 1 hour or less per week; moderately with 2-4 hours (between classes and practice)… but if you can give the whistle 1-hour per day 5-days per week (or more) you are likely to advance rapidly. The dangers with self teaching are poor technique (perfecting bad habits/mistakes), failing to make the early progress commensurate with the level of effort and simply missing out on the encouragement, motivation from a teacher who can guide you the early attainment levels.

I was very lucky when I started learning in having a Comhaltas branch nearby and a wonderful teacher with whom I learned for 7 years. I’m pretty sure had I not had that structure (and all the support that being a member of an active community of trad musicians offer), my interest and progress would have waned and I might never have accumulated the tunes and opportunities that I did.

With regard to percussion .. the obvious choice is Bodhrán and, unfortunately, some folks go that route as a path of least resistance thinking it must be easier. But in reality playing rhythm PROPERLY is actually quite a challenge - at least the equal of learning a melody instrument. A learner on a melody instrument quickly finds places where they are able to join in… a percussionist who is less than excellent (steady rhythm with nice variation) can be a challenge to play alongside. This isn’t a discouragement to give it a go.. especially if you already have good percussion (drumming) skills… but the idea that it is an easy pathway to welcome participation should be dispelled.

In any case, good backers for Irish trad are expected to KNOW the melodies and to shape the backing to suit the arc of the tune (both in terms of rhythm and chord selection). Irish Traditional Music performance is (predominantly) a melodic form - the tune (melody) is preeminent (versus accompaniment, harmonising or rhythm). Obviously when you are looking at advanced level musicians in performance/recording scenarios - this is a different scenario and all bets are off regarding the selection of instruments, backing and rhythm etc.

For the kinds of places where beginner or intermediate learners play, generally informal get-togethers or sessions - knowing and >>playing the basic melody well<< is the primary objective.

With regard to tunes (beginner friendly) .. you’re going to start in all likelihood with a few very simple AIRs (songs) that you already know - to get the absolute basics of the playing the instrument out of the way. These may not even be “traditional” - it doesn’t matter - they are just to get your fingers and blowing/breathing basics (assuming tin whistle) going. After that the next steps are likely a couple of simple polkas which you will learn to play slowly. And then some basic jigs. But a teacher would likely pick out some easy steps - based on YOU and what you already know.

DON’T dive in to a book with “101 BEST SESSION TUNES” or the like.. because it’s unlikely to have an appropriate progression in terms of learning difficulty. There are quite a few books which are beginner tin-whistle with accompanying CD which aren’t bad if you’re going down the self-taught route. An example of such: https://www.waltons.ie/product/mckenna-a-complete-guide-to-learning-the-irish-tin-whistle-2-cds/

Finally, let’s dispel the idea that the tin-whistle is a “beginner” only instrument. Well played, the tin-whistle is an awesome instrument and always welcome in a session and as strong a contribution to any session as fiddle, banjo or accordion. Learn to play it well and a lifetime of fun awaits.

Enjoy your learning. And welcome on board.

Re: Beginners guide

I’m pretty sure I wouldn’t suggest the mandolin as a starting instrument in this music unless someone really knew that the mandolin was what they wanted to play. It’s not particularly difficult, but it’s certainly harder to get a tune out of it than the tin whistle, and the mandolin just isn’t a great instrument for most session play, unless you’re in a part of the world where you have a choice of several small, quiet sessions to play in.

On the other hand, I’m pretty sure that if you have to suggest what instrument someone should play, they’re not going to stick with the music long enough to find out whether you were right. You don’t get the motivation to push through the hard and unrewarding early stages of learning any instrument from some abstract desire to “play Irish music”. You get it from a concrete desire to play something that sounds like the sound in your head.

“expensive”, the instrument is free after you account for the $$$ value of the time you put into learning to play it, the $$$ value of the time you spend playing it, the money you spend on lessons and on guinness.

Re: Beginners guide

I think the discussion is getting a bit silly now. No mention of the time required to build up callouses? To become familiar with the fretboard? To tune the instrument?

Having taught both, I can say that there’s no comparison at all - the mandolin is a more complicated and more physically demanding instrument than the whistle, and if you have two similarly prepared and similarly motivated students, one on the mandolin and one on the whistle, the whistle player will be playing recognizable tunes long before the mandolinist. That being said, anyone with a full complement of fingers and the time and energy and the interest can learn either instrument, and the time spent learning will be a pleasure if they’re learning the instrument they really want to play. So they should figure out which one they’re burning to play, and they should play that, and it really doesn’t matter which one is easier to learn.
