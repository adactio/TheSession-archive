Re: Payment for playing at sessions

I’ve heard of a fair number of sessions built round a few core musicians who are paid to show up every week -- that way the pub owner is assured that players capable of leading the thing are around every week. When they can’t make it, the paid player pays a player friend to show up in their place. Usually, those players are the session leaders and it’s their responsibility to keep the session moving well.

In some cases, the paid players chip the cash into a group kitty to pay for whatever -- drinks, food, a lending library of CDs. In other cases they keep the cash for their own expenses of showing up.

Me, it’s none of my business if somebody in the session is paid to show up or not, and I don’t much care -- for me the question is, is it a good session for me to play at, and that’s about it. It’s nice if there’s a bar and/or food tab, but that can get pricey for a bar owner trying to turn a buck (my husband used to own bars, so I tend to think down the same lines as a bar owner) and I can understand their being reluctant to offer it.

And in exchange for not having to police a session as the session leader, I’ll willingly let someone else get paid to do it. Leading a session is often a pain in the patootie. Just fielding player complaints of a night is enough of a psychic drag that it can really bring your evening down, so I’m glad there’s people out there willing to do it, paid or not.

I’ve kniown people get almost apoplectic with rage at the idea of paid, led sessions. Somehow it violates their idea of sessions being some sort of pure amateur tradition. This is really so much claptrap, and I’m always happy to point out to them that early sessions in London at places like The Favourite and Bedford arms were usually led by musicians like Michael Gorman for example, who would receive some consideration for their efforts. If you want a session with a high enough standard of players leading it, you should welcome any event that has musicians that someone thinks worth paying at the helm.
And as Zina quite rightly points out, it’s no picnic being responsible for and/or leading a session, I’ve done it myself and it’s a major pain in the arse (though I’ve never been paid to do so). Trying to reconcile the needs and wishes of a motley band of musicians, the landlord, and the punters can be less than relaxing. I remember a long time ago going to one session where the landlord had paid a young banjo player to lead the session, on the grounds that ‘it was time to give a local lad a crack of the whip.’ (and the fact that he seemed to have an inexhaustable repertoire of tunes). I didn’t know a lot of tunes then, and neither did any of the few other people who had bother coming that night, and I remember him staggering to the end of the umpteenth set of reels played solo, with beads of sweat on his forehead, pleading “Come on lads, help me out!”

Re: Payment for playing at sessions

I think the answer to this question is dependent on where the session is and the level of interest and skill in the local population.

For instance in Ireland and Scotland, where there’s generally no shortage of players, good and bad, it shouldn’t be too necesarry to pay players, and a less rigid and informal structure is BY FAR better. If the landlord wants higher quality, he should hire a propper band on a different night!

But, in other areas where the landlord wants to benefit from the atmosphere of a session, and where there’s unlikely to be enough groundswell to maintain the few lead (or any) players interest, then paying might be the only way to go, or else have only paid bands, if the pub can afford it. I’d just as soon see some kind of session.

Incidentally, most landlords would prefer to have the duke box playing, especially outside Ireland, so if there’s a chance to encourage any of them by taking up their offers of gold or hard currency, then great, and lets not be jealous of the chosen ones!

Re: Payment for playing at sessions

Trevor, this seems so obvious I wonder why I’m posting it, but in my experience a good session is *nothing* like an orchestra, and I’d run from any session that had an orchestral feel. I think that applies to playing session leaders as well--I don’t care if the pub owner wants to pay the leader(s), and it won’t affect my enjoyment of the session, but I agree with Kenn that we’re better off without it. It’s justified when there’s no other way of drawing a couple of core players to carry the tunes, but then you have to question how viable the session will be. If you have to force it, should you even be doing it?

As a non-paid session leader, I go through cycles where the demands on my time and patience wax and wane. Some weeks it’s like a regualr job, but most times it’s not too crazy. And there are compensations besides money: I rarely pay for drinks even when we’re not in the session pub, and people are always giving me cds, tapes, and event tickets for free. It’s also nice being near the top of the “to-call” list when a visiting musician is in town or some other fun is about to break loose.

But I also think of myself more as the session organizer, rather than the “leader.” During the actual session, I sit back and encourage everyone else to start tunes, sing songs, join in, set the pace, etc. I can step up to the plate when needed, and some nights I’m the primary lead player, but most weeks, I’m just another fiddler in the crowd. And I like it that way. It creates a very open, welcoming feeling within a fairly well-defined circle, and I think outsiders pick up on that. If a paid leader let it go to his or her head, that might interfere with the craic and friendliness of a session, but that can happen without the bribe as well.

Schy, if that was a rebuttal to my point about “forcing it,” I’ll thank you for hammering my argument home! :o)

I was trying to look at it from both the pub owner’s and musician’s perspective. I can see why a pub owner might think s/he has to pay someone to get the session up and running, and to keep showing up every week. Bars are used to paying for live music to attract customers, and as we’ve all said, if there aren’t any strong session players locally, you might have to offer an incentive to get someone to make the drive. But that’s not ideal, is it? And it wouldn’t be for the musician either, if s/he has other priorities (family, work, health, etc.) that would normally prevent making the trip to run a session. How much would you expect to get paid to do this? I put my weekly time in for the one session I attend to. If someone wanted to book me for another session, they’d have to equal my hourly wage at my regular job, ’cause I won’t take that time out of my family life, and I won’t work for less at something that requires significant musical and people skills.

I’d say a better solution for communities that lack strong session players is to leave it up to the musicians to improve--hosting their own sessions and learning sessions--until they’re ready to run it on their own. That’s how we did it here in Montana. It took a year or two of noodling around in our own kitchens before we went public, but we’ve got a happening session now, and there’s no need to pay anyone to come play. The session runs on friendliness and enthusiasm, a core group of players who can keep the tunes flying, and a sense of community that includes (nearly) everyone who walks in the door, musicians or not.

Re: Payment for playing at sessions

Some folks have made a slightly similar point already, so forgive me if I’m piling on.
I don’t have a problem with “paid” session leaders, per se. Some pubs have been able to “grow” their sessions organically over time, and have little trouble attracting numbers _and_ varieties of musicians. Others, obviously, are likely to find it challenging, whether because of geography, access, newness, etc., so why not ensure you have at least a core of musicians who -- if no others show up -- can keep things going?
What concerns me is when a session is actually a performance in disguise. Now, it’s fine and good to have a close-knit group of musicians who, by playing together regularly at or outside of the session, have not only a rapport but a repertoire -- one that, understandably, might go beyond what might be considered the “standard” fare of sessions.
But sometimes in these situations I get the feeling that I’m trying to pick up the repertoire rather more than playing tunes. It’s not that I don’t sit and just listen occasionally, instead of trying to flail around uselessly, but for me playing at a session is a rare and precious opportunity -- if I want to listen, I’d rather go to a concert.
Now, obviously, this sort of thing can happen at _any_ kind of session, paid or unpaid. I suppose, as always, it reflects on the personalities of those involved: Are they comfortable with newcomers and strangers? Are they good at soliciting or encouraging others in choosing and starting tunes?
I was talking with a friend recently about session trends. He said that in the past, there were two kinds of pubs: those that encouraged and nurtured sessions as part of the pub’s essential character, and those that tolerated sessions because, well, they fill the place up. Nowadays, he pointed out, you have pubs who seem to be most concerned that the session provide performance-quality music than encourage participation by other players who may not be of the same stripe as the leaders. Now, there’s nothing wrong with that in and of itself, but perhaps the rub is how well this is communicated and understood by all involved.
