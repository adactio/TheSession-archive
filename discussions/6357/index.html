Re: Their likes will never be heard again?

“. . .do we lose something by learning only from the playing of our contemporaries?”

Yes. In point of fact what we often lose is the very “Irishness” of Irish music, and thus the very understanding of just what “Irish” music is. Take Grey Larsen’s observations on the evolution of ornamentation, for instance, something that most Irish musicians hold to be the very essence of “Irishness,” and yet it is very, very different today than it was at the dawn of the recording era. Some of those old masters hardly employed any at all by modern standards, but they were most certainly still Irish.

I’ve already expounded on what I feel to be the evils that equal, and even just, temperment have foisted on the music.

“Should we be spending time dusting off the old 78’s and studying the techniques of the ”Old Masters?“ ”

Obvioulsy, given my already expressed views, my answer to this is “abso-effing-lutely,” at least if one wishes to make any claim to being “traditional.” This is not to say that one cannot learn the tradition from a contemporary. That would be a ludicrous statement, but one must learn the tradtition from one who learned the *tradition* and practices within it. Without personal knowledge of the tradition one has no means of judging whether the person you are learning from is within the tradition or not.

To use another genre as an example, one is not going to learn traditional blues by listening to the contemporary recordings of Eric Clapton, but one is going to learn the tradition by listening to recordings of Dave Van Ronk, who learned it, directly, from people such as John Hurt, Gary Davis and Brownie McGee (would that be Scottish blues, I wonder?).

To take it one step further listen to Rory Block, who also got her musical tradition from John, Gary and Brownie, as well as Dave, but is of a much younger generation. Her playing is at once identifiable as being traditional, but *also* contemporary. She has changed the tradition, but has done so “traditionally.”

I am not speaking against changing the tradition. I live in a very glass house myself in that regard. I am a “fusionist.” I come from New England and our *tradition* is innately fusionist.

No, what I’m saying is simply that oft repeated maxim in every field of endevour that in order to break the rules first one must understand them. Lewis Carroll could not write in the manner that he did without a thourough grounding in how to write “properly.”

In short, what Dow said.

Also, a tradition should be a living thing, in that we cannot seek only to imitate what we view as “the tradition”, but we must allow it to breathe and grow. That is not to say that all directions it grows are good for “the tradition” as a whole, and in those cases (to further the aforementioned “tree” metaphor), the branches should be pruned to allow new, healthy growth in their place.
A tradition that is viewed only from an historical perspective becomes an artifact, and all attempts to imitate it become just that: imitations. The “living tradition” is something I think we should strive for, which has elements of the old as a base for innovation, experimentation, and sometimes fusion of other elements - a graft, if you will - which may grow, being fed by the old tradition, but also may not.

“The fool seeks to follow in the footsteps of the Master, The wise seek what the Master sought.”

↳ Posted by Kerri Coombs on April 14th, 2005.

Re: Their likes will never be heard again?

1. As a beginning fiddler I prefer to listen primarily to the “old (fiddle) masters”, partly because I do want to be connected with the (earliest) roots (we have) for the tradition in which we are playing. The main reason though is that their playing [while not in every case up to par technically with many contemporary players (some of whom have certainly surassed anything I’ve heard in many of the old masters from a technical point of view--as to _some_, but not _all_ techniques--)] appeals to me more than that of most contemporary players I hear. The old masters have the “draoicht” (I’m sure I’ve got that spelled wrong), whereas many of the contempory players do not (though some do!), or at least not to the same extent. It seems to me (based on aan admittedly limtied survey) that there has been of late a tendency towards homogenization accompanied by an overemphasis on certain forms of technical virtuosity--alike influenced by commercialization and the modern concert/“recording artist” phenomenon (and in some cases by the need to “compete” in perceived intensity with rock)--at the expense of the spirit of the music. It should be observed as well that certain techniques of the fiddlemasters have been very much neglected in the modern mainstream; one more reason to go back to old recordings.

2. Silly as it might sound ITM is in the process of “canon” formation which occurs inevitably in any cultural tradition in which preservation in fixed form of “authorative” “texts” and dissemination of those “texts” becomes technically possible. (Sorry for the jargon, I had to spend a lot of time reading “criticism” in graduate school.) Certain of our contemporary players and recordings (like Kevin Burke) will eventually be recognized (or are already recognized)as “authentic” “classics” of our growing (as opposed to only “changing”) tradition. Most will not.

The core of the tradition is the _forms_ of the music. Just as classical music had for a long time the “sonata-allegro form” (among many others), we have “reels”, etc. Our forms are all (a few more ancient forms excepted) based on the country dances of eighteenth and nineteenth century rural Ireland.

One consequence of this necessary simplicity of form (if dancing is to be possible, at least in theory) is that the music can be be played “spontaneously”, i..e., players who have never met before can sit down and play together (as in a session). (This is also made possible by, on the one hand, the widespread dissemination of particular tunes, and on the other, by the relative ease with which truly proficient players can learn new tunes.) Another distinctive characteristic of the tradition is that it is not polyphonic (as a certain ancient Norman said the music of medieval ireland was). Single-line melodies played in unison (with occasional accompaniment) prevail.These two characteristics inevitably make our tradition somewhat conservative. The development of new basic “forms” comparable to the classic forms is unlikely as the dance tradition is dying out. No new dances, no new forms.

The one major change we have been seeing since the Chieftains and Bothy Band (and I believe Sean O’Riada as well, though I am not familiar with his work) is the introducation of some polyphony and variations in form, via the complex arrangements of groups “performing” prearranged music exclsuively for passive listeners. The music has also on occassion been adapted to other prexisting forms (such as the cinematic music branch of the Classical tradition seen in The Secret of Roan Inish and other films).

There will always be an interest by some of the musicians in the roots of the tradition. These musicians will develop their own styles in dialogue with a wider range of masters than those players who focus solely upon the contemporary scene. On the other hand, the second group may be more likely to develop pleasing new innovations and to help the tradition to “grow”.

There is no “should” or “should not” with regard to listening to the music of the 78rpm era, no matter what the CCE might say. Musicians should follow their own hearts. Curiosity will often lead to an interest in the earlier tradition. Other players will be put off be the “difference” of the older recordings, whichwill not be what they expected or are used to; others simply will prefer modern masters to older ones. And it’s all good. :-)

Re: Their likes will never be heard again?

Tradition isn’t a thing. It’s a lineage.

I am fortunate to have been born in a time, place and in a family that gave me at least some direct access to many Old Masters in multiple genres, as well as those newer masters that also learned from the old.

But I most certainly didn’t have as much access as I might have liked, and most certainly not to all the Old Masters. I may have learned some of Woody’s songs from Pete, Arlo and Jackie (an original Weaver, prerecording), but certainly not all of them, and none from Woody himself. I am part of the direct lineage, but still find the recordings both useful and necessary. They inform me of things which I could not otherwise know. They are a valid part of the traditional record.

I can play a John Hurt song that I learned from Dave who learned it from John, and compare that to how Rory plays the same song, which she also learned from John, and note that they are all different.

To hear how *John* played it I have to rely on recordings, and have both his original recordings from 1929 *and* his recordings from the early 60’s. . .and note how they are different.

This informs me musically. It lets me know “where I have come from” and when I diverge, how and in what directions I am diverging. Thus my divergence is informed, not random.

I don’t put the tradition in a bottle for preservation. I partake in it, including in passing it on, but I am able to do so to maximal effect because someone *did* put the music “in a bottle.”

God bless Alan Lomax and Stefan Grossman.

Where my direct lineage is weaker, which happens to include Irish music I’m afraid, the preserved record becomes absolutely imperitive. I can’t remember a time when I didn’t listen to live Irish music, it was all around and I’m “imprinted” with the stuff, but I really haven’t a clue who I was listening to and didn’t start playing it until into adulthood. It was a process of pure “osmosis” which, I suppose, in its own way makes me a true part of the lineage, but it left me without a history because that bit was *not* all around me. I’m not Irish. I grew up in the poor black and Jewish neighborhood next door to the Irish one, where people said “Hey, remember when you and Woody. . .” not “ Hey, remember when you and Sean. . .”. I came to own the music to some degree or other, but bereft of its context, because I went “next door,” listened to the music, then went home.

So I have to slowly and laboriously learn that context from the preserved record. The process of doing so has taught me things about the value of that record, both positive and negative.

The negative I’ve already written about. It tends to fossilize the music when people mistake the record for the tradition.

Tradition is a lineage. It gets passed on. Osmosified, not Ossified. You have to live where you are, not live where they were, but I think you’ll find that you better understand where you are if you understand how you came to be there.

Coleman himself isn’t some God, or even necessarily an exemplary example of the form, but he is *notable* for being part of the earlist aural part of the preserved record; and that record informs.

The fact that Coleman may sound “rough” and make you cringe is the very reason to listen to it. That response is because you lack the information that tells you that what he is doing is the music, and more importantly the music the way it was meant to sound at that time and place. You needn’t strive to emulate it, but you really should strive to understand it on its own terms.

And if you do so I’ll wager your understanding of the music in general broadens, perhaps even to the extent that what once sounded “rough” to you once now sounds like the music.

Flook will still sound like the music too, but different music. Not better, not worse, just different.

But not “traditional.” It may well be part of tomorrow’s tradition, who knows. Whenever you speak of tradition you have to speak of tradition in a temporal context. Today’s “traditional” music is the avant garde music of 300 years ago.

The traditional music of then is almost completely lost, because instead of being preserved with whatever tools they had available at the time it was conciously stamped out.

If only there were some old “wooden cylinders” in an attic somewhere that Liz and Cromwell missed, not to copy by rote, but to learn from. This is a downside to a pure aural/oral tradition that holds its knowledge as a jealously guarded trade secret. The individual might win by this, but the society loses.

I coach cyclists. Yes, I want to watch footage of Jacques Anquetil *and* footage of Lance Armstrong. I don’t have to choose between one or the other, and I can learn from both, if only what not to do in some cases. I wish I could watch footage of Major Taylor. I like watching footage of Roger Bannister and could watch Edwin Moses nearly all day because that man didn’t simply have technique, he was art in motion.

Don’t confuse technique with the music though. Your analogy is flawed. We perform a certain technique to produce a certain sound, but the *sound* is the goal, not the technique, and music, despite the opinion of some, is not measured with a stopwatch.

When I want to race I’ll get out the bike. When I want to play music I’ll get out the guitar or something. I hope I ride the bike with a certain amount of artistry, but my performance will be judged by the watch. I hope I play the guitar with a certain mastery of technique, but only to the extent that it produces the sounds I want it to.

When they start measuring the quality of music with a watch I shall retire to the caves of Vermont and make “rough” fiddle music on all mankind.

Coleman’s technique is not flawed because it sounds “rough” to you in the same sense that a runner’s technique makes him too slow to win a race.

That’s the way *it was supposed to sound, * and even if you never learn to like it you should at least listen to it and understand that it isn’t “flawed,” it’s different, and the way things were, just as a traditional Indian raga is supposed to sound that way even though it doesn’t fit your equal tempered trained brain.

“. . .we *are* all nutters and ignoramuses.”

Everyone is ignorant, just about different things.

When a group of people get together to share their knowledge we call that a resource. When a group of people get together to share their ignorance we call that Congress.

“. . .wondering if I’m the only person in the known universe who needs to learn *how to listen* to unfamiliar types of music.”

Oh, no, no, no. Buried somewhere in the above (I’m afraid I wrote it piecemeal over an extended period while I was actually doing somthing else and only had the vaguest idea at any given moment where I had been or where I intended to go) is the point that *everyone* learns to listen to music, even the sort you are familiar with. You just don’t remember the process of learning the music you are most familiar with because it happened in your extreme youth. It feels natural to the point of being innate, but it isn’t. It’s learned.

The catch being that you can’t actually hear what you don’t already know, so familiarity has to be “bootstraped” up one little bit at a time until you can finally grok it. This is what Ostrichfeathers is on about with her measuring and quantifying Irish music with a micrometer and stopwatch, giving highly trained, from youth, classical musicians a “native” means to understand the music to the point where they can begin to actually hear what they’re actually hearing.

While in youth the brain is a remarkable device for taking in anything and everything and internalizing it to the point where it seems innate, in adulthood it turns into a remarkable device for taking in anything and everything and retranslating it into something it learned in youth, no matter how inappropriate that translation is. In just the wrong sort of situation this translation can prove fatal. Or, alternatively, it may simply refuse to accept the input as having any meaning at all. You can get some sense of how this works by thinking of looking at things approaching you from a distance. For awhile the thing looks like “just noise.” Things as basic as shape aren’t even clearly perceivable, because you don’t already know what the shape is. Then there comes a point, with remarkably little transition period, where you grok it, and the amorphous “thing” suddenly becomes a “red car.”

But if someone *tells* you it’s a red car you can see it clearly much earlier, even if it, in the end, actually turns out to be a tan camel.

The brain is a funny little critter, one moment making fine distinctions beyond any man made sensing device, and the next minute insisting there’s an elephant standing on your toes when there isn’t an elephant within 100 miles of you.

I’m confident that in a blind test I can tell the difference between someone who learned Irish music by ear in their youth and someone with little exposure who learned it from the dots as an adult. Very confident. Conversely, I will *refuse* to believe my own ears as to the tonal qualities of two different flutes *until* I have blind tested them. My experience tells me that my brain will often insist on including “price tag” as part of “tonal quality.”

When fiddle shopping I try to get the shop keeper to just give me fiddles and let me play them, without telling me what they cost, or giving him any clue as to what I’m “worth.” I know I’m more likely to get the best *sounding* fiddle that way. The shop keeper is always loath to do this. They don’t want to sell you the best sounding fiddle, they want to get as much money out of you as they can in the least amount of wasted time and the first question they always ask isn’t “what kind of sound are you looking for?, ” no, it’s “How much are you looking to spend?”

My standard answer to that is, “Ideally, nothing,” which is the God’s honest truth.

Of course there are risks to that approach. You might well end up with a really, really great fiddle for $500, or God help you, you “get chosen” by one that’s going for $60K, and you work in the food court at the mall, or, even worse, as a musician, without a girlfriend, and a fiddle case doesn’t really offer much in the way of living accommodations.
