Thinking while learning and playing by ear

I’m just starting out with fiddle and whistle. I play guitar and can read sheet music, but I know that I rely on the sheet music when playing. I want to be able to play from memory and to pick up new tunes by ear.

For those who learn by ear and play from memory: what are you thinking about while you learn and while you play tunes you already know?
Note names, such as B and C#?
Intervals between notes?
Finger positions, like numbers?

Any insight you can provide into your experience will be very helpful. Thanks!

This is a description of what I personally do and not necessarily a recommendation nor a suggestion that it’s the most efficient way.

What I do when learning is basically sing the melody to myself. I don’t think in terms of the notes, I just work on getting to where I can sing the melody in my head from memory.

The thing is, I’ve played my mandolin for a great many years so once I can silently “sing” the melody, I have like a mental map of the fretboard - I know where I should go for each note. It just kind of happens. Since you’re just learning your instruments, I think it’ll take time to build that connection between hearing a note and playing said note without a step in between to bridge that gap

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

I agree that it is difficult to juggle everything when you’re first starting, especially if you’re trying to learn the instrument, the style of music, and how to learn by ear all at the same time. As someone who plays somewhere around 1000 Irish tunes, and doesn’t read music, I can attest that it is possible, though!

It is really difficult to really put into words what I’m thinking about when learning by ear. I can tell you that I am generally NOT thinking about specific notes, except occasionally when a tune does something unexpected, like playing an accidental that is a big part of a tune. (Like maybe the F natural in the first part of Steampacket. I will sometimes pay attention to what note it is, but generally thinking “it’s an F natural instead of an F sharp”)

I do think about intervals, but not in the way of thinking “oh, that’s a third, and that’s a fifth”. I feel the intervals, and my familiarity with my instrument helps me reach the next note by knowing how to reach a particular interval from the previous note.

But in reality, I am mostly thinking about the contour of the melody (or a string of intervals, maybe). If you use visual contours as an analogy, think of mountains, the outline of a cityscape, or even just what the horizon looks like when you look out your front window. You learn to recognize contours like that, and for me, recognizing a tune feels like the same thing. So I am thinking of a tune more in terms like “it goes up here, drops off sharply there, and bounces around in that one part”. I am not thinking about it in those words, but what I remember the most, and how I recognize a tune that I’ve heard before (even if I don’t play it) is the contour of the tune.

I can combine my knowledge of the contour with my familiarity with my instrument to be able to play tunes, sometimes ones that I’ve never actually played before. But thinking in contours can be a bit of a pitfall, too. If I don’t remember what key a tune is in, or what note it starts on, I can sometimes end up trying to play tunes in a different key than usual. (I can usually tell when I’ve done that, because it will feel somewhat unfamiliar).

So this can make tune learning easier. Instead of trying to remember a string of 128 notes and what order they come in, I really only have to remember one thing, and that is the contour of the tune… And the contour is not something that I remember as a whole, it’s more fluid. When I have heard a tune enough, I just know where it goes up and down, and then all I have to do is pay attention to where it does something unexpected.

But getting to this point was also an evolving process that has been going on for 25 years. It certainly took me 5-6 years of learning to play my instrument before I was able to just reach for an interval without having to think what note I was reaching for. And the process is ongoing. At about 15 years into playing is where the whole process started feeling really natural, but I’ve certainly gotten even better at it in the last 10 years, and I’m guessing that kind of progression will continue…

But everybody is different, and what works for me may not be the way that works for you…

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

Brandon, as others have said, learning Irish trad tunes by ear is a blend of three broad skills:
(1) knowing your instrument (where to find the notes and produce them with lift, pulse, and feeling),
(2) knowing the idiom (understanding or intuiting what makes Irish music sound like Irish music), and
(3) being able to follow or “sing” the tune in your head.

I’ve been playing fiddle, and specifically this music, for a long time, so the first two come easy now, they basically run on autopilot.

Which means my brain is free to focus on the tune itself when I’m picking one up by ear. Like Reverend, I listen to the overall contour of the tune. And as I play along, I’m following the *phrases* within that overall contour. Often they do some sort of call-and-response or question-and-answer pattern. Many two-part tunes do something like this:

First Half
phrase 1 | phrase 2 | phrase 1 | ending that doesn’t quite resolve |
phrase 1 | phrase 3 | phrase 1 | ending that does resolve ||
Second Half
phrase 4 | phrase 5 | phrase 4 | ending that doesn’t quite resolve |
phrase 4 | phrase 3 (or 5) | phrase 4 | ending that does resolve or turns back to the First Half ||

Of course there are many variations to such a pattern. Sometimes a phrase from one half reappears in the other half. Sometimes the phrase before the “ending” is part of the ending, or it goes in a completely different direction.

I don’t label the phrases like this while I’m learning the tune, but they do register, at least subliminally. As do the “surprises,” the places where the tune goes someplace unexpected.

Often, at a session, I’ll just listen the first time through a tune, following the contour, letting familiar phrases sink in. Then the second time through I’m actively (quietly) playing along. Third time through is a chance to fill in any gaps. A fourth lap is nice if the tune leader is up for it.

A fun and really helpful practice for all this is to imagine a phrase in your head. Sing it in your mind, and then play it on your instrument. You can imagine a snippet of a tune you already know, or try a snippet of a tune you’ve heard before but haven’t learned yet. If you don’t get it all on the first try, try again. But first slow down and mentally sing it in as much detail as you can. Usually, wherever you get off track on your instrument is where you don’t have an accurate mental map of the tune.

Be patient but persistent. You’ll gradually develop a knack for all three skillsets and be off and running.

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

Other comments pretty much cover it. The brain controls everything, so you have to make a regular practice of getting a sense of the music in flow, what chords and chord changes and notes are going on, in a general feel of it (not necessary to know which actual key/chord it actually is), and to feel the sense of the notes within a chord; is each note a jump of a whole tone, a semitone, a third, fourth, fifth, octave, two octaves, etc., …………

and the next thing is to translate that into “Instrument Speak”. You’ll have to get used to the instrument and how the notes are laid out. Playing the guitar without looking at the fretboard, and moving all over the fretboard and constantly using different strings to pick out notes, is a good way to quickly learn how to correctly guess and select notes within a chord. One thing I’m constantly doing when running music through my head is watching the melody notes and wondering where in the chord’s octave is “that note”; what does it feel like, is it the tonic, the second, third, fourth, fifth, a sharp or flat, whatever, as that helps get a sense of the “picture” of what’s happening. That picture needs to be directly transferred to an instrument’s structure. “The brain is a real-time, fluid and self-organizing musical stencil.” Tell your teacher that and you’ll get 3 extra points for luminosity.

On piano you also have it kind of easy to train this process, since the piano is laid out physically and no matter how great or crappy you are as a keyboardist, the keyboard always sounds like the keyboard whether you hit correct, wrong or experimental/exploratory/creative notes. On a whistle, almost as easy, as it’s 6 holes up and down for the most part. Kids stuff, right? Fiddle: you might want to spend a lot of time training the fretboard hand to do its work finding notes, by picking the strings with the right hand, to basically keep the difficulty of good bowing out of the experience while you’re training the left hand. Learn how to quickly feel the music, the tone/semi-tone note jump distances, and find them on the fingerboard, shifting around using all the strings. Then when you bring back the bow, the note-picking is done correctly and waits for the bow to work properly. The confidence in the left hand actions has settled in as a given.

Any instrument; you’ve got to get used to it like riding a bike, you know all the moves, all the actions, from easy to severe. Then play things spontaneously. Make things up and keep on going, on and on. That trains the mind to pay attention and work the Brain/Instrument connection smoothly. If you only practice known pieces of music, that’s OK, but doesn’t have the edge of attention that spontaneous playing does. Deeper learning happens when it’s more passionately done. When the moment matters. No safety net. Ecstasy and terror united.

It’s more finite than you think: G & D major, E & A minor.

I really liked Gimpy’s comment pointing out how that most tunes are built around a call & response structure.

I am not that good at learning by ear (I’m getting better), but… I can usually start to pick up pieces of a new tune after it’s been played a couple of times. Another point is to listen for the key notes in the melody, the “Gs” in a G tune, often emphasized by melodic interest, dynamics (volume), timing (e.g. dotted quarter note) or rolls.

Getting to REALLY know your instrument can be hastened by practicing scales, arpeggios, and also “pedaling” structures which are pretty common in our music.

To answer a couple of specific questions.

I don’t think of note names - I can name them if I want.
I do sometimes think of fingers used. On flute: three fingers down… oh, that’s a G.
I don’t think of intervals, but my fingers seem to know them: All those arpeggios.

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

I agree with the consensus - getting the tune in your head is key, and singing it is both good practice and good evidence of success.

If you want another suggestion to bridge the gap between where you are and where you’d like to be, one thing that worked for me, when I was learning, was to write out the tunes in a sort of modified ABC in a little pocket notebook (like the small Moleskin ones, for example, or a spiral-bound memo book like you can find in any pharmacy), and I’d “practice” the tune on a mental whistle or mandolin or banjo or accordion while listening to the tune in my head, cuing it off the page.
I don’t know if that makes a lot of sense, but I found that it worked pretty well for me. Basically, it’s the muscle-memory equivalent of visualizing, but I don’t know if there’s a good word for that.
At the time, I was walking a half an hour to and from work on a nice day, which gave me plenty of time for “practice” of this sort. I’d go over a tune in my head until I got tired of it, and move on to the next one, and after a bit I’d find that the pieces were coming together in my head, and eventually I’d have the tune, and then I’d play it through in my head without the book a few times, and then there it was. Usually would take a few days to a week for any one tune to get lodged, and most of them are still there.

The key, it seemed to me, was the part about practicing without the instrument. I don’t know why, but it seemed to me that I learned the tunes a lot faster when I did this. Possibly because when I didn’t have the instrument in hand I had to create the sounds in my head? I don’t know, but it seemed to work.

I don’t know if this will work for anyone who isn’t me, and in fact I don’t think I’ve ever heard of anyone else using this approach, but a pocket notebook is pretty cheap, so it’s maybe worth a try.

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

For both playing by memory and learning by ear the general key is to break the task down into easier parts.

For memory you can just listen to a tune over and over until you can sing it like others have mentioned. Then you can go through each bar on your instrument repeating it up to 10 or so times, and put it together.

For playing by ear it’s a skill that takes practice. Generally reels are played too fast for a beginner to start trying, so you can start with nursery rhymes. Just try to work out a nursery rhyme like Polly puts the kettle in D or G, as best as you can. I don’t think there are any short cuts but starting on simple tunes and moving up to polkas then to jigs was the key for me, and doing it very regularly at least for a while.

It’s a slow process at least for me but bears fruit eventually. I still can’t get most tunes in one play but many tunes I can get a passable rendition after 2 or 3, but it’s taken years and is not effortless. It’s very satisfying when it happens well though, good luck!

Reverend, my call-and-response post was mostly just an add-on to your insights about learning tunes as contours. Call-and-response is one way of hearing the contour.

When I give someone a tune, I emphasize the bits that repeat, e.g.: “And then we play this phrase again,” or “Since this is a hornpipe, the last lines of each half are the same.” It no longer surprises me when some people struggle to hear the repeated part and recognize that they’ve already learned it. Sometimes it helps if I present it this way:

This tune follows a call-and-response pattern.
Here’s the call: diddly diddly
Here’s the response: eidely eidely
And then we play the call again: diddly diddly
And then there’s an ending of sorts: deedly dum

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

The central question in the OP is actually two questions: “what are you thinking about (1) while you learn and (2) while you play tunes you already know?”

gilmo789’s answer—“there is almost no thinking, music goes into the ear and comes out at the fingers”—really resonates with me, so I’ve been pondering this a bit. I hope you forgive my rambling to follow—likely I haven’t yet had enough Ardbeg Uigeadail today for this to make sense. ;-)

On one hand, in the throes of bringing a tune to life (either learning a new one or playing an old favourite), the only thing my active mind seems to be doing is “hearing” the tune in my mind’s ear and following it. The more aware I am of the mechanics of playing or consciously identifying the notes/key/mode/meter and so on, the clumsier I play. I’d go so far as to say that the more verbal my thoughts are, the poorer my playing.

So if there’s “thinking” happening, it’s not at all the manner of thinking that happens when I’m not playing music. (Except when I’m mentally singing tunes in my head as I described in my post above to Jon Kiparsky.)

On the other hand, I believe that we mostly play music with our brains. Fingers, lungs, lips, fiddles, whistles, flutes, pipes, etc., are simply the external mechanics that make our thoughts audible. Take away the tune skirling in our brains, and those external mechanics go quiet, or produce gibberish.

Which makes me wonder: what sort of thinking is it, this “hearing” a tune in my head and following it?

For me, it seems to be mostly what I’ll call “aural imaging.” It’s like I have a map in my head of the tune, but it manifests not visually or verbally but as sound (the tune’s contour, phrasings, an intuitive sense of the tonic center and follow-on intervals, etc.). To be clear, this “map” isn’t a rectangular page that shows the whole tune all at once. It’s more like flying underwater (bear with me), chasing the leading point of a laser beam in a dark sea. Behind me, the beam evaporates, while ahead it lights up just a small patch of ocean depths, swooping and swerving around and through the darkness. The laser beam draws the map just a second or two in front of me, and I swoop and swerve to follow.

But it’s a laser beam of *sound.*
And the sound is driven by a swirling current of emotion, of what the tune makes me feel.

About 20 years into playing this music, I noticed that playing tunes I once knew well but that had gone rusty felt a lot like learning a new tune by ear, or composing an entirely new tune—I had a general sense of where the tune was going, but the details were something of a mystery. The more I learned to trust myself to follow the tune wherever it led (and turn off the mental chatter about mechanics and labels), the better I played. Now at half a century playing this music, that feeling is even more intense, and enjoyable. I take more chances with tunes, and I hear the tunes with more nuance now, which makes the flying more varied, freer, more spontaneous in many small ways. In short, learning tunes and playing tunes are one and the same activity. And it’s mostly about learning to listen with ever greater nuance—and trust.

The other analogy that resonates with me is that learning and playing tunes is storytelling. There’s a similar sense of knowing the overall outline of the story and plot but filling in the details as you go. Maybe changing some words or your cadence or inflection as the story unfolds, but following the familiar arc.

Still, I’ll stick with the deep sea flying. I wonder if this is anything like what goes on in a dolphin’s mind as it follows its sonar?

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

That post really resonates with my experience as well, gimpy! In my original post, I said “the contour is not something that I remember as a whole, it’s more fluid”, and what I meant by that is that it’s more linear. My mind is anticipating where the tune goes next, but I’m not thinking about what it does after “next”, until that time arrives, and my brain is thinking about what comes next after that. And it’s not a super conscious thought that I put effort into, it’s more like surfing the tune, and anticipating my next move.

Will Harmon used to explain it like driving on a winding road at night. Your headlights are only looking so far ahead. You’re not paying attention to the part of the road that you’re currently on, because you’ve already prepared for it. So in other words, my mind isn’t focused on the notes I’m playing right now, it’s focused on where it’s going next. To continue the analogy, I see the next curve approaching, and guide the car to be ready for it, but by the time I’m in the curve, I’m looking at what’s next.

To continue the analogy even further, in terms of your storytelling analogy, I am also deciding in that moment how I am going to approach the next turn. Much of my conscious brain is making decisions about how I am going to approach the next phrase as far as what kind of emotion I am trying to convey, what kind of melodic or rhythmic punctuation I am going use, and whether what I am doing is going to crash into anyone who is “driving” alongside me… Am I going to be cautious or aggressive, am I going to swerve a little to make it feel like a harder turn, etc. So I am conscious of the road (or notes) that have passed, and how I have driven (played) it, but it’s focused on the near future, which is as far as my headlights can see…

I realize that this is beyond the scope of the OP’s question in a lot of ways. This is more what I’m thinking about when I’m playing, not what I’m thinking about when I’m learning a tune, other than the fact that my brain is trying to anticipate the road ahead even when I’m learning the tune, and can make educated guesses about what happens next in the story even if I’ve never heard the story before.

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

Sure, to focus on tune learning a bit more:

When learning an unfamiliar tune, my listening is focused on getting the tune to feel familiar as quickly as possible. Which means listening for the overall pattern/contour (in part based on similarities to the large body of tunes I already know) and also listening for the phrases and turns that are similar to other tunes I know.

But it’s still mostly just listening, following the tune. Not much different from playing a familiar tune.

This highlights a point that hasn’t been made yet in this thread (unless I missed it)—the more tunes you already know (the larger your dataset), the easier it is to learn new tunes. For someone new to the music, with only a limited number of tunes in their head, any new tune will be mostly made up of unfamiliar bits. Like listening to a conversation in an unfamiliar language. The more tunes you learn, the more you become fluent in the many similar phrases—idioms—and the more likely you’ll recognize them, as well as the surprises that set a new tune apart.

Which brings us back to the adage that there are no shortcuts. Someone just starting out on this path, and learning by ear, has to learn:
The layout and mechanics of their instrument.
The overall parameters (rhythms, melodic guardrails, etc.) of this music.
It’s essential relationship to dance.
How to glom onto phrases, intervals, patterns and both retain and regurgitate them.
A shedload of tunes.
Local session etiquette.
How to hold steady on a tune in the center of a caterwauling session.

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

This has been a fascinating discussion to read and think about. I just want to add a dimension which I hadn’t previously noticed about ‘playing a tune’ in one’s mind, as we often do, whether learning the tune or just going over it when an instrument isn’t at hand. I play the flute and each note on the flute is a unique combination of lip configuration, breath pressure, angle of blowing, and heaven knows what else. The point is that each note is different from every other note (this is particularly true of the simple-system wooden flutes that are the predominant type of flute for this music). What I hadn’t noticed before is that when I am playing/listening to a tune in my mind, I ‘feel’ the notes the way they feel on the flute. I’m not conscious of fingerings or note names or even necessarily aware of the way the note feels, but it is there nonetheless. I should add that with the simple-system flute, many notes also have unique timbres and these contribute something to what one mentally hears (without the instrument).

I also play the guitar although not for Irish music (flamenco and classical) and what is true of the flute is also true for the guitar to a certain extent: each string has its own feel and each fret (varying as one goes up the fingerboard) its own pressure and feel. This means that each note has a muscle feel, and I think that when one mentally plays a piece that one knows on the guitar, one senses these unique string-fret ‘feels’.

The relevance of this to learning to play by ear is, I think, that there is no need to think of anything consciously as one learns a tune. Simply by playing a tune, one is acquiring a set of note-feels, that will be utilized unconsciously when mentally playing the tune. No need to think of note names or intervals or anything conscious at all. The melody resides, at least partially, as a set of note-feelings.

I’m not sure how well this transfers to other instruments. It should transfer perfectly to the fiddle, partially to the concertina, not well to the piano, at least partially to the banjo and mandolin, and I have no idea how well to the button box -- it would depend on how uniquely each note feels under the fingers. Chet

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

Chet, yes, that same “note-feeling” happens on fiddle. Given the tuning of fiddle, every key—and every note—has its own resonance, so you can tell, for instance, when a recording features a fiddle tuned up or down, and certain notes ring differently than they would in standard tuning. An old thread dove into this at some length as people tried (and eventually succeeded) to decipher the tuning and key on a clip of a fiddler playing a particular tune.

I’d go so far as to say “note-feel” is how intonation and fingering works on a fiddle. The more accurate your aural sense of a note is (not just pitch but resonance, timbre, etc.), the better your intonation.

For me, note-feel is a critical part of playing tunes in my head, away from fiddle. Imagining a tune seems to activate all the neural firings that would happen if I were actually playing fiddle. My muscles and fingers twitch, and I can feel my bow arm very subtly following along despite not moving in space as with actual bowing. It may not be 100% the same as physically playing the tune, but it’s fiercely close.

Re: Thinking while learning and playing by ear

thelightisahead, sounds like you’re off to a good start in adding ear learning to your approach to this music. I think it helps to take a break from sight reading at all while you focus on developing your ear skills—it will compel you to listen, and listen again, and to start trusting your ears as you improve.

That said, the way I read notation is mostly about “sight singing,” hearing the tune in my head from the dots or abcs. For instance, if a new tune posted in the tune section here catches my attention, I’ll read through it a few times, without an instrument, to get the sound of it in my head. And then I’ll play it on my fiddle without reading. If I miss anything, I’ll glance at the notation again, but only long enough to hear that part in my mind’s ear. This actually feels very similar to learning from an aural source.

As for playing the tunes in my head, I think I’ve always done this, even as a young child. For the life of me, I can’t sing audibly on pitch, certainly not over the range of many trad tunes, so it’s more effective and rewarding (not to mention less disturbing to others) to lilt the tunes in my head. Having done this now for decades, it seems to be a very developed skill—the tunes are richly detailed, I can “play” them at any tempo or degree of swing, change key, and explore variations. I’ll imagine different ways to bow a phrase, and “feel” changes in bow pressure as I vary the dynamics. I often come up with “sweetened” notes and melodic variations this way, and am then eager to test them out on fiddle. Sometimes that doesn’t happen until I’m sitting in a session, and it feels a bit like jumping out of a plane without a parachute. But that’s just my conscious mind screaming doubts. I’ve learned to trust the mental playing as basically equivalent to physical practice.

I also do this to simply run through spates of tunes to knock the dust off. It helps as a way to keep less frequently played tunes from withering. It’s also a good tool for warming up on the way to a session or gig. Get those neurons firing! And it’s a handy diversion for places like medic’s waiting rooms or the dentist chair.

In short, it’s an enjoyable way to play the music when you’re not with your instrument.

This ought to be a skill, like any other, that anyone can develop to some degree. The more you do it, the easier and more layered it gets.

Oh, and in response to David50’s post, I can choose to hear the tunes on flute, whistle, and fiddle. It’s like choosing a playback option.

WhistlingFionn brings up a good point about progressions in one’s playing.
