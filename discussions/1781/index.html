Re: When do you know it has clicked?

One thing that’s been bothering me lately is that most people seem to believe that something can’t be in two states at once. (ONLY AN EXAMPLE; NOT STARTING A POLITICAL DISCUSSION:) You can oppose U.S. policy AND Saddam Hussein at the same time; those points of view are not mutually exclusive. Similarly, being a learner doesn’t mean that you can’t be a player at the same time; being a player doesn’t mean you can’t also be a learner. You can be both.

So: it’s clicked when it FEELS like it’s clicked TO YOU. No one but you can reasonably set any standard for you. If you’re a rank novice and one day you find that you’re able to accomplish a D-major scale with a nice even balance all the way up, that feels good; that’s a click. Recognize it for what it is, and enjoy it. If you’ve been playing for a couple of years and you find that you can pull off that roll in the middle of The Humours of Ballyloughlin, that’s a click too, equally worthy of celebration. If you’re a beginner and can start a tune, playing the first two bars cleanly enough for the old hands to recognize it and cover your mistakes for the rest of the piece, that’s a click--yahoo!. If you can lead your way through a reel set, that’s a click--good for you!

Recognition from other people is nice, but if we’re to avoid swelled heads or excessive self-deprecation, I think the best way to go is to try continually to set and and then accomplish new goals for ourselves. When you accomplish those goals, no one will hand you a player’s certification; no one will deny it to you, either. It’s up to you--but a big part of it is recognizing that a) the clicks are personal; b) they’re points on the journey, not ends of it; and c) they never stop, as long as you don’t.

---Michael B.

Re: When do you know it has clicked?

Last year, the day after the end of the Willie Clancy Week, I was in a session in Clancy’s, Milltown Malbay. As anyone who has been to Willie Week and stayed on past the end of the festival will know, this is when one comes into contact with the dregs of society (i.e. you are what you drink). This is not to say that there are not representatives of this part of humankind whose musical pursuits are as lofty as those of even the soapiest-breathed teetotaller. One such musician at this session was a whistle player (whom those who frequent the Lahinch/Ennistymon area will know) huge in both stature and talent, not so big on manners. I had met him once the previous year and played with him briefly. On recognising me, he launched into a barrage of criticism: “I remember you. You’ve got no spirit in your music. You know every f*!&%{?g tune off the radio, off every f*!&%{?g record, but you can’t f*!&%{?g play them!”

My initial was reaction was, “What should I do? Stop playing altogether?”
In his eyes, my even putting plectrum to string was a mortal transgression. Arguing with him would have been out of the question, him being 4 times my size and half his body volume occupied by ‘spirit’. Not wanting to admit defeat, I did not leave immediately and carried on playing, tentatively. But the atmosphere soon became stifling, so I made my excuses to some of the more equable musicians present, and left

Whilst it was tempting to pass off the said whistle player’s comments as a mere drunken outburst and brush them aside (some might even have responded with their own drunken outburst, had they been sufficiently intoxicated and suitably dimensioned), I felt I couldn’t ignore them. I would, of course, maintain that there is no excuse for that sort of rudeness. But, in trying to find some kind of justification for my being there, I came to the conclusion that I was there to learn to play, and if it was the case that I was playing with only technique and repertoire, and no ‘spirit’, then that was an inevitable part of my learning process.

It has made me aware, however, of the danger reaching a state of equilibrium where, for example, acquiring new tunes becomes very easy, to the point where it requires almost no effort. In this state, one is not really learning anything, but just exploiting a sensory-cognitive process that has already been learned and takes place ‘automatically’.

I believe this can happen any level. When a particular process ‘clicks’, whether it be playing in steady time, playing triplets or rolls, learning tunes by ear, improvising complex variations or playing tunes backwards with your feet whilst whistling and singing two other tunes simultaeously, it doesn’t mean you’ve reached the top. It just means it’s time to move on to the next level.

Re: When do you know it has clicked?

I think what Michael is getting at with his “500 reels” is making a comparison with someone who might learn reams of poetry off by heart, superficially word perfect, but with little or no understanding of the meaning (as many children were forced to do in school in days gone by), or those “memory experts” who have developed techniques for memorising thousands of unrelated facts. Of course, there are those players who do indeed have the “500” but also have a deep understanding of the music. Such players are often immediately recognisable in their playing by the time they’ve got halfway through the A part of a tune.

Michael’s suggested technique for getting at the heart of the music is applicable across all types of music, and other art forms. An in-depth study of a small but representative number of works will give you the deep knowledge and confidence to tackle the rest of the repertoire later on. I remember an orchestral rehearsal in which the conductor spent 2 hours working us on just the first page of a Dvorak symphony, and then he explained that now we could do that page to the level he got us to, then we wouldn’t have any problems with the rest of the movement. And so indeed it proved to be.

Of course, as Michael pointed out in his following post, this technique takes time, but it will pay off. And when it “clicks”, you’ll realise that you now have that understanding. Depending on the individual, I think it could be weeks or months, and certainly not days. Brendan McGlinchey once remarked during a workshop that it took him 9 months to get a slow air to the stage where he was ready to play it in public.

Re: When do you know it has clicked?

I wonder if this whole thread isn’t spinning around the original question. We’ve heard about many different levels at which your playing can “click,” but maybe the definitive “click” is when you can make a reel sound like a reel, or a jig like a jig, and also release (and perhaps even expand) what you hear as the essence of each tune’s individual personality. For example, Beare Island Reel is a reel, and so is Mason’s Apron, and so is Dinky’s. They all have enough in common--their “reelness”--that you could connect them in a set and not throw off the dancers. And you’d be a passable player if you could do that. But you’ll have clicked on a higher level if you can hold them together while also playing them as individuals, drawing out their distinguishing features. Even better if you can do this while winking at the pretty thing who just sashayed in the door and not spilling your pint….

In broad terms, you might have three levels of clicking on a tune, each building on the previous:

1. Going through the motions of playing the “correct” notes with proper intonation, tone, and clockwork timing.
2. Imbuing the tune with the qualities of the tune type (reel, jig, slip jig, etc.).
3. Exploring and conveying the tune’s personality and different moods.

So--there are thousands and thousands of tunes. In fact, there are thousands and thousands of reels alone. I’d wager that it’s possible to know 500 or 1,000 or 3,000 at Click Level 1, or Click Level 2, or with years of playing and conscious effort, Click Level 3. In my experience, once you’ve clicked on even just one tune at Level 3, then you’re hooked--you can’t help but take all the tunes you know at Level 2 and spend time with them till they’re as familiar as the inside of your own mouth. It’s just a matter of finding the time to do it.

And I’d agree with Michael (yay!) that you’ll be better off in the long run (well, in the short run too) if you slow down and take the time to work a few tunes up to Level 3 before simply amassing tunes. But I also think that you can’t fully appreciate a tune’s individuality until you hear it in the context of the hundreds or thousands of other similar tunes. In short, you’ll understand Rakish Paddy much better if you also have more than a passing acquaintance with Bank of Ireland, Nine Points of Roguery, Bantry Lasses, Dinny O‘Brien’s, and maybe even the Wicklow Hornpipe, Chief O’Neill’s Favorite, Frieze Britches, and Rambling Pitchfork.

In my book, it’s still acceptable to play at Level 2, at least in sessions, because that’s how you learn tunes and get beyond the acquaintance stage. You can hear that in a person’s playing--the difference between playing a tune as a representative reel or jig or whatever, and playing the tune as an individual. But that doesn’t mean you shouldn’t haul a tune out in public until you can play it inside and out. I’d go so far as to say there’s nothing wrong with knowing hundreds of reels just so you can join in on them at sessions. But you’ll be a more welcome asset to the sound if you can play at least some of them with real heart. And if you play this music long enough, you’ll no doubt learn a few tunes just because they’re the favorites of another player in your circle. By the 2,000th time you hear the Concertina Reel, you’ll have learned it, whether or not you wanted to. I say there’s nothing wrong with playing it then, especially in a session, even if it never becomes one of your Level 3 tunes. It’s part of being sociable--making small talk with the tunes. Save the profound conversations for the tunes that call your name…..

(That said, never write a tune off completely. Years from now you may be dumbstruck at the intricate, miraculous beauty of the Concertina Reel and wonder how you misread it back in your neophyte days :o).

Re: When do you know it has clicked?

I’d say I get lots of clicks, amazingly enough for someone with a bit of a tendency towards negative thought patterns. I get a click when I realise I’ve learnt a tune in my head. It’s a bit like putting something in the microwave and it lets you know when it’s done - ‘ping!’ Except that it doesn’t take 2:30min, more like several weeks, or even months.
(Tunes go round in my head almost all of the time, and I think of it as the tunes bedding themselves in - it feels as though it happens by itself without much input from me. Possibly like cooking a risotto - don’t stir! You *want* a nice crust to form!)

And then when my hands learn the tune and I can play it through, another satisfying click. And there might be one bit I can’t quite get - and then I get that - another click! Like a blimmin’ Geiger counter… ;-)

With singing, sometimes it feels like hard work and my voice doesn’t feel good and the connection isn’t quite there. Other times, my voice seems to come sailing out with no effort. And although the emotional connection certainly *is* there, my voice seems to be an independent entity - I’m not having to push it and think about it, it just has this life of its own.

And likewise when I finally ‘get’ a tune - I’m thinking ‘blimey, did my fingers just do that?’ - cos my brain wasn’t really dictating the action.

It seems that all these clicks have one thing in common - a feeling that an automatic process has taken over and that my concious mind has stepped back and left that process to run unaided. Evidently it takes a lot of concious effort to make each step forward, but once you achieve that step, the concious mind has to let go - like taking the stabilisers off a kid’s bike - you can’t do the thing properly till it becomes automatic and you let go of the handrail…

Re: When do you know it has clicked?

I’ve heard a top notch fiddler comment that he’s usually not impressed when people play to try to impress him. Not to take anything away from Seane Keane’s technical abilities, but that’s the difference my ears hear between Seane Keane and Martin Fay. The former seems to be saying, “Look at what I can do with this fiddle and bow,” while the latter is saying, “What a beautiful tune….”

So another way it might “click” for us as musicians is when we play unself-consciously, as a vehicle for the music and nothing more.

In my experience, beginners and old pros alike play more relaxed and “better” when they realize that the focus belongs on the music, not the musician. But I think there’s a key difference between how beginners and old pros approach that enlightened mindset. Beginners might rightly worry that their technical deficiencies will draw attention away from the tune. Sure, the third time through Inisheer won’t be enjoyable for anyone in the room if the tone is grating, intonation variable over a whole step or more, and rhythm spasmodic. Beginners *must* be conscious of those problems or they won’t correct them and improve.

On the other hand, old pros who worry about technique (while they’re actually playing in public) are probably concerned more about how listeners perceive *them* (especially as compared to other old pros) than how listeners perceive the tune.

My point here is that beginners have some legitimate reasons for “moaning” as Michael puts it, as long as it doesn’t become a habit. And beginners appreciate it when old pros moan too, in commiseration, because everybody’s allowed to have an off night once in a while. But there’s a difference between moaning in good humor about accidentally wrecking a nice tune and moaning about not impressing people with your instrumental fireworks because they were damp tonight.

In short, I think it’s healthy for beginners to hear that everybody struggles with this stuff in the early stages and it’s okay to moan a bit about it *as long as that doesn’t stop you from playing and enjoying the tunes.* And it’s healthy for old pros to moan supportively with the beginners and then explain how to move on. And it’s also healthy for old pros to be scolded for moaning about their deficiencies and their wounded egos.
