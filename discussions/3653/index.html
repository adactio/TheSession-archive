Time Signature

Things have changed a lot since I started playing trad. In 1983, “The Thistle & Shamrock” had only just come on the air, no one played trad flute in my area, and recordings were hard to come by. As a beginner to trad, but not a beginner to flute, I went through a lot of confusion about how written tunes should be played, because the reels were written in 4/4, rather than as alle breve, or cut time as it is alternately known (the “C” with a vertical line through it in the key signature). A reel played in 4 sounds very choppy, and not at all Irish, whereas when it is played with two beats per measure, you then have the proper rhythm.

My guess is that so many learn their repertoire at sessions now that this is much less of a problem than it was when I began, and didn’t have local trad enthusiasts to guide me. Still, I wonder why the music is so commonly written incorrectly, and if beginners on this site who read try to play tunes as written and wonder why they don’t sound right. Any thoughts?

Re: Time Signature

4/4 vs 2/4 (cut time) does *not* cause the page music to suddenly have any swing, ornamentation, variances, stylized bowing (or breathing?), or any other aspect of the music that makes a tune an Irish tune. In fact, 4/4 or 2/4 or 4/2 simply mean that the beats are subdivided or combined, so instead of quarter-quarter you might have eighth-eighth.

The reason tunes are not written as played is because everyone plays them differnetly. There is in fact music that attempts to capture all the subtle things a musician might attempt - but these are specific to the player, the instrument, his mood, and the position of the sun. Beyond that, a classically-trained player would still sound stilted and mechanical playing literally what was on the page.

So we trade tunes on paper (only in emergencies) putting the most simple representation of the tune down, so that the player can take it and change it at will.

Re: Time Signature

Ailin, I think to play reels with an ‘authentic’ feel (whatever that means), it’s important to account for the four quarter-note beats. Some phrases want the 1st and 3rd beats emphasized, and some want the 2nd and 4th emphasized. To me, it’s more accurate (though still hopelessly inadequate) to notate reels in 4/4.

In short, each tune is an individual, and playing that accents only the same two beats per bar would quickly sound stale to my ear. Four beats to the bar at least hints at other possibilities with a phrase--the sorts of things trad musicians like to tinker with.

Re: Time Signature

Uh-oh - looks like I opened a can of worms, here. Let’s see if I can explain time signatures:

The upper number (numerator) indicates the number of beats per measure; the lower number (denominator) indicates the length of time (note value) each beat is held. A tune written in 2/2 or 4/4 will look exactly the same in terms of notation. However, in 4/4, the rhythm will be bah-bah-bah-bah for each measure. In 2/2, the tune written exactly the same way, will be baaah-baaah. When tapping your foot to a reel, you probably don’t consciously notice you are tapping twice per measure, rather than four times, but try it. You’ll find that reels played at tempo are almost impossible to count at four beats per measure.

Don’t confuse rhythm with speed. Reels sound fast because of the rhythm, and even at breakneck speed, they are easy to count. Counting four beats per measure may work while learning a tune slowly, but as soon as you get up to tempo, four beats per measure is almost impossible to do, and the tune sounds all wrong.

None of this matters much if you look at a tune written in four, and instinctively played it in two. It only becomes a problem if you come to trad from a background of playing written music, and try to learn new tunes from a book, and play the tunes as written.

I see where we’re all getting off.

The “beat” from the numerator and the moment your tapping foot hits the floor. These are not the same thing.

I tend to tap my foot every time I play 4 notes (of the 8th-note in 4/4 variety). For the sake of discussion, I’ll call those notes ‘running notes’ and so I tap my foot for every 4 running notes I play.

I could re-write any reel (4/4 time with 8th notes running) with one whole note per measure. Instead of an 8-bar part, I’d suddenly have a 32-bar part. You’d be tapping your foot every time you played 4 measures.

I could re-write that same reel (still in 4/4) making my running notes dotted-halves. You would then tap your foot every time you played three measures.

As to 13/3… Just picture the reel as written. Now replace 4/4 with 13/3. Now remove all the bar lines, and start putting them down every 6 8th-notes (Assuming I’m correct in that /3 means the beat is dotted halves).

So there. ;)

I just read a few of the posts while I was doing my last one, and I guess I’m surprised at the variations of what people do. Let’s not talk about tapping the foot, but rather think in terms of metronome beats. That is to say, we are talking about beats per measure. It is the pulse of the music and has nothing to do with accents, ornaments, or anything else. It is the essential beat of a tune whether you use rests, accents, syncopation, or what have you. All these things give music character, but none alter the beats per measure, no matter how the resulting music sounds. However, if the rhythm is written incorrectly to begin with, you absolutely cannot play it so that it will sound right unless you already know how it is supposed to sound, and simply ignore the time signature.

Will’s question:

My answer: Perhaps the best way I can answer this is to give an example. Most hornpipes can be played as reels and vice-versa. It is only the time signature that makes the difference between the two. Without the time signature change, one could never make a hornpipe sound like a reel, or a reel like a hornpipe. That sole difference, the time signature, tells you whether it is a hornpipe or reel. The ornaments and notes could be exactly the same in all other respects. So you can write a tune however you wish, but if it sounds like a reel, it has only two beats per measure.

Oh, you people. Here was a perfectly good argument; I leave you alone for a few hours and you let it slide into social harmony and warm fuzzies.

Over dinner with my girlfriend - a classical pianist - we talked a little about this thread. She hummed a bit of a classical tune to demonstrate how the time signature actually presents a clue to the reader how to phrase the music, or at least what to emphasize. It became suddenly very clear.

Zina said it. In classical music, the dots come first; the music comes from the dots. You use everything on the paper (including the time signature) to tell you how to play (including pulse, phrasing, style). The time signature matters a lot; it’s a clue to the musician.

But in ITM, the music is first; what is on paper comes next - a representation of the music. Therefore you *don’t* use anything (including the time signature) as a clue. The only way to know how to play it is to listen.

So the disconnect was between classical and trad approaches to the music.
