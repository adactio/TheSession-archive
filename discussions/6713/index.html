Re: tunes chords

The three-chord-trick will only get you so far, but you shouldn’t be thinking in terms of what are the correct chords for a given tune. The harmonies and eager strumming provided by well meaning backers can often be too over
bearing for the tunes.

It’s all about the melody.

As you play fiddle I’m assuming you know the tunes already.

Well you don’t have to be Tony McManus but you should know the tune in your head at least, be able to hum it – know where the tune is going, so you can help it out (not that I’m implying it needs help) with appropriate chords,
chord fragments, bass runs or depending on your skill counter-melody.

Going through your CD’s maybe concentrate on getting the bass notes, changing when you feel the tune changing (others will describe that better)
Concentrating first on rhythm - once you’ve done that a few times consider adding to the bass line, where the bass is a chord tonic add the 5th say.

A lot of guitarists leave out the 3rd of the chord, DADGAD tuning is suited to this, as some feel the definite major minor distinction is too definite, although DADGAD has it’s opponents too.

Re: tunes chords

As a word of advice, even if you do find some books out there, please don’t go down that path. You are on the right track listening and playing along to recordings in the privacy of your own home. Your ear needs to become accustomed to what other more experienced accompanists are doing. Then you need to familiarise yourself with the tunes if not actually learn to play them on the guitar, learn them so that you can “hum” them.
When you put these two experiences together you are then ready to sit in an understanding session and try to accompany the tunes you know. Be guided by your ear and the music, but you must first train your ear (auditory cortex).
Good luck

Re: tunes chords

fiddlemax,
Don’t let this outpouring of advice discourage you--there is a place for guitars in sessions, if the right people are playing, and the guitarist has the right frame of mind. And you don’t have to throw away music, don’t have to avoid ever playing thirds in your chords (although leaving them out sometimes is nice), and don’t even have to retune your guitar, as standard tuning can do the trick in the right hands. Remember, opinions are simply that, and you need to take all advice with a grain of salt.
This is another of those “tastes great” versus “less filling” arguments, like in the old beer commercials. The battles between the advocates of the different camps were fierce but pointless, since the two positions were not mutually exclusive (at least according to the brewers, who wanted us all to think that their beer was both great tasting and less filling). You can get a lot from just listening, but you can also get a lot of good ideas from sheet music with chords. Using written music as an addition source for ideas can be an essential part of building your skills. I would point to the David Mallinson series of tune books: “100 Essential Irish Session Tunes - Dave Mallinson ed.” is the first, and others are “100 Evergreen Irish Session Tunes,” “100 Eternal Irish Session Tunes,” and there is even a companion book of 100 polkas. They have chords that generally work pretty well, and I have gotten many good ideas from these books. That being said, you don’t have to use these chords, and there are many alternatives, so be willing to experiment, and never stop listening. I also agree that the Chris Smith book is good, but when I was starting I found it a bit confusing, its value came once I had been playing for a while. I agree with the cautions about using books only if you try to use the books exclusively. If they are part of a broader approach that includes listening, learning and experimentation, you are on the right track.

Re: tunes chords

Al I apologise for the Celtic thing - that was below the belt. I was a bit annoyed that you thought I was on my high horse, when I see it as sound, helpful advice, and a well-meant warning to avoid pitfalls.

I have both the Walton and Mally’s books, and I actually think they contain a good selection of session standards (some of the settings are a bit weird though). However, I have looked at the chords and I simply don’t rate them. They seem to have been written by someone who looks at a particular phrase in the music - e.g. |B2GB AGEG|DGGF G… and assigned an “obvious” chord to each one, in this case G, then Am, then G again. That’s simply *crap*, and not how it should be done. A lot of the time it’s possible to substitute much more interesting chords. That’s only possible if you *listen* to the tune as a whole and get a feel for it before you try and add any guitar.

Tunes aren’t just a string of notes that have a set of “chords” to them which are separate from the tune. You have to think of backing more as a way of showcasing the rhythm of the tune that should already be there if you’re with people who can play. It’s more about subtly responding to rhythms and creating textures than simply playing chords. To be able to back effectively, you have to develop a framework for it in your head. Following someone else’s chords written in a book just does not help with this, especially if the chords aren’t very good. In fact I think it is a hindrance because it’s likely to make you lazy and not listen enough. You need to listen to and watch what good backers do and get some useful tricks from them like chord substitutions. Pay a lot of attention to bass lines and how they move, and end cadences, suspended chords, countermelodies… basically what sounds pleasing to the ear. Learn how keys and modes work. Then you need to experiment on your own for a lot of years until you find out what works for you and how you like to hear yourself play. Don’t just learn a few chords from a book and mess around. That’s fine if you’re playing on your own, but if you want to play in a session, you have a responsibility to the other musicians.

And Bliss, the best guitarist in the world wouldn’t be asking for chords in the first place. If you read through my posts again, you’ll find that I’ve actually been encouraging - “good luck”. I think some people have been incredibly irresponsible with their advice on this thread, and I’m keen for Fiddlemax to disregard what they’ve said and instead use his ears.

With that G-Am-G progression for the start of Fr. Kelly’s above, I picked it out because the bass doesn’t move anywhere. It’s 3 chords in complete isolation from each other and no cohesion w.r.t. the tune as a whole. Am is a chord II min. If you hear chord II your ear expects the bass to move to *anything* except back to the I. You could go to a IIIm, IV, V or VIm, but going back to a straight G triad is *so* lame. A Gsus4 or something might have been a much better choice than IIm so that you get a bit of a drone on the G bass, but these tunebooks don’t include those sorts of chords or ideas for drones etc. Chords in tunebooks are just completely lame and unimaginative. Re: tunes chords

Another common idea which most good guitarists use, but which (significantly) never appears in tunebooks, would be to go G - D (2nd inversion) - G (1st inversion), so G, then D with an A in the bass, then G with a B in the bass, so that your bassline goes up in “steps” each bar: “G-A-B”. This is the sort of thing you have to take into consideration if you’re going to back tunes, not stupid chords out of some book.

Re: tunes chords

Um, in any case, I wouldn’t recommend practising along to backing if you can’t back. If you can’t do chords on your own then it’s likely (I’m not saying necessarily - *likely*) that you will not have developed a fluid rhythm yet on guitar. If this is the case, it won’t be a good idea to use it to play along to, even if it is yourself playing. It’s best to practise solo and make sure you sound good when playing fiddle on your own. Then you should set aside a few years of your life and become a good backer, and make sure you sound good backing, and *then* put them both together. In other words, *the tunes come first*. Guitar comes after (but before bodhran).

I still stand by my advice from before. Backing isn’t there as something to help you keep in rhythm or even provide rhythm for you. It’s there as an afterthought to emphasize or enhance what already should be there in terms of the tune and rhythm.
