Sessions - are they good for the tradition?

Okay, the title was partly a lighthearted attempt to get you to read this, BUT….
It’s my understanding that sessions are a relatively recent phenomenon in ITM - recent as in mid 20th century - and having been exposed to so much discussion about them since I started visiting this site, it set me thinking on a few questions. (If anyone would like to add learned references to this assertion, that would be great.)
One thing I hear a lot is: why learn an unusual or highly ornamented setting of a tune if it’s no use in a session? Good question? Probably no point if your musical goal is to play in sessions. Is this a good thing for the state of the music?
Occasionally I meet some (fairly elderly) players who come from tradition-bearing families, etc. who don’t seem to feel really comfortable with the whole session thing if they find themselves in the midst of it. Perhaps they feel their settings are outdated, or even superior, or just disturbed by a bunch of people they don’t know joining in when they start to play. They just don’t quite “get it”. I’m not here to put a value judgement on their point of view or on the more modern ones.
It does seem to me that quite a lot of the time there’s not a lot of listening going on in sessions, that it’s sacrificed to the need for the group “sport” of playing tunes together. Again, this isn’t a value judgement, more an observation.
I’ve mentioned here before that I haven’t gone to public sessions for a long time. I’m kind of realising why, maybe. I think I’d rather sit down with somebody who is interested in my tunes/settings, and who has interesting ones themselves and listen to each other more intently, as well as play together. (So if you want to come round and sit in my kitchen let me know. And that’s a serious offer.)
I’m wondering what others think. After all it’s Sunday morning, plenty of time for a can of worms on toast.

Re: Sessions - are they good for the tradition?

I’ve mentioned this before but I believe there’s always been sessions of a sort, although the pub sessions as we know them originated fairly recently. Whenever musician friends met up, I’m sure they’d have a tune and song, eg in houses, bothies, even some pubs. Travelling people kept tunes and songs alive and I’d imagine sailors and the like got together to play hornpipes etc. I’m sure there were all sorts of things going on.
I think the modern day pub session is just and extension of this, even although it is more energetic, sophisticated, and it many ways cosmopolitan. It’s a mistake to believe that there’s only one type of session, even although “an ideal or typical session” is often discussed here. They come in all shapes, sizes, formats,and levels. Some are better for “passing on”, learning, and swapping tunes than others. Even the fast and furious ones will do this too as fellow musicians and punters(if they’re interested)will at least hear and be aware of new tunes.
So, yes. They are good for the tradition and always will be. However, they are not the only way of keeping it alive but that’s another discussion.

In the context of a dscussion of the tradition of Irish music, quite a lot, I’d say. That isn’t to say I don’t love the music, or shouldn’t be allowed to to play the music, or that I can’t do my bit to promote it - it’s just a statement of fact. The whole point about a tradition is the fact that it is an unbroken line. If that line completely breaks, as it has done with a large part of the English musical heritage, those who try to pick it up after the break tend to be called revivalists. Because the Irish tradition has not broken (It might have been sorely stretched at times) those who are not part of it who want to participate in playing it are somewhat hard to define. Most of the people I meet in that position are too dedicated to be described as dilettantes, but what are they/we? I’ve read a lot of posts on this site which appear to be attempts to move the goalposts in order to include the writers in the tradition itself(!) - but I think that merely muddies the waters. However, it’s important to remember that the “Celtic Revival”s of the 1800’s and the early 1900’s played an important role in how we view “the tradition” of this stuff.

All I have to do to remember the recent nature of some of this stuff is to remember that for instance the famous Daughter of Erin dress that stepdancers now “traditionally” wear dates back to about…1940, and boy has it changed since then, becoming steadily more glitzy and stiff ever since (but that’s another subject entirely). Around 1900 or so, the “traditional” dress for stepdancers (girls) was a red hooded cape or shawl over a white dress (for instance, the one you might wear to church), and bare feet were looked upon with approval. And before that, it was street clothes. Previous to 1900, in fact, it was considered unseemly and unhealthy (it might affect a girl’s later ability to have -- shhhhh -- babies) for a girl to dance in hardshoe. :) My, how times have changed.

When, in the 20’s, the Commission came together (in, gasp, London, not Ireland) to put together a handbook of the cultural dancing of Ireland, there were literally fistfights in the street over the issues brought up by this process. I suppose you can only be thankful that people cared so much about it, really.

Re: Sessions - are they good for the tradition?

Well, I can’t complain too much since some of my income comes from making the silly things, you know. (In fact, right now I’m working on a team costume for one of our most adorable of dancers, a girl named Alena who appears to have springs in her legs.)

Oddly enough, part of the reason that the dresses look so different now is that many of the designs do NOT come from the Book of Kells any longer. The trend has been towards more La Tene sort of designs, or geometrics -- things that make a fairly big splash from stage when stitched up in glittery sparkly fabrics.

But that’s still better than the old “story” dresses. Probably the most infamous was the Wizard of Oz dress, with the Yellow Brick Road running all round the dress and the characters from the book dancing all round it. But the one of the Salmon of Knowledge was particularly hilarious, with a portrait of Cuchulan with his finger in his mouth.

I like our school teams, which i suppose I should, since I designed it, but it wasn’t actually ever meant to be the school costume -- it was meant to be the senior ladies’ team, and so was only supposed to be made eight times. I’m not sure how many I’ve made this far, but well over 50, for sure. If I’d known it was going to turn into the school team, I’d have designed it differently…faster to make, for one thing! :)

Re: Sessions - are they good for the tradition?

Hi guys -

In any LIVING tradition there are three general camps - (1) those who look back and think “that’s the way it should be done. Don’t hear anything that good anymore.” (2) Those who go along with the status quo, i.e. participate but don’t think about it very much, don’t give a shite (Dow?) and (3) innovators who deliberately test and cross the boundaries between what is/n’t traditional. Any one person might be in all three camps at various points in their life. So wherever you think you are, you’re an important part of whole thing!

Without the thitd group, the backward-lookers (not meant in ANY derogatory way) start to dominate, the status quo-ers can’t be bothered anymore (too many rules, too serious) any the music turns (dies) into a museum culture where we all drag out our national costumes on national whatever day and pretend.

Chris
