Head-wobbling on the concert stage

In the old days, the great musicians simply sat on the concert stage and played their instruments. Now it appears that anyone under thirty, playing traditional music, in Scotland at least, on the stage, has to express themselves through exaggerated and continual head-wobbling and other jerks and wiggles which the great players of the past seemed able to completely do without. My suspicion is that these youngsters are taught head-wobbling as a component of ‘stage-craft’ in the various traditional music courses that have sprung up, i.e. it is supposed to make them look as though they are really into the music. Observing one professional head-wobbler in a recent session, I noticed that as soon as he was trying to play a tune that was not part of his usual repertoire, the head-wobbling immediately stopped as he concentrated on the instrument. Surely, if there is any musical merit in head-wobbling, and I am not denying that this may be the case, then the attempt to master an unfamiliar tune should demand an even greater amount of head-wobbling than usual from the player in order to ensure his early success. The absence of head-wobbling in the cited case suggests that wobbling is musically useless, and merely a visual gimmick. Has anyone else noticed the head-wobbling phenomenon ?

Re: Head-wobbling on the concert stage

To Copo24, I don’t doubt they are into the music, nobody would get to grips with an instrument otherwise; my point there is that head-wobbling seems to be about convincing the audience that the musicians are into the music. I am not against physical movement when playing, it is after all a very physical activity, but I suppose as a player you know that head-wobbling is unnecessary and then it begins to look ludicrous when it happens. I suppose it is the old tension between music and entertainment: if the music is insufficient on its own to amuse the audience, then something else must be done, and as the musician has his/her hands and arms occupied, that leaves the feet or the head. The logic is that to increase public appreciation of traditional music, we should all wear massive ostrich-feather head-dresses a la Mardi Gras, so that even slight head movements can have a huge visual impact and cause increased pleasure in the audience.
