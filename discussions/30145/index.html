↳ Posted by NewToItAll on June 28th, 2012.

Re: Borthmyn

The two outdoors videos are lovely, and you boys are adorable. (I’m old enough I can say that.). For your next videos, you should work up some medleys, as Irish* traditional music is usually performed and recorded in medleys -- 3 2-part tunes 3 times each is common -- rather than one tune 4+ times through. (Leave that for American old-time music.) This isn’t a sacred rule, but it is a very common practice.

*Scottish and IIRC Welsh, too.

Also, please, please step away from the sheet music. Start learning the tunes by ear, even if you have to rely on recordings and videos. Learning by ear will improve your phrasing, ornamentation, and overall fluency. Do you have some favorite bands or soloists? Listen and play along with them. We aren’t all fortunate enough to have teachers and mentors living nearby, so make use of what you do have.

Denizens of this mustard board will be eager to recommend musicians to inspire you. Don’t let the cranks and curmudgeons scare you away. Some of them know what they’re talking about, but have an idiosyncratic way of expressing themselves. Others are just cranks and curmudgeons, bless their hearts (as we say in the American south).

Good luck!

What kind of band do you want to have? Trad? If so, you’re not ready yet, as ethical blend said. You need to spend about 5 years at least playing in sessions first. Re: Borthmyn

This wouldn’t be my choice of listening, but it is clear that these boys are fairly new to traditional music, so there is great potential. Living in Wales myself, it strikes me as a sad reflection of the state of traditional dance music in Wales that they feel the need to play Irish dance tunes off the page, without any reference to the tradition, rather than playing Welsh tunes in a way that claims ownership of them (not that there is anything at all wrong with Welsh - or Polish, Thai or English [I had to include that one, or I’d have to give up] - people playing Irish music).

On the positive side, as a band, they play well together and have good energy. That’s a good foundation to build on. Also, it is always a good thing when young people take an interest in the music, if if they need a bit (or a lot) of judicious steering.

In the 1st person: If you have a real interest in Irish music then, as has already been suggested, some serious listening is a good place to start. But, in Carmarthenshire, you’re only a stone’s throw from Fishguard and Pembroke, so why not hop over to a traditional music festival or summer school in Ireland and find out what it’s meant to sound like? You could all do with some work on your individual playing - something like the Willie Clancy (Co. Clare) or Joe Mooney (Co. Leitrim) summer school will provide you with instrumental classes, for fiddle and whistle, at least; there might even be something a bit closer to Rosslare, if you’re pressed for time.

If you are interested in Welsh traditional music - I may be telling you what you already know here - there are small but active scenes around the Tawe Valley (‘Sia ’Nol, Sia ‘Mlan’ in Ystradgynlais/Ystalyfera in late October), Cardiff and Aberystwyth - and also around NW Wales (Caernarfon, Bangor, Nant Peris).

As for paying gigs, it’s great that you are getting them - and you wouldn’t be getting them if you didn’t have the ability to entertain people. The danger, as I see it, is that, if you take the number of bookings you get as a measure of musical quality, you could end up putting all your energies into gigging and none into improving your playing. Inevitably, years of gigging will make you tighter as a band, and make you more proficient at what you do, in the way that you do it, but it won’t teach you a thing about traditional music.

You need quite a bit of experience to get the timing of Scottish and English dance tunes right (and for SCD, using sheet music is a non-issue - the very best performers do it, and always have done, since the tune repertoire is so enormous and the arrangements for specific dances are so variable that nobody could carry it all their head).

Creadur: “I’m not sure I agree with that. It depends, of course, where you are, how strong the tradition is and/or how high the general standard is. But where a traditional dance scene exists, people are no fools and could tell a dabbler a mile off. Exactly the same goes for Irish music - evidently.”

I’m only speaking from experience here. Take Northumberland and the Borders where I’m from, for example. The tradition is pretty strong, but sessions are few and far between. A lot of the time, you have to travel for an hour or an hour and a half across deserted moorland to get to one! Even then, there’s no way the standard is anywhere near the kind of sessions you get in many places in Ireland - less of a music thing and more for the social aspect. There are very few young people getting involved in the music, and those who do, gravitate towards Irish music so they can travel round enjoying sessions. In this kind of community, if someone starts a band up for fun like Borthmyn, it’s regarded as a bit of fun and you’d never get anyone thinking to criticise the band’s musicality. I’ve noticed that people who try the same thing in the Irish scene over here in Australia get b1tched about behind the scenes and sometimes face-to-face. Might be upsetting to be on the receiving end but it keeps people on their toes and people become aware there are expectations that you should be of a certain standard before you start getting a band together and doing gigs.

I think YouTube and the affordability of new HD cameras and editing software is partly to blame here.

It used to be that a promotional video was one of the last things a group was able to accomplish after years of practice, touring, and CD sales. Now it’s the first thing out of the gate for a new band. And it can be done in a fairly polished way, as in the above examples (the outdoor vids).

Promotional videos can become an affirmation of the band’s worth, especially when there are positive comments on YouTube or Facebook from people who aren’t familiar with the genre. It can shortcut the traditional feedback loop we would normally get from instructors or one’s musical peers in sessions.

Well, that’s my curmudgeonly thought for the day. Video killed the radio star, etc.
