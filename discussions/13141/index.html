Added by ceolachan on March 26th, 2007

Dance anyone? :-/

Alright, if you insist you can lead. I can take either part ~ or remain completely neutral and just observe dejectedly from the sidelines… Sorry, an ‘aside’…

Here’s the question, is there dance anywhere near you? ~ maybe even woven in as an integral part of your music making or a session or other musical activity you frequent or are party to organizing? Maybe you even partake? If so, whatever degree of involvement, does it affect the music you play, the way you play, your choices, the repertoire you play, or those about you choose to play? (‘you’ can be interpreted as meaning solo or en masse)

If there isn’t any dance at hand, do you think that has an affect on what is happening within and about you musically? Is it important? Also needed to be clarified in this discussion would be consideration for the many forms that ‘dance’ can take ~ formal, informal, new age and sandled, latex and domineering, head bangingly subversive, anarchic… As with people, not all relationships between dance and dance music are necessarily healthy or well balanced…

I’m not sure how to present this, but I’d like to see if a close proximity to or invovlement with dance means a wider variety of tune forms in a session’s or an individual’s repertoire? ~ as well as any other side-effects?

I was listening to some old tapes of music making where I’d just turned on the recorder and forgot about it, and I was amazed by some of them, pleasantly, that were quite varied. In fact, reels at most made up only about a quarter of the play list on some tapes. As examples I could include the geographic locations of Cork, Kerry, Tipperary, Clare, Donegal, Fermanagh, Tyrone, Armagh ~ to name just the few ;-) ~ where the musicians present in these cases were also fond of dancing and also played regularly for dance. Despite the different locales, the variety of tune forms was impressive, markedly different from your average big city reel dominated pub session…

So, what about it, is there dance in your life, or not? Is it plentiful, in short supply, missing altoghether? If it’s there, is it welcoming or tyrannical or ~??? Whether there or not ~ do you imagine that this affects in any way what and how you or those about you play the music?

Here in little old Helena, Montanny, we have not one but two Irish step dance schools. One is homegrown, geared toward anyone who wants to have fun. They offer classes for kids and adults, hold ceilis (mostly in the fall), and dance for special community events. The other school is affiliated with the Trinity school out of Chicago and is more competition oriented. They also dance at community events.

There’s a small group of adult ceili/set dancers--they used to get together every other week or so. Not sure how active they are these days.

So…it’s always fun when a couple of the step or set dancers show up at our sessions. And we’ve done regular ceilis in the past, gathering in a big community hall with decent acoustics (no sound system needed). These were usually a potluck, too, and open to all. Great times.

And an older (late 70s) couple who love to dance and always ask for a a waltz or two. Seems I play for them at least 3-4 times a month. A true joy.

Re: Dance anyone? :-/

It’s interesting that this should come up on the heels of discussions about who the heck are those people who have learned tunes from the dots without having done any serious listening?

My band is composed of dancers who play for dancers. In this case, contra dance. It most definitely has had an affect on what and how we play.

We started with a need for tunes and a knowledge of what type of tune was necessary -- 32 bar tunes, generally AABB. And we knew what we’d danced to. So we found dots, learned the tunes and added a boom-chuck piano and bass behind it to give the dancers the beat. Dancers seem to like it.

We stick to jigs and reels that meet the format, and play them with little variation of speed in deference to the dancers‘ needs. Two waltzes a night, and that’s where we get to have some creativity (although that speed needs to be fast enough to be danceable). Waltzes are my favorite -- you know you’ve done a good job when there’s a pause the length of an intake of breath between the last note and the dancers’ actually stopping. I like to hope at least one couple has bonded during one of our waltzes….

I have been lucky enough to meet other musicians who taught me the error of playing just from the dots, and I’ve found that the freedom of playing at non-dance speeds gives me more opportunity to express myself and what I think the music wants expressed.

So here’s the challenge -- continue to play within the needs of our dancers but convince my fellow band-mates that there’s more variety and better approaches after we’ve been doing it from the dots and without much further knowledge for well over 10 years? Especially when it’s “easier” to just read the music rather then really learn it.

Re: Dance anyone? :-/

We -- four of us from our local weekly session plus a guitar player -- play about four contra dances a year. We live in a small town (8,000 people) on the Olympic Peninsula of Washington State, but there’s a fairly vibrant contra dance scene on out here.

On one hand, the dance music is more diverse than that heard at our usual weekly session. At our session, 99% of the tunes played tend to be Irish, whereas at the dances, we throw in some Scottish and Cape Breton tunes (e.g., Frank’s, Spootiskerry, Stan Chapman’s). We also toss in a few American old-timey tunes. We play 2-3 waltzes per night, whereas no one ever, ever plays a waltz at session.

On the other hand, we’re limited to tunes with two parts, all AABB. That eliminates all our favorite 4- and 5-part tunes, single reels, etc. We’ve squared up a couple tunes to that format; most notably, we play the Tarbolton set’s Sailor’s Bonnet with two A-parts (horrors! I can hear it now). We’ve also eliminated tunes that we consider “too notey” (The Twelve Pins comes to mind), and all Paddy Fahy tunes. Callers rarely ask for a hornpipe. So overall, I’d have to say that the musical diversity is less, rather than more, at our dances.

That being said, we love to play dances. We’re having loads of fun on stage, and it’s great to watch a sea of dancers having fun on the floor. The mix is a good one: everyone from little blue-haired ladies to purple-haired kids with nose rings. In terms of musical discipline, there’s no better way to learn to start together and keep a steady, unwavering pace than having 75-100 dancers relying on you. Beats a metronome every time. And I have to believe it transfers to the session. Also, at the dances we get a chance to play with a guitarist (no one in our little town plays Irish tunes on guitar, so our session is all melody instruments, with a bodhran at times), and he happens to be superb. And finally, playing each tune more than the standard three times each allows a musician the unusual chance to stretch out and experiment a bit with nuances during each tune.

I feel, however, that we all play with less ornamentation for the dances. I know I do, and it’s a nice change of pace to play in that fashion.

Although I wouldn’t want to play dances all the time, I’d welcome the chance to play a few more dances during the year.

Re: Dance anyone? :-/

I regularly go to set dances. The music consists of reels, jigs, polkas, hornpipes and slides. I would know most of the tunes the ceili bands play, and tend to keep an ear to how the tunes are played (sometimes to the point of forgetting that its my turn to dance the figure if the band are particularly good !). One point I have noted is that the rhythm and beat is not just provided by the drums and piano - it is mostly in the playing of the melody instruments, and the drums and piano emphasise what is already present. I have tried to bring that into my own session playing.
The tunes at the dances are mostly standard repertoire tunes that probably originated for dancing anyway. We would have a wider range of tunes in our sessions, and would also play marches, some slip jigs, and set dances (such as St Patricks Day and Jockey to the Fair.)
The main influence of exposure to dancing is not in the tunes played, but that the many musicians in the session who also dance have a better sense of the rhythm and swing appropriate to each tune.
