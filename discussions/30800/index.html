Re: beginners’ hour

“The format in use at present is to go around the circle getting each player to start a single tune.”

The “round the circle” format can be quite intimidating to newcomers, but if everyone is comfortable with it, then perhaps it’s a good way to get people used to starting tunes. In a ‘grown-up’ session, it tends to make the running awkward and stilted.

“Do you think a fixed set of tunes a good or bad thing in this setting?”

It depends on the size of the collective repertoire. I don’t think there’s anything wrong with a few fixed sets, which include tunes that everyone plays, so long as people are also encouraged to bring other tunes to the group and make up their own sets.

“Perhaps the round of tunes should be ”themed“ eg. ”only jigs this time round?“”

Is the idea of the “round of tunes” to play a continuous set, without breaks, or just to let everyone play one tune on its own? If you are trying to play a continuous set, then definitely try to keep it to one tune type (e.g. jigs). It’s good for developing timing and tune recall if you are forced to think of a tune and start it without dropping a beat after the previous one. Sets that incorporate more than one tune type (e.g. jig into reel) generally need to be rehearsed beforehand to be done well, so there isn’t the same room for spontaneity.

Re: beginners’ hour

In our most recent involvements, ‘slow sessions’ and a ‘community ceili band’, we avoided that round-robin approach, though several local English groups have used that format successfully for years. I was never comfortable with it myself, and, for me, it seemed a bit contrived. The situations here were literally round table, with most using sheet music, and even stands being present.

Instead of going that round-robin route we just had an open and relaxed start, using a set and varied list of tunes but also making sure everyone who wanted made a contribution, and all did, including to the repertoire. For those tunes someone contributed, or took a special interest in, we’d more usually have them starting their contribution.

We also, as you’ve mentioned, had a ‘teacher’ to teach, guide and help. In our little gatherings, not to everyone’s liking, sheet music was not allowed, though dots were provided, and recordings, for home use only.

To start with we did not play sets but only a tune at a time, and at a relaxed tempo - ‘slow session’ - with considerate repeats, several times through each tune. Our numbers were growing, but, sadly, we lost our venue…

As I’m sure you’ll agree, it is very rewarding, satisfying…

The biggest single step for beginners and those trying to adapt to a group context is correctly identifying the form being played, and following the form at a useful speed. Learning a limited number of particular tunes breaks down at this point.

Re: beginners’ hour

I’ve never liked the Circle of Death in beginner or intermediate/“advancing” sessions.

Aside from the pressure it puts on newbies, it’s also an open invitation for players to do their favorite party tunes which nobody else can follow, every time their turn comes around. In the extreme case, you get a situation where someone treats their “turn” as just a performance opportunity, where everyone else in the group is expected to be the backup band.

I like a format for advancing player sessions where an established (and more advanced) leader can call tunes that he or she thinks the majority of the group knows. Ideally, that should be mixed with people spontaneously starting tunes that the group as a whole knows, so the session leader isn’t calling every tune. And if it looks like someone is being left out, then the session leader notices it, and asks if they’d like to call a tune or set… with a free pass and no questions asked, if refused.

The session leader acts as a traditional party host. Is someone being a wallflower and not joining the conversation? Then you do what you can, to draw them in.

In many respects, it’s a much harder balancing act than just walking into a high-level session as a player among equals, and playing some tunes for the craic. That’s easy. I admire and respect people who are good at leading the more mixed sessions with lower-level players, because that’s hard work and it helps keep the tradition alive.

I’d be curious to know quite how David50 and DrSilverSpear, above, learnt to play. Was it by an immersive process of some sort, a musical family? That seems to be a common background for musicians who announce their lack of formal training as though it were desirable or beneficial in some way.

My daughter doesn’t play sessions much and doesn’t really take much interest in it, preferring to reiterate her orchestral pieces and play with structured groups of that sort , but she can announce “6:8 time in C” or whatever within the first few notes, and strike up some sort of part on the odd occasions she does attend one.

I do quite a lot of leading and mentoring of inexperienced local teams in my own professional field, which happens to be surveying. I usually follow the approach of simplifying decision making by limiting the number of available options, and leading towards a limited range of likely solutions.

Beginners or improvers who learn specific tunes, don’t follow this course. They know a limited range of specific answers and the logic of the situation tends to be that whatever is played next, they probably don’t know that particular tune. This means that they are presented ( especially in an “Irish” format where a series of tunes which may change key or time in an unspecified manner are played in rapid succession ) with an unmanageable number of choices requiring to be made at an unmanageable pace, so that tunes they do in fact know, pass them by before they can react.

Watch the next new face that walks into a session you are at, sits on the fringe for an hour or so and puts their instrument away; you will see the process play out quite clearly, and probably won’t see them again.

Conical bore, above, is clearly following the same course, of limiting the number of choices and the speed with which they need to be taken, and providing the highest possible level of confidence in the decision. The object is to lead the improving player to the stage where they can take accurate decisions quickly with a high level of confidence

Conical bore is also spot on about “drawing people in”. There are a very limited number of people who learn to play instruments in a group setting from an early age, but in an age where music is basically a passive consumable, where people mostly grow up without ever handling an instrument at all, where the most likely form of self-taught play is noodling along to lead breaks on youtube, the need for structured encouragement and proper teaching was never higher

Re: beginners’ hour

I haven’t a bloody clue, to be honest, but I’ve wondered about this myself.

The best theory I can come up with is that the ONLY way to control the timing and phrasing on the pipes -- and at the heart of it, the length of the notes -- is by wiggling your fingers around. Of course, this is true of other instruments but on the fiddle you have the bow, the flute and whistle you have your breath, banjo you have your strummy hand, and the box you have the squeezy bellows. So all of these instruments have a degree of “natural” phrasing that’s just a part of making the things work. You’re forced to articulate the phrasing in some way. Pipes don’t have that. So until the player develops enough awareness and control, especially over ornaments/articulations, their phrasing is going to be all over the place.

Just reading the interesting dialogue between ethical and the Doc, re phrasing and rhythm.

I think what Doc says makes perfect sense - in my understanding, melody + drones (no regulators) played on the pipes has no dynamics, ie it’s always at a fixed volume, so the rhythm / phrasing has to be achieved by the time spacing between notes.

Totally different on box etc, as mentioned before - I’d guess that the box (possibly out of all instruments) has the capability of the most powerful dynamics / accents (and I’m meaning volume fluctuations).

Re: beginners’ hour

I attended my first slow session last night. The session started with a review of two tunes learned at the previous session. Each tune was played a half dozen times or so. Then the core players played a few tunes up tempo for the patrons. After that we learned two new tunes bar by bar using a combination of dots and ear. The leader would play a bar a few times, we would play it together, and then the leader would tell us to close our eyes and we would play it a few more times. Somehow she always caught me if I tried to peek. Eventually we played lines, parts, and finally, the tunes through several times. Then members of the core group each led a set of tunes at full tempo which, again, the patrons much appreciated. We finished off with a closing song sung acapella.

I played about an hour out of the two and a half hour session which was all my fingers could take. It was a little nerve wracking at first especially committing to an upper register A or B on the whistle. Folks were very nice and dedicated to teaching. We meet once in December and then go to twice a month at the New Year. I was very happy with the way the session flowed and the ratio of playing to listening. It was well worth driving 30 miles each way.
