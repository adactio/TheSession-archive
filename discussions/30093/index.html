Chord Substitutions

I’ve been reading Chris Smith’s instruction material regarding chord substitutions;
http://coyotebanjo.com/music-48.html

While I’m not a chord player (I play flute & whistle) I often play with players who do. Some of whom are good at harmonizing (mostly those who also play the tunes); & others less certain about which chords to play (often asking for chords, perhaps feeling clueless).

The reason I’m posting on the topic of playing chords with trad tunes, even though I currently only play melody, comes down to a question of whether information given about “how to sort it out” is either well thought out & applicable or not.

So, the page I linked imho is ‘well thought out & applicable’. If I were to begin playing chords today this (from Chris Smith’s instruction) is where I would begin;

“If you play melodies as well as chords, try experimenting with different/substitution chords when you FIRST start learning the melody, so that right from the start, you’re aware of the various possible different sounds. Most Irish tunes fall into one of four modes (scales)”

Yes, no, maybe?

Also would like to hear responses regarding the entire article on chord substitutions.

Thanks in advance,
Ben

Hmm, I think it’s a mistake to get too hung up on the issue of chords in accompaniment. The rhythm is so much more important. A good backer with a good right hand can make a one-note drone accented in the right places with sparse chord application sound really good for the duration of a whole tune. There are also other ways to figure out what chords are going to sound good, like becoming adept at “hearing” a nicely shaped bass progression in a tune and letting that dictate what chords should be voiced over the bass.

Re: Chord Substitutions

Substitution of different harmony scenarios to Trad Irish Tunes is what us backers do. One should not think along the lines of each tune having its own chord sequence. I hardly ever play the same thing to the same tune twice!
Remember us backers are the ones really improvizing. We should be well versed enough in both general harmony & rhythm AND the Trad Irish idiom to supply an appropriate background. We are busking in the old sense; e.g. when the word was used by pub’ piano players to extemporize a piano accompaniment to an ad hoc singer in what ever key they happened to sing in!
This background maybe just percussive (deadened strings or bashing the top of your piano/ harmonium/ harp/ Vulcan arse flute), drones or bass lines, dyads, full triad chords or even the occaisional extended and/ or altered chord.
‘Substituting chords’ is a automatic process if you know your stuff! The stuff in the article about substituting two chords that share two notes is very good advice. Just playing dyads is a good safe and clever ploy. E.G. If part of a tune is in G one can happily use the root (G) with a fifth (D) and leaving the D ringing and simply changing the G to A gives a dyad that implies the dominant fifth but just moving one note about; not a whole fistful!

Sorry this is such a rantish posting! It’s difficult to explain all this in one hit!

In most ‘popular music’, jazz and the like in the last cnetury or so the solo or melody players improvized around a presripted harmony sequence, whether something as simple as a pop song or a bluegrass tune or something as complex as bebop or the more adventurous avant garde contemporary stuff. In Trad the tune is set (apart from wonderful ornaments and the like) and the harmony is busked appropriately.

Another thing for the all round backer to pay attention to is all the sounds that occur other than the tune: the backing stuff that’s always been there i.e. regulators, box basses plus the drones and occasional twonks on bottom strings of the fiddles, banjo-ness (as I have called it before, that is the strange middle ground between tune playing and backing that banjos often seem to inhabit!) and so on. You must be a mimic. Listen to the whole lovely mess and try and play a reduction in four voices of all you hear.

This is why a backer has to be as au fait with the tradition as the ‘melody players’ claim to be (!). Due to the ad hoc selection of harmony one has to be aware of which choices from the palette of chords are gonna be ‘traditional’ sounding. Another thing to consider is that a lot of (other) music relies on the harmonic architecture being novel and adventurous (e.g. Beatles, bebop, Mozart variations). It’s what makes it sound new and different. Often the melodies taken away from the chordal settings are fairly straightforward diatonic.

OK!

It’s very difficult to write a chord sheet with the basic text doodah that theession.org has and is very unsatisfactory and ifractionally inaccurate but here goes:

Chords are all for 2 beats except those marked with an asterisk (*) which are one beat. The Em chord stream at the start of the second part are a beat each.

I did this using Sibelius score writing software and if you email me (via thesesseion.org) and I’ll send you the Sib file and the MP3 audio. I scored it for flute, solo violin and honky tonk piano. Sib users will know why I used honky tonk!

| B7 b10 (anacrusis)

First part:
|| C#m7b5 Cmaj7 | B7+5 *Em7 *A9 | Dmaj7 Bm9 | Gmaj9 D |
| C#m7b5 Cmaj7 | B7+5 *Em7 *A9 | F#m7 Bm9 | Gmaj7 *B7b10 *Em7 | | repeat

Second part:
|| Em Em(D# bass) Em(D bass) Em(C# bass) | Cmaj7 *B7b10 *Em7|
| Ebo | *Ebo *Gmaj7 |
| Em Em(D# bass) Em(D bass) Em(C# bass) | *Cmaj7 *B7b10 *Em7 *A7 | F#m7 Bm9 | E7+5 A7 || repeat

Starting with the bass in the C# sort of defines it straight into a sort of Edor. Of course the subsequent harmonies borrow chromatically. Using the seventh with a flattened tenth flavour of B hints at a bona fide dominant seventh i.e. with the leading note D# for Em but also has the flattened third (D) of the dorian mode thus implying modal duality and delious ambiguity.
The Minor chord with the chromatically descending bass line cliche that starts the second part ,hopefully messes up the expected harmonic frame and leads to a very non trad diminished seventh. Tthe second time it resolves to a jazz like walk around the fifths.
I think the overall effect is less jazz than I hoped and rather like a early 20th century art song setting of a folk tune: think Bunting or Corigliano et cetera…

In my own experience and suffering of novice and other backers, by far the most common problem has been a general lack of understanding of Modes, which directly equates to a lack of understanding of the tune.

Enharmonics: Naming an extended chord b10 as opposed to #9.
To me flattened tenth implies adding on a flattened (i.e. minor) third additionally to the natural (major) third but an octave up. A b10 chord is supposed to imply a chord with both major and minor third. The third up an octave is a tenth. Calling it an added sharpened ninth (i.e. a second added an octave higher but sharpened) is just the same thing but, for me, doesn’t emphasize the duality of the major & minor thirds; rather more implies a BLooZ note. This is popularly known amongst six string devil botherers as the “Hendrix” chord (e.g. Purple Haze, Foxy Lady).
7+5 or 7b13? ‘Seventh augmented fifth’ means: root; third (maj); augmented (sharpened) fifth; dominant seventh. ‘Seventh flattened thirteenth’ means: root, third, fifth, seventh, ninth, eleventh and altered (flattened) thirteenth. The former has both a natural and a sharpened fifth (although it is easier to express them both by adding the altered one as a flattened sixth albeit an octave higher (13th). Also any thirteenth chord (altered or not) should include all the other extensions as listed. E.g. a ninth implies the addition of a 9th and a 7th. An eleventh implies an eleventh, a ninth and a seventh- and the third remains as well. (by the way: an 11th is not the same as a sus4. 7sus4 or even a 9sus4- these don’t have the third in them.)
