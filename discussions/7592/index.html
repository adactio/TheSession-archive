Why must people play so freaking fast??!! Meh.

I don’t see the point. I suppose for set dancers, I can understand [from what I’ve heard/know] playing a hornpipe at almost reel-speed but I’m not talking about that!! I’m talking about sessions. Sessions!!!

At tonight’s session, I’ve never played a reel so fast in my life. It was horrible. And when I mean “so fast” I really do mean 8340293409823 miles an hour.

The tempo I like is the one where I can hear the tune. I like those. Unfortunately, in my case, whenever I try to start a tune/s [after a good deal of fast-paced tunes] that I think is at a nice slow pace, it really isn’t and it’s so unsatisfying by the time the set is finished. It’s the influence of the sessions I go to!!! Well… Maybe musicians rather.

Anyone have the same problem? Or anyone feel the same way? How fast do you play? Is it satisfying? Why?

---

So this is pretty much a venting post for me, but I still want opinions!!! Forgive me if I forgot something or am missing something here. It’s very late and I’m quite upset. And school’s tomorrow. Farewell summer!!

Cheers-ish?
Armand

↳ Posted by Ptarmigan on August 31st, 2005.

Re: Why must people play so freaking fast??!! Meh.

There’s a big difference between playing fast, and speeding up someone else’s tune (Which should be a hanging offense, at the very least, probably after some sort of ghastly torture or evisceration or something). Some of the best sessions I go to tend to be pretty fast. Some people are so good they can lift everyone and take them all flying at a pace and level of musical quality that it’s really exhilarating. Some people play faster than they are able to think and sound appalling. Some people want to be Martin Hayes and play in a drear and deadly fashion that destroys the tune as surely as playing it so fast that all the bends straighten out. It’s not a black and white thing. Different tempos can bring out different character in tunes. Or indeed some tunes will always be destroyed by too much velocity. Sometimes it’s nice to play in a laid-back, relaxed fashion. Sometimes it’s great to just ‘go for it’ and play flat out, just for the devil of it.
One generalisation I might offer is that the size of the session has a bearing on how good certain tempos sound. A big session can’t really do ‘relaxed swing’ as well as a little session, so speeds tend to be higher, and they suit a fast stomping sort of sound, whereas small sessions are more versatile … (I’m sure that someone will shoot me down in flames here)
But although there is a tendency amongst some people to play ‘too fast’, it’s because it’s too fast for them, not necessarily too fast for the music.

↳ Posted by Ptarmigan on August 31st, 2005.

Re: Why must people play so freaking fast??!! Meh.

Armand asks why. One reason could be that as we all learn the music, one thing that we strive for is to play faster, to play “up to speed”. So speed becomes a measure of success from the very beginning.

I think there is a tipping point where people can manage speed physically. (This tipping point occurs at different times based on the instrument played.) There is great joy in this, being able to manage speed. To be able to keep up with so-and-so. Never mind what it sounds like.

The ear eventually catches up and one realizes, hopefully, that yes it is fast, but no, it doesn’t sound all that good. How come I can play as fast as so-and-so but it doesn’t sound as good?

Speed can also cover up missing notes and a lack of other skills. So it can seem that you are playing better when you really are not playing better.

--------------------------
As to the other side of the conversation - speed is relative. Al has a good point that “dance speed” is a good measure.

What I particulary dislike around this subject is reverse prejudice from people who do not practice their main instrument enough to play at even danceable speed. People that don’t understand that playing at dance speed is not playing too fast.

And I am not talking about speeding up sets here. I try not to do that, especially for people I know are beginners.

↳ Posted by Jode on August 31st, 2005.

Re: Why must people play so freaking fast??!! Meh.

A number of possible reasons occur to me why today’s set dancers (though mercifully by no means all) are so fast.

1. They are generally fitter and faster than their grandparents’ generation. Few of them, for instance, now come into the kitchen or church hall or the cross roads for set dancing after a day’s hard physical labour in the fields or the home.
2. They use specialised dancing shoes, and, where ever possible, dance on fast, smooth floors.
3. Let us not forget the set dancing competitions, which engender a competitive spirit, thus encouraging speed and an athletic level of fitness.
4. The faster pace of living today, and the speed of playing in other music genres. Classical playing today is faster than it was before the recording media were invented. I’ve been told on good authority that professional symphony orchestras today play up to 10% faster than their best amateur counterparts. The two orchestras I play in have professional conductors who are bringing us up to professional speeds and standards, with some success I may say - no prisoners are taken. This is what the public now expect, for better or for worse.

These factors conspire to increase the speed of the dance music by perhaps 10% or more, compared with a couple of generations ago. It only needs a small number of good players who are used to these speeds to introduce them, perhaps unintentionally, into sessions for the damage to be done.

Most bands who do a lot of ceili work also produce CDs, which again introduce the high speed of the music to the rest of us.

I know from personal experience that when you get used to set dancing speeds those can become the norm that you strive for, and some session playing can seem unaccountably slow when the ceili guys aren’t present :-)

Trevor

To me, one of the beauties of this music has always been how most of it can be played with a metronome setting anywhere between 60 and 200 and enjoyed immensly, albeit in different ways. I have heard beautiful renditions of “Fig for a Kiss” played on wooden flute at a slow tempo. I have also heard the same tune played very fast, and it was beautiful in an altogether different way. A tune played at 60 is really a different musical experience - almost a different tune - than the same tune played at 100, which is different yet from 130, and 170 is an altogether different creature yet. The issues I see are:

1. is the tune played well at the tempo it is being played at?

2. is the tune being played at a tempo appropriate for the situation? A bush leaguer sitting in a major league session going full bore is one situation. A virtuoso sitting in with a group of amateurs is a different situation. (Having said that, I know that there are a lot of amateur virtuosos) And there are many other situations - each having it’s own character that should be respected. Ptarmigan’s idea that Cape Breton music caused Scottish music to speed up strikes me as odd. I don’t know enough Scottish music to know whether or how it’s changed, but Cape Breton music isn’t all that fast. Tempos fall into a very narrow range, probably because everybody dances and everybody plays for dancers all the time. I haven’t gathered the data yet, but a few samples suggest that reels are nearly all in the 112-116 range with a few exceptions as low as 108 or as high as 120. There’s a total absence of what you find in Irish music, with lots of players in the 90-108 range and a bunch breaking 120 regularly.

Also, tempos are rock solid. Once they hit the reels in those marathon sets that might start with an air or a march and then a few strathspeys, there’s virtually no change in tempo. All tempo changes occur when they change type of tune or else during the strathspeys.

I’ll clarify the panel discussion a little.

Joanie Madden, Mick Moloney, and Liz Carroll all agreed that their influence as professionals may have had something to do with the increase in speed of the music in the States (the panel was about the state of Irish music in the States.) They all nodded their heads when Joannie talked about her father making the argument that the next generation “played too fast” and she talked about not being able to play with him and the other CTL fathers unless they played at a slower tempo. The consensus was that much of the beauty of the music was lost at Warp 10 and also that there were tunes that stood up well to an increase in tempo. I think that the concern was that every tune was played fast and that a lot was lost in the process.
The same thing happened in Bluegrass music. Any perusal of early Bill Monroe or Flatt and Scruggs will surprise you with the moderate pace of the music. Granted a few tunes are lightning fast but most were not. As the years went on and others played the same tunes on record, the speed went up. It may just be that the music is so compelling that adding speed is a normal part of the evolution of tunes. Especially as the players are more technically adept. The real problem may come when players not prepared to play at those speeds do it anyway because that is what they think they have to do.
