The art of “Faking it”.

This post might be considered to be in same vein as those on “noodling” or the practice of playing when you don’t know a tune but, perhaps, we could approach it from a different angle.
There are many expert “fakers” who attend sessions. I remember meeting a guy at a folk festival several years ago who appeared to play (tenor banjo, sometimes fiddle)every tune in the session, except that he didn’t really play any of them at all or very few. He actually sounded very effective and his “playing” seemed (to the casual listener at least)to fit in with the overall sound and not clash with the other instruments. In fact, when I explained that I didn’t know many of the tunes, he offered me advice as to how I could still play. Over the years, I noticed that he still continued in the same manner and had learned no new tunes. By this time, of course, I had learned many more without having to “fake it”.
I’m wondering, therefore, whether it’s ever appropriate to “fake it” during a session. Can it ever be useful? I’ve done it myself in some situations where it didn’t matter too much but my normal behaviour is not to play and “Listen, listen, listen” if I don’t know a tune. Of course, if the tune gets into my head, I will often have a go at playing it. That’s slightly different, though. The strange thing is that this guy and others like him probably have the potential to be very musical if they put the same effort into learning the actual tunes as they do into “faking it”. What do you all think?

Re: The art of “Faking it”.

I think there are various grades of “faking it”.
At one extreme you have the player in the session who is basically just miming. To the punters he seems to be playing but, in fact, no sound is coming out.
It’s easier to do this on some instruments than on others. On some it’s practically impossible. Such a person just wants to be “in the photograph” and be seen by the punters at the session.
But then you have players who are learning tunes on the fly, and they could be at any stage of the learning curve of a particular tune.
I’ve learnt large parts of some tunes on the fly at sessions, but I’ve always worked on them at home and finally got them right.
When you’re learning tunes on the fly there’ll always be a turn or two that you haven’t quite got yet, so your fingers keep moving over the buttons (concertina, for me) but you don’t push or pull the bellows.
I’m talking about parts of the tune that last no more than a second or two, and the player could be said to be “faking it” for those brief intervals.
Of course, the important thing about learning on the fly is not to be heard if you play a bum note, but all this has been covered on another thread.
I don’t think I’d want to play at a session with someone who was “faking it” just to be in the photo.

Re: The art of “Faking it”.

There’s another type of fakery that’s perhaps more familiar to the classical musicians, but can also be found in sessions.

It’s when a tune you know and play reasonably well suddenly takes off at a rate of knots and you realise you haven’t quite got the technique to play it at that speed. What do you do - stop playing, and look slightly foolish if it’s a common session tune? Or do you, as I’ve done on occasion, slightly alter or leave out some notes so that I’m still playing the tune but in a slightly simplified version.

A good example is Sally Gardens. On the fiddle the opening bars can be a bowing nightmare for the unwary inexperienced player caught out by, for example, a silver flute going at said rate of knots. A good fail-safe ploy in this instance is to play the first E in the tune on the A-string rather than as an open E. This will stabilise the bowing at speed.

Translated to the classical sphere, a lot of modern (and sometimes not so modern) orchestral music is so horrendously difficult that not all of the string players can play all of the notes all of the time at the required speed. The first desk or two are usually competent enough to provide stability, but a sensible conductor, realising the technical problems for the other players will often tell them to simplify certain passages, as long as they are with the conductor’s beat at the beginning of each bar - this is the crucial factor. Concerto soloists, especially the pianists, have also been known to simplify spectacular florid passages in performance, and they get away with it because the orchestra is being very busy and is playing fortissimo at that point.

At the other end of the scale we have the example of Heifetz who admitted to playing a note in an octave passage very slightly out of tune so that the audience would know he was playing in octaves!

Re: The art of “Faking it”.

No matter what you do, someone is going to be irritated. If you don’t really know the tune all that well and don’t play along, someone’s going to think you hate them because you didn’t join in. If you DO join in and somebody else notices you left out this or that roll, or that you’re playing something that fits perfectly but isn’t quite right, you’ll irritate them as well.

If you’re a decent faker, you should try to get to know the other players’ attitudes enough to guess whether they would rather you play with them despite a few grey areas, or whether they would rather not have their tune corrupted by imperfection. In my experience there are an equal number of both types of players.

If you’re *not* a decent faker - well, we just won’t go there.

To follow up on my previous post….

I hear Jack trying to nurture a simple idea here: When people walk into a session, it would be decent of them to listen a bit, before attempting to join in, to determine what sort of session it is (a “jam,” with no small amount of noodling/faking/improvising/learning tunes on the fly going on, or a tight, clean rendition of tunes by people all playing predominantly the same notes, or somewhere along the spectrum between these two ends).

To the extent that many people don’t do this, or that they waltz in without checking their own expectations at the door, somebody’s nose gets out of joint. As I said above, in my experience, that’s life--not just sessions. (In fact, in contrast to board meetings and family reunions, most established sessions I’ve been to somehow manage to make their expectations known, so noses stay in place and everybody’s happy.)

Seems to me each of us has our own threshold for when noodling/faking/improvising/learning tunes on the fly becomes bothersome, and for some of us that threshold may change depending on the situation, our mood, etc., on any given night. Which makes it all the more difficult for a group of players to reach agreement about what the threshold should be for a session, on that night. Particularly if the door isn’t locked and newcomers keep walking in and taking empty seats.

Most of the sessions I’ve been to deal with this one of four ways - by: (1) working to include the range of expectations while minimizing the friction; (2) talking with the people whose expectations seem most out of synch with the group that night to explain the situation and asking them to adapt their playing to the group’s; (3) overtly slagging or shunning those don’t cooperate; and (4) limiting the circle to like-minded players.

Some of this is made explicit, some is unspoken, and sometimes a “leader” steps forward to steer the group one direction or the other.

It’s important to say that I’ve seen these tactics work both ways - used to compel noodlers to stop and, in other cases, used to encourage a session to become more seat-of-the-pants, more improvised, perhaps less polished.

But most of the sessions I’ve been to unfold largley on their own, with understated guidance from a senior player or two perhaps, but with great generosity of spirit, tolerance for mistakes and miscues, and respect for people’s differences. I think those traits are at least as important as the tunes, are at least as central to the tradition. But maybe I’ve just been lucky.
