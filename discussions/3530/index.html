Re: Does your session have a leader?

I would disagree with what you said in another thread, PP. Most sessions have at least de facto leaders, unless all the participants are very evenly matched in their musical ability/repetoire. You’ll often find that one or two of the stronger players will lead most of the tunes, although there’s nothing to stop somebody else starting a tune. I think it’s better to have one or two people who do this as it helps to keep the session flowing. In most cases, it’s an unofficial thing and occurs with the tacit agreement of the other session participants. It’s not a matter of the “leader(s)” imposing their will on the whole group or deliberating “hogging the tunes” for the sake of it.

On other occasions, you’ll maybe get sessions advertised as “Pied Piper and friends :-)” or similar but, in effect, these are often just open sessions. However, in such cases the named person who is supposed to be in charge is normally expected to keep the thing going if it starts to flag.

Re: Does your session have a leader?

i can see both sides of this question. in the past, i have been paid to “run” sessions by a pub landlord. if the attending musicians are a good crowd with some musical talent, it can be a doddle - just point them in the right direction and they run all night. if they aren’t, it can be very hard work indeed trying to find enough common ground that some of them will be able to join in with most things. at the moment, i seem to spend a lot of my time at sessions where, quite deliberately, there is no leader at all - the music either flows or it doesn’t, as the mood of the musicians goes. the “directed” session generally should be a good entertainment experience for the punters; the “flow” session is capable of being better - but can also be much worse. my view would be that where the music needs to be entertaining to the punters (so as to preserve the goodwill of the pub landlord, for instance) a gently “directed” session may be a good idea - and may also be helpful to beginners who just want to soak up the atmosphere and tunes. where the only point is the music itself (a rarity in the pub session but true of a couple of those that i frequent at the moment) the “let it flow” approach is rather better - but may make for some quiet nights if the muse isn;t with you.

The best session I’ve been to, Gleeson’s in Coore, Co. Clare, was very clearly led by Jackie Daly. He started the vast majority of sets of tunes, playing a few bars of each so everyone knew what tunes to expect. He invited most of the others present to lead one set each (maybe everyone got a turn - I missed the first 45 minutes or so). Kitty Hayes was there, and she got to play four or five sets of tunes in a row (she plays most tunes a whole step lower than usual because of the keys her concertina is pitched in, so she was joined just by Peter Laban on C whistle and C pipes). Everyone seemed comfortable with the format.

I read somewhere (maybe here quite some time ago) that in West Clare the norm is that the oldest player gets to start all the sets.

At our session in Massachusetts, we have a leader who worked hard for several years to build the session up from the beginning, but now just lets it flow mostly on its own, stepping in from time to time to make sure everyone gets a chance to play or sing.

I went to the session that Brian Conway leads in White Plains, New York a few months ago. On the surface he led it much the same way that Jackie Daly led the one in Coore, but the two were very different. Daly was the senior player in an existing session where the musicians were clearly playing for themselves. Conway was the (paid, I think) founder of a session with an invited featured guest musician, playing (amplified) mostly for the pub patrons.

And I went to a nice session near Chicago led by two young women, a fiddler and a harpist. Again, it was like the Coore session. They started nearly all of the sets, but made sure everyone got a chance to start a set. It seems to be a model that works well if the leader has the respect of the other players.

Re: Does your session have a leader?

I don’t know that I “lead” our local session, but I do put a fair amount of time into organizing it. The cowboy culture in Montana doesn’t exactly nurture Irish trad music, so if we want it to happen, we have to work at it a bit. I put out a weekly email “Session News” message that says the session is on for the coming week (or off, on those rare occasions), and listing any other upcoming events (ceilis, touring musicians coming to town, nearby festivals). I also coordinate with the pub owners so we have the same expectations and no one feels taken for granted. Mostly, I try to make sure that two or three musicians will show up Tuesday evening.

At the actual session, anyone can start a tune, and anyone can jump in to keep a set going. The core players tend to do this, but we also sit back and encourage other players to get their feet wet. On a slow night, I make it my job to start tunes that I know less assertive players also know and are just dying to play, and I work my way around the circle, trying to include everyone. Some of the other core players also do this.

To some extent, this fits my sense of ‘leadership,’ but without any of the dictatorial connotations. It’s more like being a convenor or facilitator.

Re: Does your session have a leader?

If you have a group consisting of more than one person then, since no two persons are ever equal in all respects, there will be one person present who will be more inclined to take charge than the other(s). Whether you term this person a leader, sensei, guru, tutor, teacher, facilitator (I like that one), etc is beside the point. Such “leadership” is a natural function of social beings, whether humans or animals.

In a music session, if there is a significant number of beginners or intermediate players present then it is essential to have a single person there to whom they can look for authority, guidance and help, otherwise no one’s interest will be served. and the session is likely to degenerate fairly quickly.

If a session consists mostly of advanced players and beyond then leadership may be more subtle, may not be defined as such, and perhaps not obvious to an external observer. Such leadership may in fact pass from one person to another many times in the course of the evening. Some of the sessions I attend come into this category.

BTW, I would define an “advanced” player as someone who has sound technique and musicianship, can play at any reasonable session speed, has a respectable number of tunes in the bag, can get to grips with most new session tunes quickly, and can lead off at least a set or two of tunes when required.

I suppose the main bias in my concept of leadership may come from my rock climbing, wherein I used to teach and take groups out to the crag and so had assimilated theory in group psychology. However, the concept I read about back then certainly diffentiated leadership from the exertion of power and authority (a lesson important to learn when dealing with troubled kids).

Anyway, I got this from one of my texts, and its pretty close to what I think of as leadership:

Leadership is a functional relationship between a person and group rather than a complex characteristic of an individual. Leaders arise through the active process of people interacting to satisfy their needs, and the leader is frequently that role which orders and plans group activity. The leader is regarded by the group as a means of satisfying needs, is often chosen by means of conscious or sub-conscious assent with this end in view and continue to lead so long as this function occurs. Artificially appointed leaders face the difficulty of non-correspondence between their needs and those of workers. The methods of coping with this non-correspondence have, in the past, included force and paternalism, but mutual cooperation in a common endeavor is found much more frequently today. This last device, to the extent that it can be realized, is the key to increasing the security of appointed leaders in their appointed roles.

.. coming late in a thread is sometimes like coming late home to talk about the great news to find everybody asleep, but here’s my two cents….

Leadership is usually associated with getting things organized and well-run should thus usually be a benefit to any session, given that the leadership is not out of place.

The most challenging situation is, however, the one where there is a big diversity in the skill level and no leadership.

Some such sessions are unfortunately ruled by the “least common denominator” Taking a leadership role in a session where the regulars in an established session are less proficient and less ambitous than your standards may not always be a good idea.

There’s a chineese saying that it is easy to move mountains, but difficult to change people. Moving to a new pub or venue may be a better long-term option than try to take leadershp in an established session .
