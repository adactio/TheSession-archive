Re: Name that reel?

Okay, here goes.

You mentioned the chords A7, Bm7, C#m7b5, Dmaj7 Emin7, F#m7, Gmaj7 for playing over Amix. The reality is that these chords don’t work particularly well because they’re too full sounding, not because the mode’s “wrong”.

That’s why in trad music modal tunes are harmonised with triads or power chords. Think about it, the key of G major is also a mode - the ionian mode, right? Now, if you insisted on harmonising it using extended chords like 7ths (Gmaj7, Am7, Bm7, Cmaj7 etc), or 9ths (Gmaj9, Am9 etc…) and *all* your G chords had extensions, like 7, 9, 11 etc, then your trad accompaniment is not going to sound trad, and the melody players aren’t going to like you for it, just like they’re not going to like that extended A7 over the mixo tune.

Ok so that’s not to say you can’t use extensions in trad; of course you can, but the message is, use them sparingly, whether you’re in your ionian, aeolian, mixo, dorian or whatever.

Am I right in assuming you’re from a jazz guitar background? Because with jazz it’s different. With jazz you *want* extensions and alterations for that jazzy sound, so even if your “tune” is in a diatonic mode like Amixo, you’re probably going to want to use chords like A7or A9 or whatever more frequently than you would in trad. And this is what it boils down to: you’re viewing modes as they are used in the context of jazz, but really modes have had different functions and different treatments in different genres right from day one, sometimes without any harmonisation at all (think of plainsong). What do you think an Ancient Greek would think if you told him his tune couldn’t possibly be in a particular mode because you can’t play C#m7b5 over it?

Another issue is that you’re trying to force classical terminology onto this music, even though it’s inappropriate. I’m paraphrasing here, but you talk of the “home” chord of Amixo having to function as a “dominant”, which implies that Amixo can only exist within the context of a Dmaj tune. Think about it - that’s what you’re implying, since D is the tonic related to that dominant. Well, in Dmaj - and let’s not forget that this is the ionian mode, your 3 main chords (disregarding minor substitutions and that II-V-I progression that classical theorists stress the importance of) are your tonic D, subdominant G, and dominant A, right? Well, in Amixo, you still have 3 main chords, A, G and D, but their functions are different (if for example you’re using them to harmonise trad on guitar). A in this case is your home chord. G is your next most important chord. Classical musicians would be tempted to call it some sort of dominant given that it’s of secondary importance, and they get round it by calling it a “subtonic” (flat 7).

The important thing to remember here is that there’s no change of key, as you said, otherwise there’d be a change of key signature. But there isn’t. That’s like saying that when you change chords from G to C to D in a Gmaj tune you’re changing key each time. But you’re not. You’re just using the chords available to you in the key of Gmaj (which, let’s remember, is G ionian).

So going back to your Amixo, your 3rd most important chord is your D which pivots between the A and the G. What are we to call that? Calling it dominant or subdominant is confusing because that expresses nothing about its function as do the classical terms for the chords for harmonising the ionian mode. This is where classical theory slips up. Then you’ve got your Bm, F#m and Em which are simply minor substitutions of the 3 main chords. Any extensions you use like 7ths or 9ths are not particularly functional in the harmony or indicative of anything significant, other than to a jazz musician or a trad backer who wants to spice up his chord repertoire with some fuller sounding chords.

So to conclude, the major scale is just one mode of 7, and arbitrarily it has become the one to be used most often in classical music. That’s why it’s the scale that has been given names for each of its notes, e.g. supertonic. But if things had been different historically, the mixolydian scale could have ended up being the most popular, and it could have been given completely different jargon for each of its notes. It just so happens that formally it never has.

And just as a late addition to this post: I’ve mentioned this problem with terminology before on this website, and after some discussion with other members via threads + email, come up with some very simple and useful terminology for backing trad music. “Home”, “Pivot”, “Contraster”.

Dmaj (ionian): D = home, G = pivot, A = contraster (in classical theory they are tonic, subdominant, dominant as you know, but that doesn’t help us with the other modes). All other main chords are minor substitutions, and your dim on the leading note is only really useful functioning as an A7 chord without the A.

E dorian: Em = home, A = pivot, D = contraster (you know how Edor tunes often go Em-D-Em-D etc, and the A pivots nicely between them). And notice also that E aeolian would use Em, D, C as the 3 main chords, and that progressions using these are also useful in the dorian, even when there’s a C# being played in the melody.

A mixo: A = home, D = pivot, G = contraster, plus minor substitutions. (Notice there is an interval of a perfect 4th between each of the 3 main chords, just as there is in dorian.

Bmin (aeolian): Bm = home, G = pivot, A = contraster. No surprise that this is the same as D ionian, but for the home chord, seeing as the aeolian is the relative mode of the ionian.

Nuff theory for ya?

AlBrown, I didn’t really even try and explain why A-D-G is such a powerful progression in Amix tunes. I would say it’s for 3 reasons:

The first is that D pivots so well because A-D-G mirrors the II-V-I shape that is so widely used in most types of Western music, be it classical, jazz or trad. The strength of the chord progresson lies in the movement of it’s bass in perfect 4ths (or perfect 5ths depending on how you look at it. The only thing is that in the mixo it’s really I-IV-bVII. However - and this is important - the fact that it mirrors the II-V-I causes some theorists to argue that you’re actually using two tonics in the one tune, one on A, and one on G, hence the term “subtonic”. I personally don’t like this theory and think it’s a copout for classical musicians to be able to talk about b7 chords convincingly. I think it’s flawed because it doesn’t take account of how things function in other modes such as the lydian, which don’t have that b7.

The second reason is the strength of the leading note. The 3rd of the A chord is C# which “leads” into D. Then the 3rd of the D chord is F# which “leads” into G. So then even if you do the whole thing in power chords and miss out all the 3rds, it still sounds great because your ear can fill in the missing detail if it likes just from hearing the bassline.

I’d also say (and this is just a personal theory of mine) that major chords tend to take precedence over minor chords in the style of most backers of trad. The minor chords are often better used as substitutes, even in tunes which are in the minor mode. So for example I’d say that for an Emin tune, it sounds better to use D and C as your staple chords and substitute them with Bm and Am for added flavour. This is because in trad, the melodies of tunes in minor modes often outline the major chords like b7. Put simply, you’re more likely to get |B2GB EBGB|A2FA DAFA| than |B2GB EBGB|F2DF B,FDF|.

Dow, what I mean is that the 6 or 9 don’t imply strong movement, mainly colour (or color for our pan-pacific cousins). A cadd9 is still a C chord, yet a C7 or C9(implying a b7) shouts out “we’re moving”. It comes down to tension and release. 6, 9, etc only add a small amount of tenson, as does the minor substitution you were talking about. It’s the use of the b7 of the main chord that creates the most tension(plus altered 9’s etc, but let’s not go there in this yarn). A minor substitution that uses the b7 of the main chord functions as a tension point. So, a chord progression of G, G7, C could be substituted with G, Bm7b5, C. The Bm7b5(VII chord in ionian mode) is substituting for the G7(giving you a G9 without a G and a moving bass line ).

To sum up, minor chords that add only a small amount of tension are used to create movement and colour, as is using 6/9 of the major chord, the 6/9 having less movement than the minor substitutions.

Phantom, I’m glad you’re signalling your readiness to listen. What I’ve been saying all along is that the classical terminology was invented specifically for the major (ionian) scale, and so I think it is inappropriate to try and use those terms for the modes we use in Irish music.

So in classical music we have this:

I - tonic
II - supertonic
III - mediant
IV - submediant
V - dominant
VI - submediant
bVII - subtonic
VII - leading note

Whilst I’d *like* to be able to simply transfer these terms for use with other modes in the way that you do, Jack, my reservation is that:

1) you’d be calling the bVII the dominant, which makes sense since it functions like the V in the ionian as you said, but there already exists the term subtonic http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtonic, which was presumably invented so that classical musicians could talk about modulations from minor keys to their relative majors (since for example in the key of Amin, the chord G is the dominant of the relative major C). So since there already exists a term in that “set” of terms, then it could cause ambiguity if you formalised it in an Irish music context.

2) My second main problem with it is that some of the terms in that “set” relate specifically to their positions in the ionian mode only, such as the mediant, which means “midway between the tonic and dominant”, and the submediant, which means “the middle point of the triad on the subdominant”. These terms in particular tell us more about the note’s placement in the scale than its function. Having said that, there is no such problem with the terms “dominant” and “subdominant”, but does it make sense to choose 2 terms and dispense with the others, when you could just dispense with them all and invent new, better ones that *do* describe the functions of these triads in *all* environments. Does that not make more sense, especially with regard to my first point about the overlap with the term “subtonic”?

It’s like, if you were to use all the terms in the set rather than pick or choose, what would your mediant be in the mixolydian mode, if mediant means “midway between the tonic and dominant”? It’d be halfway between the G and the A in Amixo = G#, which isn’t even in the mixo scale. I just think that that overcomplicates things.

I dunno, Jack. I just think that this stuff could be made a lot simpler with terms that reflect the reality of the patterns that occur between relative modes as they actually occur in Irish music, instead of using a set of terms that have obviously been invented specifically for the ionian mode. Consider that many classical theorists *still* have a problem with consecutive 5ths, and so would be dismissive of all those Irish tunes that go from I to bVII anyway, on the basis that it’s fundamentally “non-musical” and “wrong-sounding”. I’ve studied classical music to a fairly high level, and I can think of a few people in that “scene” who would have taken this attitude, unfortunately.

Interesting point, Cuch, about the IV not seeming as necessary. Maybe it’s because of the very different ways in which the tunes are structured depending on the mode. In dorian tunes, espeically the more traditional ones, you often find that you’ll get one full bar of I then one full bar of bVII, or 2 full bars of each, like |A2cA eAcA|G2BG dGBG|, and that or something similar can be repeated until the end of the part. This rigid structure means that you could view IV as being unnecessary, but if you want to work it in, you can play the IV over the last part of the I phrase, so you get chord progs like this:

Am///|Am/D7/|G///|Em///|
or
Am/D/|G///|Am/D/|Em/G/| etc

Try it for tunes like the Bag Of Spuds, Old Copperplate, Swallowtail, Pigeon On The Gate etc.
