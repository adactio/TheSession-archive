what other people think

I’m mulling over a paradox about being thin-skinned & playing the music. While so much of the tradition depends on listening to others & valuing and/or integrating what they have to say, as evidenced by this site, there’s also an emphasis to ‘find your own style’ & ‘make the tune your own.’ It’s unlike succeeding in the business world or in athletics, say, where your prowess is usually determined financially or statistically, not stylistically, or in medicine, based on patient outcomes, or any other discipline where status is really measurable or quantifiable. I think so much of this tradition is subjective, ie based on what other people think, & yet, if you are on the receiving end of some bogus criticism without proper filters in place, it can be really devastating. I think many folks have probably fled the music, simply for having been on the bad end of some serious slagging, & with no defense mechanisms in place to take it in stride or to get past the hurt feelings to hopefully acknowledge some possibly well-intentioned advice.

There’s no real question here, except possibly, how thick do you think your skin has to be to survive in this Irish music subculture, & how long did it take to get that way?

Re: what other people think

Good topic. A few observations in addition to those already made:

1) As somebody said, one of the best ways to thicken the skin is to work on your chops. I recall a master-class given by John Miller Chernoff (great ethnomusicologist, learned Ghanaian drumming in Ghana and was initiated by master players, etc), wherein somebody asked him some earnest ethnomusicological question about “what should you do if you’re concerned that you might not have reached the right phenomenological insight with your informants…what if you think that perhaps they’re not revealing to you the real information that you’re seeking for your dissertation?” and without missing a beat (he was playing conga at the time) he said “well, I worry about that stuff too, but when I do, I always figure the best thing to do is work on my chops.”

I think he has a point: worry about others’ opinions is often a result of one’s own insecurity about one’s playing. Working on your chops is a way to feel stronger and more confident about that.

2) Often-times when people worry what others are thinking of their playing, it’s a product of confusing one’s own sense of self-worth with one’s skills as a player (I am guilty of this meself, constantly, despite years of trying to get over it). Another good antidote to worrying about others’ opinions is to say “I am not my playing…my playing is not me.” Makes it easy to accept constructive criticism of one’s playing without hearing it as destructive criticism of one’s self-worth.

3) This is a virtuoso music. That does *not* mean that everybody has to be a virtuoso (referencing another thread, there’s nothing wrong with being an intermediate player, *provided* you realize, accept, and appreciate that about yourself). It *does* mean, in my opinion, that to play the music right takes an astonishing amount of dedicated practice, commitment, and focus. So if you’re not a virtuoso, and you meet someone who is (even if s/he is an absolute assh*le) interpersonally, it’s best to admit that truth, and say “yes, s/he is an absolute assh*le, but I could learn something from him/her.”

4) This music is a meritocracy, not a democracy. In other words, it’s *not* primarily concerned with making sure that everyone gets “their fair share.” It is rather concerned with making the best music possible, and if that means that some folks play a lot and some folks play little, that’s the way it goes.

If you want to play more, learn more tunes.

If you want to feel confident that you can withstand what other d*ckheads might be saying about you, learn more tunes.

If you think that someone’s being a jerk to you, ask yourself “does s/he have tunes I need to learn”, and if the answer is yes, put up with the crap and learn more tunes.

5 (or is it E?)) I’m reminded of another analogy with jazz (another topic that’s been beaten into the ground): in the old days, the way you learned new skills, tested your current ones, and measured your competence, was at “cutting contests”: informal jam sessions wherein players who didn’t necessarily know one another would get up and trade improvised choruses on jazz standards. The implication was that, if you unexpectedly ran up against a player much stronger than you, you could get “cut”: shown to still have areas that needed work. Jazz players understand that the cutting contest was a very valuable means of testing skills, garnering new ideas, and taking stock of the current state of the common musical language.

And yes, it took a pretty damned “thick skin” to handle one. You had to develop an attitude of “I don’t care what kind of bullsh*t these people lay down, I’m going to learn this music.” But if you did, you could learn a lot, and the payoff would be worth it.

I think the analogy holds here.

Emily: you’ve got all the attitude, guts, and dedication it takes to hang with this music. Being tough is useful--being dedicated to the music is even more useful.

chris smith

Re: what other people think

Yeah most of the time insults are actually praise. Last night we went to see John Carty play live and everyone loved it - absolutely fantastic concert. At one point one of my friends (who has spent a lot of time in Ireland) turned to me in the middle of a soaring set of reels and said “this is such a heap of sh*te” with a totally deadpan expression, to which I made sure to respond: “well don’t just sit there, tell him so! Go up on stage and tell him to stop playing - put us out of our misery”, not: “no, actually I think he’s really good and that’s such a rude thing to say”.

On the other hand, some slagging is meant. You’ve just got to realise what’s irony and what’s not. If you think it’s meant, then yeah you do need a thick skin in that you need to ride it out and use it positively in order to improve on your playing.

Or maybe not thick skin because that implies repelling the insult completely. It’s better to have a highly absorbent skin with a very effective immune system that can turn insults into something positive. Em, that minidisc episode wasn’t very nice at the time, but it made me realise all this stuff. I remember deciding at the time that I was going to treat it as something really positive, and it worked. That happened about a year ago, and over the last year I’ve tried to improve on the weaker points of my playing. As a result, I enjoy sessions much more than I did, and that’s a great feeling.

It’s worth remembering that for every person who insults you, there’ll have been many more who have said “I like your playing”. Having someone compliment you is really nice, but it doesn’t necessarily spur you on to get better.

I think the main thing to remember is that if you can show yourself to have a thick skin, then people are less likely to slag you behind your back and more likely to comment constructively about your playing in a friendly conversation. If anyone ever comes up to you and says “I’d really like to hear you cran your D more crisply” or something, then you should feel honoured that they’ve been so honest, and practise cranning your D if you think they were right. If you concentrate on improving your D cran, then it will get better, and you’ll enjoy using it more in sessions.

Also it’s important to remember that beginners and intermediates don’t have a monopoly on being slagged. However good you are, there are going to be people out there who don’t like your playing or simply don’t like you.

Re: what other people think

One comment I’ve not yet seen made:

My observation and experience suggest that, in any session of considerable longevity and involving the same core group of people, certain patterns, behaviors, expectations, or responses develop. Such sessions are like other human communities: people get to know one another’s strengths, peculiarities, and limitations, can anticipate them, and can get fed up with them.

So if you’ve been living on the same street as someone for 40 or 50 years, and you *know* that every morning at 5am s/he gets up, kicks over the motorcycle, and lets it “warm up” for 20-30 minutes, thereby ruining everybody else’s sleep-in, then on any given morning, you can probably predict “that jackass is going to do it again.” Someone overnighting at your house for the first time might say “well, what’s your basis for thinking that?” or, if it does happen “well, can’t you just be tolerant for this one time?”

I’ve seen analogous things happen in sessions--been prone to the same expectations myself. If *every other time* some whistle player has been to the session, s/he hasn’t really learned any new tunes, but just busks along in the general vicinity of the melody, I’ll probably get fed up with it sooner or later and say something--maybe not very gently and not under my breath--like “Just play the tune or shut up!”

Someone who hadn’t been around me and the whistle player a lot, hence not knowing the past history, might conclude “well, jeez, how come that nasty banjo player is slagging that whistler who’s just trying to learn the tune?” They might conclude that I was mean.

Which I am, of course, but the above scenario would not be evidence of it, but rather of past history b/w the two parties of which the witness might not be aware.

Well, I think it’s important to realize and remember that we’ve many different levels of player here giving out, and that some things really do matter more at different levels. For instance: at my stage in playing, it really has been most important that I know fewer tunes better. Shannon Heaton drummed that into me constantly. Speed isn’t as important as playing it well. Number of tunes isn’t as important as playing the tunes I know well.

But the next stage now looms before me. The consensus is that the tunes I know, I know well and play beautifully. But I don’t play them fast enough (still keeping the sounding good) for the dancers, for instance (113 at the minimum for regular speed dancing), and I don’t know enough tunes to carry me through a session, really.

So now I get to buckle down to it…again. Speed on the tunes I know. Learning more tunes so I’m a better session player.

I would never expect a dead beginner to pick up a tune at full speed at a session, that’s a ridiculous thing to ask them to do. I want them to listen to get the feel, I want them to have the confidence to play out on the tunes they do know, and *feel* how they play it differently from everyone else and make decisions about whether that’s good or not. But eventually -- yes, you do have to learn to pick up the tunes on the fly. When the time is right, not before, not after.

That’s just the way it works. You can’t do it all at once, it’s impossible. You have to pick it up as you go. If you expect too much out of yourself or another muso, you’re just asking to fail.

Then you can let ones you’re not really bothered about slip away without worrying that you’re compromising the balance in your tune-bank!
If I look at myself honestly I’d divide the tunes I know into different categories. There are tunes I will happily lead today, current tunes if you like, which probably number about fifty to sixty. There are many other tunes that I could lead, but I would only play if that was all the other people at the session knew (very common or overused tunes like the Kesh, The Rakes, Merry Blacksmith, etc) - though it’s sometimes good to give these tunes an outing just to stir po-faced people up. There are tunes which I’m quite happy to play if someone else leads them - these includes tunes abandoned because they are too similar to other tunes I know, or which have slipped into disuse because I’ve never found anyone else to play them with me, or because for whatever reason I find difficult, or tunes which I’ve picked up at sessions and don’t even have a name for, which makes them hard for me to mentally file away, or tunes I’m in the process of learning.

I know people who only play once every month or two and always with the same people who are quite happy to have a repertoire of about twenty to thirty tunes which expands at the rate of about a tune a year!
I can’t stop myself learning new tunes, its like an addiction. But I don’t think you should confuse learning new tunes with necessarily being able to play those tunes properly. The more tunes you have, the easier it is to pick up new ones as you already have all the building blocks, but each one still needs to be learned as a proper tune in is own right, and polished up to be presented if you’re going to lead it.

Re: what other people think

Chris, I wasn’t responding directly to your earlier post with that last bit I wrote, but rather to the general discussion that’s come up in between. On what material do strong players work when they want to become better players, you ask? Well, tunes of course. As you say, they *are* the etudes. But which tunes? Unlike musics where etudes are the etudes, there’s no ordered or standard progression through the universe of tunes, as there is through the universe of etudes in musics where there are etudes. Thus in ITM, the onus is on the learner or teacher (if there is one) to answer the question “What do I do next?” The answer ought to be something like “learn to play rolls”, “improve my tone”, “learn to play in a key that’s difficult on my instrument”, etc.The answer ought not be “learn to play tune X”, with X being “that cool tune that the hotshot at the session showed me up with last night” or “that flavor-of-the-month tune that everyone else is playing these days because Solas recorded it”. Maybe at this stage of my learning I’m not ready for tune X, and spending time on it, followed by tune Y (next week’s flavor of the month - and yes, the flavor of the month often changes weekly or even daily), and then tune Z, ad infinitum, will actually slow down or halt my progress. Whereas, if the first question (“What do I do next?”) is answered correctly, then of course the next question very correctly should be “And what tune can I learn that will help me do that?”

Of course I know that I’m preaching to the choir with you on all of this…
