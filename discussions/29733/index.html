Re: What are the best ways to learn, as a group of players, how to avoid speeding up?

Record yourselves. Listen for the passages that seem to encourage the speeding-up. It could be fast runs of short notes, for example. Practise the bits causing the issue. Once a couple of you are more confident in time-keeping, the others should focus their listening on those people. Your bodhran player is almost certainly no better than anyone else in keeping time. It’s a myth that ownership of a drum suddenly bestows a wonderful sense of rhythm and tempo on someone. Actually, sack him or her. They’ll merely impede your ability to listen to the ones playing the music. In a very limited way, a metronome might help you to identify the rogue passages that are making you speed up, but don’t think that playing with a metronome will help. Actually, you shouldn’t even need it at all - you should be able to hear where the problems are in your recording. If you can’t, there isn’t a lot of hope! If you think your bike-riding is getting a bit wobbly, you don’t suddenly put your stabilisers back on. You identify the precise issue and work on it. Metronomes are incredibly blunt instruments that do not understand tempo and rhythm in anything like a human way.

You do occasionally get people who think they know the “correct” tempo for a tune and, if someone else starts it too slow for them, they deliberately try to accelerate. If you have anyone like that, give ’em a bloody good talking to.

Re: What are the best ways to learn, as a group of players, how to avoid speeding up?

Certainly don’t use the bodhran as a metronome, or use a metronome as a group. Unlike rock and many other genres, where you start with a rhythm, add a chord progression, and then add some sort of melody on top, this music works the other way round - you start with the melody, and all the backing follows (or should follow) the lead player.

So as Mix says, within your group you need to learn two skills - playing alone at a steady speed so that you can lead the group (you could use a metronome at home, alone, for this), and listening and following, so that when you are not leading, you don’t pull the tempo.

↳ Posted by Dow on April 15th, 2012.

Re: What are the best ways to learn, as a group of players, how to avoid speeding up?

Ditto what Doctor Dow just said.
The problem is that just one player speeding up can drag along others if the others aren’t paying attention to their own well-calibrated inner pulses. So each player has to feel the pulse and feel its steadiness.

You can’t keep a steady beat unless you already well feel the beat.

Being able to keep a steady beat relies on two things: feeling the full weight--the heaviness--of the beat, and giving the beat its full due time. Players who speed up typically don’t have much (or any) emphasis in their playing--all the notes are the same weight and length. Then they start leaving notes too soon, hurrying on to the next, and you’re off to the races.

So it helps to show such players how to feel the weight and duration of the beats. Walk them through half a tune and make them play the beats louder, fuller, and longer than they need to be. Describe the heaviness to them in terms they can feel in their body--the weight of the beat pulls your foot down to the floor, your head nods down on the beat, your diaphragm pulses to boost the air column, your pick drops through the strings, etc. You can *feel* the pulse when you fully express that weight with the hand that controls the pick, bow, bag, bellows, etc., or the diaphragm on wind instruments. If someone has trouble with this, ask them to exaggerate it, throw everything else out the window and focus just on pounding out the beat, feeling it in every muscle and in your gut. It’s easier to lighten up a bit then, and play with pulse.

For most people, a metronome is just another distraction. The best way to infect someone with pulse is to sit them down with another player who has a strong pulse and then overemphasize it till they feel it. That way, the learner hears the pulse itself, and the timing of the notes between the beats, which should sound effortless and relaxed, and lively. (I’ve yet to hear a lively metronome.)

Re: What are the best ways to learn, as a group of players, how to avoid speeding up?

1) Start all sets a little slower than required
2) Have a leader who can keep time, and also give some indication of the beat - preferably who can also speak (swear) and play.
3) WATCH the leaders fingers/bow/knee etc - whatever they use to beat time. (playing isn’t just a listening activity)
4) LISTEN. If you can’t hear the leader, you are too loud.

I have noticed two points -
1) in the sessions I attend, I can deal with the goat-bashers and guitars, but the melodionists/pa’s usually seem to be the culprits
2) people who speed up, often don’t notice if you tell them whilst they are playing - i.e. they either don’t listen or can’t listen AND play at the same time

Re: What are the best ways to learn, as a group of players, how to avoid speeding up?

Do it! But do it intentionally and with control. It will draw attention to who you have as a leader. By this I mean something the old ceili bands and some dance musicians used to d o for a lark, to tease dancers. Set out to change the tempo with every repeat of say a shared and well known 32 bar tune. Start out relaxed and steady, like at a nice trot, then when the repeat comes, boost it up tempo and play it once through faster, and you could do that again for a third time. Next, drop back down to something more even and relaxed, and lastly, shoot for a decent but unrushed dance tempo. Have fun with it, and take the mick out of each other. Don’t take it too seriously, but do learn to master that steady beat.

For dancers, the worst thing is a varying tempo. It will quickly tire them out as they try to figure out where exactly your beat is. That’s wear being together with it and taking it steady helps the dancers and the dance. I’ve seen a poor ceili band, half way through the night, unable to get anyone up on the floor to dance. Losing control of the rhythm and being out of synch with each other makes for a lousy dance experience. We’ve been to such events and were happier with our pints than getting up to dance. Worse, in the case that just popped into mind, they did the worst possible thing to try to raise interest, they raised their volume. Eventually, the ears tired to, we left…

Re: What are the best ways to learn, as a group of players, how to avoid speeding up?

I don’t know that I agree with Llig’s advice. If it’s group interaction that’s causing the pace to go up, then you need to work together to resolve the issue.

Does the group speed up in practice and in sessions, or just at gigs?

If you don’t have at least one rhythm ace who can keep a lively, steady groove going at any pace, then putting yourselves in the spotlight is just asking for trouble and disappointment.

Does one particular person tend to lead the charge, or do several start it?

If you’re having such problems, set time for more rehearsals to work on the specifics. At least in the beginning, it may help to settle on a specific tempo for each set. Nothing wrong with, say, starting out with an easy lope through a few simple tunes to let the band settle in. You don’t have to “wow” the audience with speed and flash right at the start. In fact, in most cases, you’ll get the audience on your side if you start with something comfortable and easy that lets you play to the best of your ability. Save the dazzle (if you have any) for later when the band is warmed up.

It was not only the Fonz that said,‘Sit on it!’.

‘Groove’ is an empathic pulse that musicians feel for a particular piece.
You don’t have to be in the rhythm section (or a backer) to sit on it. You can be playing the tune with your head and fingers but feel the groove and sit on it in your heart or body.
And really try feeling the beat in half time or even quarter time. Take a reel, typically in eight quavers to the bar. Don’t try counting 123456781234567812345678!
You’ll go out of time! Feel it as 1.3.5.7.1.3.5.7 (i.e. crochets) or even 1…5….1….5…. (i.e. minims)
Then go to semibreves : 1…….1…….1…….1……. (now you’re sitting on it).
(Blimey! I hope this makes sense!)
Feeling the groove in half time will make you play in a cooler and more lyrical way, I assure you!

“Anacruses. Or rather phrases that start an eighth note or two before a downbeat. Some of the more clued-up printed editions of Scottish music notate the upbeats that way - a longer upbeat at the start of the tune than at the start of the second part. Makes it clearer to the dancers what the structure is - that tiny shift tells them they’re still in the same tune and hence the same structural unit of the dance.

“As with a lot of terms and phrases borrowed from standard western music and standard western music notation, the term ”anacrusis“ doesn’t really fit with diddley music. What it suggests is that there are a few notes which precede the tune. But really, it’s just a tune that starts a little bit before what’s normally (and again only borrowed from standard western music notation) referred to as the 1st bar.”

Patronizing crap. Look at any decent text on music theory (I just looked in Prout) and you’ll see they get it right. Anacruses occur in many musical genres, and it’s a lot *more* obvious that they are a part of the theme rather than something preceding it when the theme in question is something as minimal as a three-note motif (as in one of Prout’s examples, taken from a Haydn minuet). Diddley doesn’t have those, and doesn’t often have contrapuntal textures where multiple themes are sounding at the same time with their anacruses falling in different places, which occurs in art music as far back as the late Middle Ages.

There is a piece by Henry Cowell for recorder trio which is basically an Irish style jig (and named as such). Melodically it’s fairly idiomatic, except that each voice has the tune with a different length of anacrusis. It’s disconcerting to play such a traditional-sounding tune that way if you’re used to actual traditional music, where everybody agrees how long the anacrusis is. Cowell wasn’t getting the concept of anacrusis wrong - he had a more general conception which includes the traditional musician’s as a special case.

Re: What are the best ways to learn, as a group of players, how to avoid speeding up?

If you have to ask it’s nothing to do with the group of players’ tempo & everything to do with prior learning.
Take your time when getting to know new tunes. Encourage your fellow players to practise with patience as well. It’s good to hear the entire tune as it’s played up to speed, so you can get it into your head.
But getting it onto your instrument depends on the player, who might need a bit of time in practice.
Listen, practise, listen to the tune slowed down, listen again (fast & slow), practise, play, session, wreck the train, start over, improve, learn ~ keep at it. Always remember to get better as you go.
