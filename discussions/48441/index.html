Added by hatao on July 22nd, 2023

Can Irish dance music be art?

Have you ever had the experience of being moved by a performance of Irish dance music? I am sure that many of you have had such an experience. What was the reason for your emotion?

I also have had such an experience. For example, I have shed my tears when I heard the single jigs played by the fiddles on the track “A Set of Single Jigs Learned from Kevins Father - John’s Grandfather” from the album “Music at the Matt Molloy’s” (1993), because of the beautiful and gentle melody and the soft sound of the fiddles, and I was also moved to tears when I listened to the tin whistle player Cormac Breatnach’s performance of a set of reels “Through The Fields - The Man Of The House - The Shores Of Lough Reagh” on his album “For Whistle And Guitar”, I am moved by the dramatic emotions and beautiful landscapes that the music conveys.

There are melodies that sound very sad and others that evoke no emotion at all. But here I am talking about the performance, not the melody itself. Dance music does not have lyrics, and the title has nothing to do with the music, unlike the program music of Tchaikovsky’s “Swan Lake”, However, when you feel the emotions that the performer wants to convey through the music beyond the brilliance of the rhythm, I consider it a remarkable performance. Is this way of appreciating emotions in dance music “traditional”? Can dance music be an art form that expresses something like classical music?

I believe that dance music is originally intended to provide rhythm to the dancers, so there is no emotion or scenery contained in the music itself. Especially unlike classical music, tune of “gan ainm” - the composer is unknown, it is difficult to believe the tune has any specific personal intent.

Classical music also includes music with folk dance motifs and sublimated folk rhythms. From Baroque works such as Bach’s Gavotte and Gigue, which are more connected to dance and music, to Chopin’s Mazurka of the modern era, dance music still has a large presence in the classical music. As time went by, dances music became more and more about the composer’s intention than just providing rhythm to the dancers.

On the other hand, although modern Irish music has come to be performed and listened independently as music, the first requirement for a good performance is still to be “danceable”. The use of Agogik or Dynamik is considered overproduction and non-traditional, and is subject to criticism.

When referring to the “art” in the context of Irish dance music, for example, there is a video “The Art of Uilleann Piping” produced by Na Píobairí Uilleann, I think it refers to the traditional style of playing the pipes and technical refinements such as ornaments and variations.

So, was the emotion and scenery I felt in the tracks I just described a one-sided assumption on my part?
I don’t think so! Some performers are certainly attempting to “express something” with traditional music.

For example, Michael Coleman, an early 20th century fiddle player, is, needless to say, a wonderful performer. It would be very pleasant to dance to his fiddle. However, I can’t feel anything more than up-lifting beats and technical perfection in his playing. On the other hand, the fiddle playing of Tommy Potts and Martin Hayes, even when playing dance tunes, their music is rich in shading, tonal variety, and full of nuance, thus the music satisfies the listener’s musical needs. As for flute playing, Conal O’Grada’s and Harry Bradley’s performances have exciting locomotive-like vertical beats that makes you want to move your body, while Matt Molloy’s playing has a push-and-pull quality in large horizontal phrases, I feel the beauty in his playing.

I am not saying that the more musically expressive the performance is, the better. Dance music is just dance music, and if it is music has great rhythm, that is good music. These differences are differences in playing style and what the performer is looking for “beyond being dance music”.

When I took lessons with Canadian flutist Chris Norman, I received ideas on how to observe the inherent characteristics of the melody: phrasing, arpeggios and scales, and chord progressions. I find this approach is more like the approach of Baroque music, but it makes sense since modern Irish music has its roots in pre-19th century music and has some things in common with Baroque music. In fact, his lessons have changed my playing as I have gained a deeper understanding of the nature of each tune.

If you have read this far, you already know that I am not talking about the superficial aspects of arranging music to make the listener feel something, such as what chords or rhythms to apply to a melody or what combinations of instruments to use in an arrangement. What I am asking is whether it is possible to satisfy the audience by a solo melody performance.

As a teacher of Irish music myself, I encourage my students to consider “how the melody want to be played” by analyzing the direction of phrases, chord progressions, and accentuated notes within the rhythm. This understanding allows them to deliver a more profound interpretation of the music. Even in dance tunes with steady beats, subtle differences in dynamics and a sense of swing can bring the performance to life. On the other hand, if they approach the performance with preconceived notions like, “This is how a reel should be played,” then even if they can play a thousand reels, each piece will give the listeners a mechanical and the same impression.

The key lies in understanding the nuances and emotions embedded in each melody and using that understanding to infuse life into the performance. This approach allows the students to truly connect with the music and share their interpretation with the audience, making their performances more engaging.

What I would like you to consider is whether or not it is traditional to express something through Irish dance music performance, and what the virtuoso Irish performers are trying to express. I believe that it is possible to make dance music more than dance music. I am also satisfied as a lover of Irish music when I see such performances, and I hope to be such a performer myself.

Finally, please share your experience of a performance that has moved you, and why it has moved you.
I would also be happy to hear your thoughts about how people who play instruments think about this kind of thing when they play music.

Re: Can Irish dance music be art?

“I believe that it is possible to make dance music more than dance music.”

There is a question here about whether it can be “more” without alteration to the point where it’s no longer foot-tapping dance music.

For example, when there is a beautiful melody embedded within music originally intended for dancing, it can be “extracted” by slowing the tempo, which allows more expression in phrasing. Recording artists will often slow down a reel to milk the expression (Martin Hayes, etc.), or flute players will slow it down and play on a low Bb flute for expression.

Of course it’s no longer anything you could call dance music at this point, except as an origin point. No urge to tap one’s foot, just appreciating a beautiful melody line.

Re: Can Irish dance music be art?

Hatao, I latched onto the sentence, “I believe that dance music is originally intended to provide rhythm to the dancers, so there is no emotion or scenery contained in the music itself.”

“The music itself” here seems to mean the written notes in a basic setting in a book or website, not the music produced by a player of an instrument. The music itself is not on a page! And you seem to come to a similar understanding, as you went on to describe teaching students to interpret a tune and bring life to it.

This brings out where I feel the art is in traditional dance music. Except in the most highly arranged compositions, any emotion is not notated, and the performer makes so many artistic decisions. A composer in a genre like this presents a framework that might imply a particular set of emotions and cerebral responses, but the interpreter, the performer, really creates the experience for a listener/dancer to engage with. So there are at least two “artists” involved -- a composer and a performer -- and in a sense, the “tradition” of tune transmission and reinterpretation, with all the minds and hands between, become a sort of “virtual artist” in the space between them. Maybe that’s getting a little fanciful. :)

On the subject of dance, I think nothing prevents a dance music from being an “art.” It involves imagination to compose, human interpretation to produce, technical skill to master, and someone listening to feel and interpret into thought, emotion, and movement, after all! The feeling that you just have to dance is one of the best responses to art I can imagine.

As for a performance video -- how about a couple of polkas from Cormac Begley and Stephanie Keane here? https://youtu.be/1B1yAluwgG0

The written melodies of polkas are barely anything: they might consist of five notes, barely more than a nursery rhyme. The notes could imply a major or minor character, and the progression between their keys could be a driver for forward movement in a dance. But the art really lies with the performer: making all the little decisions, swelling and pulsing dynamics, accenting, harmonizing, leaving space, even tapping a foot, to create a complete experience. And the dancer or listener takes that in and experiences another mental state, be it wild abandon, contentment, euphoria, or on the other side sadness, discomfort, or agitation.

Re: Can Irish dance music be art?

If we talk about the art of music, we must define what art is.
My definition is that it is an experience that evokes or inspires some kind of emotion in the listener. This is not something that is common to all people, so it is possible to be exposed to something that is considered highly artistic but not feel anything. Therefore, art is relative.
Since we are talking here only about Irish dance music, we consider a performance of a dance piece to be artistic when it “evokes or inspires some emotion in the listener,” more than just providing rhythm.

As for “dance music is originally intended to provide rhythm to the dancers,” I actually have a follow-up to this.

If dance music is simply to provide rhythm, then why are there so many songs and why do we musicians need to learn as many of them as possible?

It is because each tune has a different character, a different rhythm, a different chordal progression, a different flavor, and a different pleasure in playing. This is not the same as a performer describing emotions through music, but I think it is quite natural for the listener to feel some kind of emotion toward a performance that captures the characters of the tunes they are playing.

In Irish music, there are some pieces that are nothing but scales and arpeggios, and such pieces seem mechanical and inorganic. However, if the hand of virtuosic player, even such a piece can be perceived as a wonderful melody.

In other words, whether what originally provides rhythm can remain just that, or whether it can go beyond that, depends on the performer’s approach to music and performance technique.

“In other words, whether what originally provides rhythm can remain just that or whether it can go beyond that, depends on the performer’s approach to music and performance technique.”

You still seem to believe that the primary purpose of dance music is to provide rhythm, but that’s only part of it. It’s purpose is to inspire people to dance. That means choosing a tune which the dancers will respond to emotionally as well as physically. Of course any music depends on the performer’s ability to interpret it. Even the greatest music will fall flat in the hands of a poor player.

Anyone who plays in a dance band will tell you how important it is to select the right tune for a particular dance. Anyone who dances will tell you what a difference a good band makes. A good one will pick the right tunes and perform them in a way which gives lift and energy to the dancers. A poor band may keep the rhythm going but doesn’t deliver the energy the dancers need or inspire them to take the floor in the first place.

Sessions may not play to actual dancers, but the music should make bystanders want to move to it. For the musicians, the choice of the tunes to play will largely depend on their emotional response to it.

It would not be a stretch of the imagination to visualize some of the group also joining in the occasion with some sort of rhythmic dance. If it did not illicit emotion and enjoyment, why would they have bothered to do it?

As @sfmans pointed out there has been a long line of elitists that look down on artists that choose folk/traditional music as their medium, but it is the musical art of the people. When interacting with others that research some aspect of anthropology or artistic history, I occasionally run into individuals that roll their eyes when I tell them I research folklore and folk music. That segways into conversation that art is not just for the museum or the stage but should be enjoyed every day by everyone.

One last thing to consider, each performance of an Irish dance tune is slightly different even when performed by the same musician. I know my mood and feelings affect my rhythmic swing and which notes are accented. Each rendition is danceable (well mostly) but could illicit different emotions from instance to instance.

Art is in the eye or ear of the beholder.

Originally, maybe people just jumped around hooting when they felt euphoric, who knows, but eventually somehow a rhythm formed. If you look at tribal story telling, say in Africa, you will find rhythm where animals are described trotting, for example, and you will find rhythm in polyphonic playing so that various instruments are able to play in sequence together, as a sort of game for fun maybe, to test synchronization and ability, ultimately to create a richer or more splendid or deeper form of music.

So there is room for different styles to interact, history shows how different styles are sometimes adopted and adapted into local forms, but to allow a foreign expression to overtake a particular form is not respectful, especially if it displaces subtleties that already exist in that music, and no matter if those subtleties are not understood or appreciated by those outside.

Equally, to rely only on form, to emphasize only form, is to ask for expression, and from anywhere.

So there is always a kind of balancing going on between all of these factors, and acceptance or rejection will vary. People tend to like the music that is in touch with where they themselves are coming from, with their own kind of sentiment. Even those who appreciate all different forms and styles would not have them mixed into just one, it would be pointless and a great loss.

I was still thinking about this yesterday.

Music, too, has been increasing its use of expression, such as dynamic expressions and agogigues.

Re: Can Irish dance music be art?

I’m not convinced that playing music is ever an art, I see it more as a craft. Composition is certainly an art: a composer creates a piece of music to express an idea or feeling. But players, no matter how much nuance and personal emotion they add, are just making copies of that original artwork, and that fits the definition of a craft, not art.

If a designer creates an iconic piece of furniture, that is art. If he then sends the design to a factory and has a dozen copies made those copies are still art - they still convey the designers original statement. But the people in the factory making the copies aren’t artists, they are craftsmen.

Writing about music is like talking about silence, but it comes out of silence and so counts as to do with it, musingly or figuratively. There seem to be two schools of thought, one is that a music can be composed and played a certain way to provide a certain effect, and the other is that the real feeling comes directly from the player at any moment.
