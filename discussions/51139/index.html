Re: 5 string banjo

The popularity of the tenor banjo in this music is pretty easy to explain: Volume + GDAE fingering + relatively cheap + extremely flat learning curve relative to most other instruments. It’s just a natural fit - unlike the 5-string. On the other hand, to make tunes work on a 5-string, you kind of have to reinvent the instrument, as most of what works in bluegrass and old-time is not going to fly in a session setting.

So you’ve got a lot of work cut out for you, and once you’ve done that work, what are you going to get for it? “Wow, you sound almost like you’re playing a tenor, that’s great!”

So, if you want to play banjo in this music, the tenor is just the obvious choice.

To answer the question in the previous post, yes, I’ve heard a few people play excellent session music on a 5-string banjo. It’s quite rare, of course, but it can work, if you’re bloody-minded enough to put in the work.

Re: 5 string banjo

The GDAE approach to banjo design can be traced back to 1882, in a US patent issued for what is now alternately referred to as mandolin banjo or a banjo mandolin. It was followed in 1885 by a closely related patent for a “banjolin.” By 1888, the latter inventor had reduced the instrument’s four double-strung courses to four single strings.

At some time between 1903 and 1907, Francis O’Neill recorded two wax cylinders including an instrument announced as a mandolin but sounding more like a banjo mandolin. In his book from 1913, “Irish Minstrels and Musicians,” (https://archive.org/details/isbn_9781167022364 p. 394) he refers to it as a “Connemara fiddle.” This also reflects the rationale behind the 1882 and 1885 patents, which were intended to make the banjo more readily accessible to a violin player.

On p. 271 in the same book, O’Neill describes a concert tour in Ireland in the late 1880s. One of the musicians on it was “Johnny Dunne, a capable vocalist and banjo player.” He appears in a sketch on the following page playing a 5-string banjo. This was unlikely to have been drawn from life, but whoever drew it apparently knew that it was the ordinary form of the instrument at that time.

There is no evidence of the tenor banjo when the O’Neill recordings were made, either, but it did begin to emerge shortly thereafter. The banjo mandolin remained in active use in céili bands into the late 1960s, and its players commonly also recorded on the tenor banjo.

There’s a lot more detail about that sequence of events in a series of three blog posts announced separately in another discussion here. They can be accessed directly at https://loopholes.blog/banjolin-articles/

Re: 5 string banjo

A couple of things…

First of all a 5 string banjo is a 4-string banjo with an extra string.
If you use a plectrum and never pluck the 5th string (typically the short string) then you
are playing a 4 string banjo.

Secondly, any banjo can be tuned in numerous ways.
If the question here is “How does a 5-string banjo tuned bluegrass style ‘G -D - G - B - D’ fit into an Irish session?”,
there are a number of answers for that, that have already been mentioned in this thread.
There are some highly accomplished finger pickers who have mastered this technique.

However, if the 5th high G string is ignored (never plucked), and the tuning is
D - G - B - E for the 4 other strings, then essentially you have
the bottom 4 strings of a standard tuned guitar -( E - A)- D - G - B - E.

So, if a guitar player can pick melodies in standard tuning then they can be played identically
on the banjo tuned this way - D - G - B - E.
This is a great option for standard tuning guitarists who never get to show off their melody skills
because plucked guitar melodies are typically not loud enough to be heard in a busy session.

That’s what I have done for years now on my favorite banjo.
(I do also play a “Tenor 4-string” tuned G - D - A - E so I have lots of chances to compare the
output.)

But truly my best sounding banjo is my 5-string.
I also love the bluegrassy fingerpicked sound I get out of this banjo, so it’s a great banjo for me.
But I don’t do bluegrass at Irish sessions though, so I just tune the high D string up 2 semitones to an E and Voila!! I have the bottom 4-strings of a guitar.

So, yes a 4 + 1 string banjo can play Irish melodies in a variety of ways.
Here is a clip that shows what I am talking about. The banjo is a 5-string banjo tuned as I have explained. There are other examples like this on my youtube channel.
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vsK-6GEpq0Y

The hammered dulcimer took its place alongside the fiddle as a mainstay instrument for traditional Irish dance during the 1720s, if not earlier. This was over a century before any type of banjo began peering over the threshold of that arena. The hammered dulcimer had receded to its last bastion in Antrim by about the time the tenor banjo was retuned from the American CGDA to the Irish GDAE, yet another century further on.

It is certainly worth wondering why it fell out of favor — particularly if the banjo might have been a factor — and what it might take to reinstate it. But how does the notion of conundrum weigh into this? Historical deets about its early days are online at https://loopholes.blog/dulcimer-pantalon/#Ireland

Re: 5 string banjo

In the archives of the online Banjo Hangout you’ll find exhaustive discussions of whether 5-string is appropriate for ITM. I won’t repeat all the points here. But, for the record, I’m a pretty good clawhammer player, but I also play STM fiddle and Scottish bellows pipes. I used to play ITM fiddle, but decided to concentrate on STM. I would never have brought my open-back 5-string to a higher-end traditional ITM.

There are practical limitations. For example, trad tunes work best on 5-string in various appropriate tunings. For 5-string to begin to work, you would have to deal with instant key changes within the same set. That would involve re-tuning on the fly, which would be annoying and uncool in a higher-level ITM session. Or a very quick capo change, which can be a bit rough and often involves tweaking the tuning. Years ago, when I played a few contra dances, I purchased a second banjo for the purpose of smooth key changes.

Another practical limitation is that, even assuming you’ve solved the flying key changes, and even assuming you are smoothly playing all the right notes, the 5-string still somehow doesn’t quite fit. It lacks the bite and distinctive ornamentation of a GDAE 4-string. Yes, many folks point to Tom Hanway as an example of someone playing ITM on 5-string. There are others, e.g. Noah Cline, who are very fine players. But that doesn’t mean that you are producing a sound that is viewed by many as being authentic to ITM.

Save yourself a great deal of trouble and purchase a GDAE 4-string. It will feel right, and sound right.
And if you still want to play the occasional ITM tune on the 5-string, then go for it. Just understand that, in the context of sessions, there are hurdles.

Here’s a fantastic video of clawhammer wizard Allison DeGroot playing Tom Billy’s on her open-back.
It doesn’t get any better. If that is what you want, it can be done. But beware of the pitfalls.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E-uuczw46HQ

There are a lot of interesting comments here. I have a thought that is probably not the whole reason but is maybe a little bit of the reason. I agree with the comments that say there is an historical aspect.

The banjo is an African instrument and came to the North American colonies with kidnapped, enslaved people, in that they were able to find materials nearby to their captivity and reconstruct this instrument from their homeland. American style 5-string banjo playing is closely related to the way African people played it and other similar plucked string instruments. Based on what I have read from people’s comments in this thread, the instrument was transported to Ireland MUCH later. Irish musicians who liked the sound (who wouldn’t?) and decided to incorporate it into their music eventually made modifications to make the instrument more similar to instruments in their own tradition and thus easier to use -- getting rid of the droning 5th string, for one, and then using a more familiar tuning. They also use the instrument very differently, playing the same melodies as the fiddlers, etc. on their 4-string banjo. There is probably more to it, but based on the way transcultural transformation usually works, I think this was part of it.

Re: 5 string banjo

Jessica, that’s an interesting story, but there are a few holes in it. The tenor banjo was developed in the late 19th or early 20th century in the US, and was a very popular chordal instrument for jazz bands - the guitar would have been pretty inaudible in a jazz setting until amplification was available - and it was tuned in fifths from the start. So the Irish didn’t need to remove the fifth string or devise the tuning, the tenor came that way from the start.

However, this leaves open the question of when and how it became a melody instrument in the music. At the time we’re talking about, the tenor banjo would have been purely used for accompaniment in the jazz setting, so it’s not very wild to suggest that that’s the role it originally played in Irish music as well - possibly brought in for a recording session or possibly as a member of a dance band, in either case basically there to make the music sound less Irish and more familiar to American listeners, same as the piano and guitar. But who first treated it as a melody instrument, and when, or even whether this happened in the US or in Ireland, on that I have no idea.

It’s also interesting to wonder when and how the tenor banjo got back to Ireland, and whether this was before or after it became a melody instrument. I want to suspect that the first World War would have played a major role in this, but I haven’t yet convinced myself that this is a sensible proposition. If anyone has any speculations (or even facts) about this, I’d love to hear about them.
