Re: Rehearsed variations and ornamentation.

Tab, I share your enthusiasm for letting the tune unfold on the fly, without more than a millisecond forethought as to how you’ll play the next phrase. But in a formal performance or in the recording studio, even the best musicians, including the ones you mentioned, are likely to stick to a sequence of variations that they’ve thought out ahead of time. Martin Hayes can be as inventive and spontaneous as the best of them, but he also pre-planned every twiddly bit for his studio albums. And players tend to gravitate toward their own sense of a tune. Over years, that will evolve, but four different takes of the same tune over a shorter time span (a few months, say) will likely reveal clear repeated phrasings.

The best fun is when you play something new straight out of your head, without any prep work at all. Sessions are good for this because you’re likely to be influenced by and respond to other people’s musical ideas, plus a pint or two loosens up the inner muse. Sessions also tend to be informal enough that you can shuffle through the occasional stumble and try the idea again the next time ’round.

Don’t make it happen, just *let* it happen. Mostly a matter of coming to the music with a different mindset: instead of playing a predetermined sequence of notes, think of the whole phrase and then let it come out under your fingers.

All these years into playing fiddle, I’ve come to realize that we play the music in our minds, not on the instrument. Yes, you have to be fluent to a degree on your instrument, but all the improvising happens in your head.

Re: Rehearsed variations and ornamentation.

@big tab: i would not say it goes against my theory. i said there are “variation-targeted beats.” this could very well imply that the player has no “standard” way to play that particular beat, but rather a stack of potential ways, and changes it every time. this does not mean it is fully planned, or spontaneous.

a few weeks ago i was recording silver spear for a student on the whistle. i was having a lot of trouble, because i play a version that is a combination of several non-standard versions (brian rooneys, my uncle’s, with my own variations thrown in). i had a lot of trouble playing the “standard” tune, even though i had originally learned it the “standard” way.

this does not refute my theory either . i have set places where i vary the tune… in the FAA2 “beat,” i do it one of several ways (in rough order of frequency).

1. F3A
2.(roll)F3 A
3. FAA2
4. FA(roll)A2
etc.

now, that’s not too special, but there are about 12 beats in the first half of the tune that i “never play the same way twice.” but, in actuality, i DO play them the same way twice, but if you do the math of all the possible variations i can put in every spot, it is very unlikely you will ever hear me play the entire TUNE the same way twice. this does not mean that i am pre-planned, nor that i am being completely spontaneous.

i have heard great players play the same tune on more than one occasion, and they do not play each tune the same way, and are always playing with many variations. i would have difficulty believing that players like maurice lennon, noel hill, etc. etc. have NEVER played a particular “variation-targeted beat” the same way before, just i have doubts that the great musician you mention really was miraculously discovering each beat as he played it.

i do not contend that it is all pre-planned, but rather that great players have a pre-populated stack of possible variations to play, and that they are in pre-determined parts of a tune. the better a player, the more variation-targeted beats per tune (some players may have entire tunes composed of targeted beats). this does not mean that there is necessarily a “standard” way to play a variation-targeted beat for every player. for example, i do not know what the “standard” way to play the first phrase of the kesh jig, nor do i know what i would consider my default way of playing the first phrase--i don’t have one. i guess you could consider such beats as missing the 0 place in their variation stack. that does not in itself refute my theory….

Re: Rehearsed variations and ornamentation.

Tab, you may be right, but I suspect you’ve made the process too mythical. Sure, it’s not a mundane matter of, “Oh, this time I’ll do a roll there, next time a melodic variation.” But the best players have thousands and thousands of hours of playing in their hands, and the basic bits that make up variations--really *how to play the tune many different ways*--are as much inside them, a part of them, as the tunes are. So it may sound and seem like reflex to you, but it’s all familiar stuff to the player, and they’re “thinking the tune” as they play. Not verbally, not in words, just thinking the melody as it unfurls.

What makes this not just a reflexive action is that ***you rarely if ever think sounds that are outside the traditional idiom.*** This is what Draguit was on about above. The variations in this music come from playing the predictable bits (tiddly bits, and rhythmic and melodic variations) in unpredictable ways. Yes, you can surprise yourself. And you can certainly surprise others. But it’s not out of playing something so startlingly odd or far afield. The “new” sound has to come from bits that belong in the tradition. Which you’ve played a million times before.

Playing by the seat of your pants is also something you “practice” by doing. I tend to play this way most of the time, certainly in sessions. Over the many years, it’s gotten easier.

(FWIW, I’m thinking out loud here. But it’s based on my own frequent experience of surprise in my own playing and also from talking with other musicians about this very notion.)

Re: Rehearsed variations and ornamentation.

Ornamentation is a widely used term in music. Typically it refers to adding notes (to the melody) to embellish certain types of music. Irish ornamentation, or as I like to say ~ articulation(s) aren’t necessarily pitched notes but are essentially rhythmic. In other words, when you listen to ornamentation in Baroque music you can clearly identify the individual notes. When you hear a roll or crann on fiddle, or pipes, you’re not so much hearing distinct pitches even though the player appears to be fingering them. Articulations give tunes their characteristic rhythm & lift.

Ya know, reading through all this all I can think of is this:

There is no best way to teach or learn music. However there are wrong ways, that can either hinder or hurt a student’s ability.

A player will learn and use the tools of different ornamentations as soon as they are taught and skilled with them. Being able to use them effectively and purposfully is just a matter practice, experience, knowledge, and time. I think it takes a lot of time and effort to do this well. It is a matter of building up the basic skills needed then adding on from there.

As a fiddler I somewhat need to learn a tune as bare bones as possible before adding in the twiddly bits. Especially since figuring out the bowings, phrasing, and rhythym take a bit of work. A lot of times there are countless variation even in those! Sometimes after a few rounds of playing a new tune bare bones a few simple and obvious ornaments start happening almost naturally without trying. The more confortable and intimate I get with a tune, the easier it is for the tune to speak to me and bring out little nooks and crannys (heheh) that allow for new and unique (to me at least) ways to play.

Anyhow, back to the discussion. I think any of the greats have done most of what they’ve done a hundred times before at least if not in the thousands of times, even if they don’t do it all quite in the same order or styling every time through a tune. Really, I don’t really care as long as it sounds great and fun and inspirational.
