Recommended book: The Talent Code

A fellow thesession.org detainee put me onto this inspiring book The Talent Code
by Daniel Coyle http://thetalentcode.com/. It links up recent research in
neuroscience about skill acquisition with some tales from the field. It’s about
how to get extremely good at whatever you want to do and how to practice without
wasting your time.

Slowing down is only part of it. Coyle tells us you have to combine that with
intense concentration on faults and failures, fanatical motivation over a long
period and clear understanding of what you’re trying to achieve, developed through
coaching. If you do all of this, you too will be world class. Few can put it all
together. For one thing you have to get lucky with your coach/teacher.

People on this site have written about aspects of this, but Coyle’s book explains
why it works, based on science and also backs it up with some field research that Coyle
carried out. The book’s examples are mostly from music and competitive sport.

Re: Recommended book: The Talent Code

I’ve always thought that the best argument for a genetic component of musical ability isn’t by looking at the gifted, but at the other end of the spectrum; the musically impaired.

Leaving aside actual neural disorders and disease that can cause severe impairment, I think most of us have run across people who “can’t carry a tune in a bucket,” or have parallel problems with rhythm timing. Some of these folks work hard, for years, trying to overcome what seems to be just a wiring problem; a reduced function in the brain that isn’t a problem for most of us who take up music.

If there is impairment for a very limited number of people at one end of the spectrum, and most of us muddle along and do well with hard work, then I think that argues for a bell curve-type distribution of innate musical ability. The “naturally gifted” are at the far end of the curve, in very small numbers like the impaired at the other end of the curve.

If you’re not buying this argument and think there is no such thing as genetically-based talent, that it’s all just hard work, then you have to find a way to explain the musically impaired as well as the geniuses.

Re: Recommended book: The Talent Code

AlBrown and some others above have it right. If you take two people who have, somehow, been matched as to all of the variables of physical and mental skills necessary to play a musical instrument (or do most anything else for that matter) the one that perseveres and works harder will be the more successful. But to not factor in those other things in making the generality is just plain silly. I really respect the idea that hard work will take one a long way. In more years of teaching than I can count I’ve seen many whose hard work propelled them beyond those who did not work hard but had more “talent” (whatever that means). I also saw that those who had talent or the right genes or whatever you want to call it could often work less hard and go farther.

As to Mozart’s youthful works: It has been said that as a 3 year old Mozart was entranced by playing 3rds on a keyboard instrument. That certainly speaks to a skill that was not gained by hard work. By 6 he was writing symphonies. Yes they are derivative. Throughout his life Mozart’s great skill was the ability to synthesize styles and techniques that were already in place. This in opposition to Haydn and Beethoven, both of whom were constantly breaking the mold and expanding the “structures” upon which they built their music, and both of whom worked hard at composition. When Mozart heard or looked at works that expanded things he seems to have absorbed whatever is new and added it to what he created…almost without effort. The story that he wrote the overture to Don Giovanni out essentially without stopping on the day of the premier may be apocryphal, but there are plenty of similar examples the indicate he did not struggle with composing. We’ve gone past the age where we can say that works simply flowed from him without effort, and we know that he did listen to and study scores of many others. We also know that to some degree he did not absorb some techniques completely. But despite all of that I know of no biographical information or remaining manuscripts that would show, as the Beethoven sketch books do and as some Haydn materials do, that he struggled and revised heavily in the process of composition. To put his efforts, or those of Schubert (who wrote out complete songs without pause often), or Mendelssohn (who was writing rather complete symphonic works by 18) down to “hard work” without admitting that perhaps there are other things at play is not at all reasonable.

Re: Recommended book: The Talent Code

On the one hand we have those people who assume that hard work is necessary but isn’t sufficient to achieve world-class accomplishment on an instrument. They say there’s just no accounting for the pure genius some people seem to have, or the total lack of “talent” that other people seem to have. These are things (“talent,” “innate ability,” “genius”) that they have “noticed,” or “seen.” Not measured. Not studied. And if we assert otherwise -- despite appearances -- then we are hiding our heads in the sand. These same people don’t tell us whether or not they have read the studies that would prove them wrong about such things. Yet they tell us that we are hiding from the facts.

On the other hand we have those who have spent years researching the issue. They have measured and studied, and have remeasured and restudied. They have published data attesting to their findings. Time and and time again they have found that, despite appearances (and, in sports, absent such genetic wild-cards as extreme height or weight) we all start out pretty much the same. And where we end up, no matter the age at which we start, we all need to put in the time and effort to get somewhere. In this case I’d rather believe the scientists than the people who unquestioningly accept the anecdotal evidence.

I offer this classic study as my final word on the issue. I can send the article to anyone who asks for it.
Psychological Review, 1993, Vol. 100. No. 3, 363-406
Copyright 1993 by the American Psychological Association, Inc.

The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance
K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Th. Krampe, and Clemens Tesch-Romer
"The theoretical framework presented in this article explains expert performance as the end result of
individuals’ prolonged efforts to improve performance while negotiating motivational and external
constraints. In most domains of expertise, individuals begin in their childhood a regimen of
effortful activities (deliberate practice) designed to optimize improvement. Individual differences,
even among elite performers, are closely related to assessed amounts of deliberate practice. Many
characteristics once believed to reflect innate talent are actually the result of intense practice
extended for a minimum of 10 years. Analysis of expert performance provides unique evidence on
the potential and limits of extreme environmental adaptation and learning."

Re: Recommended book: The Talent Code

I will stick with what I know, which is the exercise physiology and genetics. One parameter that is genetically determined and does not respond or responds very little to training is the ratio of fast to slow-twitch muscle fibres. Sprinters and weight-lifters have a very high ratio of fast to slow fibres in their muscles, and cross-country skiers and marathon runners have a very high ratio of slow to fast. The reality is that training (either for speed or for endurance) has no significant effect on that inborn ratio. Those in the middle can train all they want, but they aren’t going to turn themselves into sprinters or marathon runners.

Unsurprisingly, most people know pretty early if they can run fast or for a long time. That doesn’t contradict the argument that training can make you better than when you started, and better and more training is more effective than poor training. However, it’s simply wrong to claim that anyone can become a marathon runner or a sprinter if they train hard enough.

These data are 35 years old (and thus available to the authors of books making unsupportable claims in this millennium), but it’s very interesting to compare physiological parameters of athletes at different levels of competition. The two used were ratio of fast and slow twitch, and VO2 max. Basically, those with lots of slow twitch fibres have high VO2 max and vice versa. Up to the national level there can be winners who are in the top 10% of the population. This argues that differences in training can overcome differences in physiology to a very significant level. If one looks at Olympic finalists in sprinting events, long distance, shot-put and discuss, weight lifting, they are all in the top 1% of the population for slow to fast (or fast to slow) ratio. In other words, to be world class, no amount of training is any use unless your starting point is being in the top 1% of the population with respect to your slow/fast twitch ratio to start with. Thus at least for this particular endeavour, training does not compensate for genetics at the top level. It does partly compensate at the national level. Even so, all the national finalists are in the top 10%, so training isn’t sufficient for the bottom 90%. I am not persuaded that talent has nothing to do with it.

I have a friend who grew up in the USSR, and went to a national sports academy. He was picked as an 11 year old as having talent, and sent off. I asked him how they picked candidates. His answer was that they played various games, and they picked kids who hated to lose. Then they sent them off for general athletic training, and tried out different sports to check for aptitude. They started him on javelin, switched him to swimming, and he ended up doing tennis. He noted that lots of kids had to leave because they didn’t have the physical attributes. But this way of producing athletes worked pretty well. So did drugging them up. But it does suggest that there are mental attributes that are essential to high level performance, as well as physical ones.

I think where the discussion is most interesting and productive is on activities that require a complex suite of skills, and therefore genetic likelihood of being excellent at all of them is exceedingly low. HereI’d predict that training is likely to be the most important factor.

@David Levine: “Why would people want to believe something that is inherently limiting: the notion that innate talent gives some people a huge leg up, and that the lack thereof means you’ll only be able to go so far? ”

Because I prefer to see the world as it is, and not through rose-colored glasses. Humans got where we are today by selection pressure on genetic variations within the population, just like any other organism. Those variations didn’t magically disappear when we developed culture and technical advancement. These differences express themselves in all sorts of ways, including what we call being “naturally gifted” in the fields of athletics, mathematics, chess playing, you name it. Why would the ability to play music be different? Why should it be fenced off as a separate case, and suddenly we’re all equal in ability?

Academic studies that attempt to make that point by looking only at people who are already in music schools, is a deliberate distortion of the data. The studies only look at the median example and not the extremes, so naturally there is a flattening effect that emphasizes the role of hard work. Ever notice how a group music class is either self-selected or selected by the teacher so that everyone in the class is at about the same level? Think about what that does to “academic research” on that question.

Like I said before, variation in talent isn’t “nice” thing to talk about, so people don’t often discuss it. Music teachers rarely address it because it affects their income (although their private thoughts can be interesting, when you get them to talk about it). Personally, I think it’s something that should be acknowledged, not in a way to discourage people, but as a way to appreciate the diverse ways talent expresses itself across a wide range of music. Otherwise, it becomes: “Hendrix? Oh, you mean that guy who got famous just because he worked his butt off, harder than the rest.”

@David Levine: “I am motivated to work much harder towards my goal. I can accept my shortcomings as a result of my not-caring, and not focusing, more readily than thinking that I’ll never get anywhere because I lack something that other people are born with. ”

The realization that talent varies among musicians doesn’t have to be a block to personal progress. I think it’s an important step in one’s growth as a musician when you reach the point of understanding that you’ll never be Hendrix, or Molloy, or whoever your heroes are, but you can still strive to be your own unique version of a musician, wherever that ends up.

Maintaining that perspective isn’t easy, of course. It’s like when my S.O. and I go to a Kevin Burke concert, and she wants to go home and burn her fiddle. We’ve all been there, right?
