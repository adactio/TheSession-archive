Re: The Northern Fiddler

Many, many thanks are due to Fernandito and Braccio for making this amazing book available to those who didn’t manage to snap up a copy when it was still to be found.

This is a remarkable book and, for all the criticism it got from opponents of its scholarly ethnomusicological and anthropological approach, there is still nothing to equal it as a study of Donegal fiddle music and the culture of Donegal music. And I can’t think of any similarly impressive work relating to any other regional Irish musical tradition. It’s well known that Brendan Breathnach did not admire Donegal fiddle music, and he slated the book.

Kevin Rietmann says: “A lot of older musicians would throw in an extra bar or note for whatever reason.”

It’s said a lot that Donegal fiddlers did this frequently, but there seems only to be one instance of it in this book, i.e. John Doherty adding an extra beat in one bar of the jig The King of the Pipers (so replacing a 6/8 bar with one in 9/8), (as you can hear on the John Doherty Celebrated Recordings record) I don’t think this was ever very common thing in Donegal and all the fiddlers I’ve ever heard doing it got it from John Doherty. This wee quirk may come from the east Donegal Inishowen style which was influenced by European tunes where time-signatures can change between different parts of the tune. The Donegal accordionist Martin Tourish knows a lot about this and has studied these rarities in detail (and plays lots of them too.)

Opposition to the book from people like Brendan Breathnach meant that Eamon O’Doherty rejected the publisher’s plan to publish a second expanded edition of The Northern Fiddler, which is a tragedy.
Another cultural tragedy is the suppression of the first of the two records planned to accompany the book.
Topic Records 12TS 397 “Various – Pass the Time: An Anthology of Donegal Fiddling” (1979 never issued)
featuring Francie Byrne, Simon Doherty, Con Cassidy and Danny O’Donnell (I didn’t know James Byrne was also supposed to be on it, but I know they recorded him even though he doesn’t feature in the book.)
Danny O’Donnell was supposed to have stopped the record’s release because he was recorded unaccompanied and didn’t want it released that way, but I stand to be corrected on this. Even if this record stays suppressed I hear that the folks at Cairdeas na bhFidiléirí may release some of these recordings as as parts of different CDs of these fiddlers in coming years.
Thankfully the next record was released: Topic Records 12TS 398 “John Doherty – Bundle and Go” (1980)

Re: The Northern Fiddler

Its absolute rubbish to assert that Danny O’Donnell did not pull out of the second Topic Record "Pass the Time ( a homage to Henry Glassie’s book Passing the Time in Ballymenone). Danny insisted on recording his tunes in Dublin with a a piano backing. There was no budget for that and the recording budget was not under my control. The money promised by Topic to the musicians for which I was but the messenger (some of whom were paid despite the lack of publication) was acceptable to all of them at that time who agreed to appear on the lp. The contracts they signed were standard boilerplate recording contracts of Topic who was and is a trusted entity in the British folk scene. There was nothing exploitative about any of the arrangements. Why I went to Topic a British company? Claddagh, Mulligan and Gael-lin were not interested in the collection. Pass(ing) the Time was meant to feature both Donegal and Tyrone fiddlers. Once Danny pulled out Topic was reluctant to go ahead for they were more interested in the Donegal tradition than Tyrone, though Tyrone fiddlers like Peter Turbit and Sean McKeown were as original as any in Donegal. Topic also felt that some of the field recordings were not technically up to standard due to unavoidable ambient noise on them. Till this day that have the masters they made.

I was not aware that O‘ Donnell, who recorded many tunes for me ,pulled out of the Brass fiddle project, but that was indicative of his diva behavior. If ex post facto several other fiddlers complained about the Topic recording project or about the book itself that can be attributed to the well known cantakerous postures many Donegal fiddlers held at that time when it came to any public presentation of their music and personas and to the lies spread about me that I had made significant money from the entire endeavour and had cheated John Doherty who actually received 600 1980 British pounds for Bundle and Go. During the collecting of the music and the writing of the book I was subsisting on two Northern Ireland Arts Council Grants (shared with Eamonn) that paid for my per diem expenses and travel and nothing more and I had to return to the USA in 1980 broke and heartbroken after 6 wonderful years performing and collecting in Ireland ( I performed in Ireland with Scott Ainslie, Rick and Sandi Epping, and Bert Levy) Eamonn O’Doherty also made no money from the project and like me went into debt and often complained how other people published his photos and drawings from the project without attribution or royalties to him. The rumors I have been subjected to were due to the fact that some Irish nationalists in the trad scene of the time objected to a “Yank” “beating them to it.” It wasn’t as if I had discovered Donegal or Tyrone traditions“ but that after having the experience of watching folks like the late, lamented Blanton Owen recording Tommy Jarrell and Fred Cockerham in the field, I was sensitized to the fact than an entire generation of musicians in Donegal and Tyrone were on the cusp of disappearing without being recorded at all or sufficiently recorded. To this day I regret not having the resources to sit John Doherty down simply to record his stories alone. I was punished for this need to salvage the undervalued musical past and present by nationalist elements of the trad scene. If you think I am exaggerating my second book was an oral history of the ”Troubles“ in Belfast called ”Formations of Violence: The Narrative of the Body and Political Terror in Northern Ireland "which included chapters on torture and the Republican Dirt Protests and Hunger Strikes. The reception of Northern Fiddler in Ireland and the greater trad scene proved to be more controversial and politicized than the reception of the Belfast book; I received more flak and begrudgment for the first book on music than the second on civil war.

The rights to the Northern Fiddler book reverted to myself and ultimately to Eamonn O’Doherty’s estate (his widow Barbara) after we both refused to renew our contract with Blackstaff Press. Why did we refuse to renew with Blackstaff. That press did a major disservice to the trad musical community by selling the international rights (including Ireland, Europe and the USA) of the book to Oak Press (Music Sales International) who promptly let it go out of print or rot in its warehouses despite the ongoing demand for it. Blackstaff received a significant subsidy of thousands of pounds to publish the book from the Northern Ireland Arts Council. The subsidized publication was meant to be a public service to the people of Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland and Blackstaff wasted no time within a year of its publication to flog it to an impersonal corporation who couldn’t give a damn about its access to the trad community. (Blasckstaff also refused to let me include an annotated index to the transcriptions due to budget concerns) Some of those annotations of John Doherty’s music, with the assistance of Brendan Breathnach, appear on the back cover notes of Bundle and Go.) We did see some royalties in 1983 from that sale but that was poisoned by the disappearance of the book from public circulation in Ireland soon after.
A year after the opening of the Irish Traditional Music Archive Eamonn and I donated the recordings, photographs and drawings to the ITMA with the agreement that there would be total public access to the archive. That has always been our motivating ethos which is why as 50% rights holder of the book’s copyright I do not object to the pdf of the Northern Fiddler under discussion.

Re: The Northern Fiddler and the Topic Records Recordings

I can clarify the situation why the second Topic records of The Northern Fiddler Project was never released by Topic and put to rest the sheer lie and fake news that the fiddlers were not properly compensated and/or contracted for their recordings which has been spread to defame my reputation and that of Topic which is a venerable institution in the folk music scene. Topic would never publish recordings without a proper legal contract signed by the performer. John Doherty was paid 650 British pounds ( about $1514 USD in 1979-80) by Topic for “The Bundle and Go” album after signing a Topic contract.
The second album was projected to feature Con Cassidy, Danny O‘Donnell, Francie and Mickey Byrne and Simon Doherty of Donegal and Peter Turbit, John McKeown and Mickey Gallagher of Tyrone. I stress here that I made many cassette recordings of James Byrne’s superb playing with his consent, who I had visited every Christmas and summer between 1974 and 1980 (enjoying having his dear mother Cassie read my fortune in her cards). However, James refused to record or be interviewed for the Northern Fiddler Project ’s Uher tape deck. Therefore he does not appear at all in the book and was not slated for the Topic recordings meant to document field performances in the musicians homes for the most part. I always respected the fiddlers’ privacy and reluctance to go public for reasons discussed in the book. I take pride in the fact that as a consequence of the book’s publication in large part, James Byrne, Con Cassidy and Francie Byrne eventually enjoyed modest careers as public performers and recording artists during the 1980s. and received the recognition that was long overdue. I mourn the premature death of James Byrne who I considered to be the peer of Tommy Peoples.

Danny O Donnell refused to release his field tape recordings to Topic, for he insisted that he be provisioned with a professional recording studio and a backup piano player down in Dublin. He was rigid about this condition but Topic could not afford the expense for this and made the decision that without O’Donnell’s participation the record was not viable. They were also less enthusiastic about the Tyrone fiddlers for reasons I can only conjecture as their playing was excellent and their styles were more diverse than the southwest Donegal fiddlers-- possibly Tyrone did not have same market caché in the folk world. st that time. I note that the recent TG4 television documentary on the Northern Fiddler utterly erased the contribution of the Tyrone Fiddlers, a shameful act for which i was never consulted. Some cuts were considered marred by ambient background noise of cars, doors slamming children playing-- the cost of recording in the field and deemed unacceptable by Topic.
No musician I visited and recorded was cheated out of their compensation for the Topic record and no recording in the field was made without the express permission of the musician in question. Subsequently a kind of xenophobic and possibly antisemitic rumor mill was spread about me “The Yank” cheating the musicians and having made significant fiscal profit out of the project at their expense. This rumor and the “secret knoweldg”e some claim about this situation slandered myself and Topic Records. In fact, both Eamonn Doherty and myself went into debt to prepare the book and I went into further debt travelling between Dublin and London to work with Topic. I was forced to return to NYC financial reason in 1980 as the time I spent on the book and the album occurred at the expense of my performing career in Ireland and Europe as an Appalachian 5 string banjo player with various American musicians. My first year in NYC was spent busking in the subway system. That’s how rich I got.
As for tune names, as an ethnographer, my project was to document local knowledge and memory. As Francie Byrne repeatedly informed me when I asked him to name a tune “We were too busy learning the tunes to learn the names.” Other musicians used to joke on how John Simi Doherty made up names for tunes. The title Gan Anim reflected the musicians cultural memory and not a clerical error. Others experts who transferred names from other regional traditions to Donegal performances or from inaccurate skeletal transcriptions in archaic tune book collections were superimposing an “official name” on the local performer and regional tradition. Many critics operated on the culturally faulty notion that if a tune transcription originally appeared first in a published tome with a particular name attached to it, then that name becomes its official and definative title within the national oral and written archive of Irish music. However, the Northern Fiddler Project was about salvaaging a regional tradition before it disappeared and to forestall that disappearance.

Re: The Northern Fiddler

As a Scottish musician I’m particularly interested in the connections between Donegal and Scotland. Three miles up the road from me in Perthshire there is a village in which, over time, Donegalians settled. Whether working on the farm estates or labouring on the hydro-electric tunnels, some stayed, roots were formed, but connections to home were hard to break. These connections persist: many people in the area are proud of their Irish heritage, and annually the local bus company (Sweeneys) take a crowd of villagers over to Donegal.

Your work first alerted me to the kinship between the music of Donegal and Tyrone and my own Scottish music. It was intriguing to me to play the tunes of the Dohertys, the Byrnes et al, and recognise melodies I knew from different sources. Some of these tunes are very well known in Scotland and, I believe, pointing this out contributes to our knowledge of traditional music.

While I completely agree that if a source musician does not have a name for a tune that should certainly be documented, I don’t believe that further information about the tune and where it came from marginalises local knowledge. Knowing, for example, that “Untitled Strathspey” is a setting of Scott Skinner’s “Miller o’ Hirn” might not be hugely important, but I do think that that too should be documented.

I can’t believe I’m communicating with one of my heroes, and it’s actually a thrill to read your views on the music. I was sorry to hear of the troubles The Northern Fiddler suffered, and I hope you managed to get over it and returned to loving music. (Gushing over: sorry!)

Re: The Northern Fiddler

Thank you dor your comments. John Doherty was very influenced by the Scotts Skinner recordings and always credited his renditions of those tunes to him. But here I can trace the genealogy of his rendition to the Skinner recording based on his own testimony. My concern is the rather arbitrary assignment of a definitive “official” name that orignates outside of the musician’s local cultural frame of reference based on a relation to say a tune played by Clare musicians, a regional musical culture that had a seminal influence on the urban Irish traditional music scene in the 1960s and 70s. So some aficionados will say the Black Mare of Fanad is a variant of 9 Points of Roguery simply on the basis that the latter appeared first in a tune book or was recorded first or was popular in session scenes played by musicians unfamiliar with the Donegal repertoire. The assignment of a relation of derivation is what I find problematic-- 9 Points is the original tune and Fanad is a derivative of the latter. So I have nothing against relating and connecting variants of tunes.The concept of variant as a relation to a first and a second is embedded in the practice of naming tunes for which the player had no name or another name.

Re: The Northern Fiddler, the PDF copy and the myth of a law suit and legal ban.

I recently read with shock the following statement made 2 years ago on this site concerning the book and the pdf copy that is still circulating on-line. I quote from the missive to The Session members:
"Dear Sir/ Madam, this book was withdrawn from publication. My uncle and other musicians had to legally object in order to have it banned. The publication objections also apply to pdf and indeed all digital copies. Please amend your online content.
Damien O’Doherty"
To quote the noted cynic and satirist Myles na Gopaleen (Flann O’ Brien) “ the plain people of Ireland have spoken.” Note the ban applies to the book itself and by extension any reproductions. It is utter rubbish!
The Northern Fiddler was never withdrawn for publication due to legal action by any of the musicians in whole or part or their families! I have never been notified by any of the presses or law offices of such a law suit. The message to The Session site is “fake news”. Its highly unlikely that the surviving families of all the fiddlers depicted in the book from Donegal and Tyrone banded together in a class action suit.
In Teelin, the home of the late Con Cassidy, there is a pub owned by Dermot Byrne and Mairéad Ní Mhaonaigh that features Eamonn’s photographs on the wall over the bar in postions of pride and nostalgia. That hardly denotes hostility towards the book by relations and Teelin friends of Con Cassidy. There is a recent television tribute documentary on the Northern Fiddler Project made by TG4 that was broadcast on that channel and on BCC and no law suit was mentioned in that doc or blocked that program from being broadcast.
The current relative unavailability of the book has nothing to do with a lawsuit. Blackstaff Press sold the international publication rights to Oak publications and then Blackstaff let the book go out of print in Ireland and the UK. Oak publications was bought by Music Sales Inc who also let the book go out of print. Used or warehoused copies of the Blackstaff and Oak Press editions are still on sale today for outrageous prices on-line. None of these purveyors have been banned from trafficking in the book in such a public manner, many are well known legitimate traders in used books.
After a number of years passed the rights to the out of print book reverted back to myself and Eamonn O‘Doherty and when he prematurely died his right passed to his widow Barbara O’Doherty. (The author of the screed in question has no relation to Eamonn O‘ Doherty or to any of the musical Dohertys who as far as I know never used the prefix O’.
Eamonn, Barbara and myself have been advocates for free public access to the book and its materials. In 2000 I donated the original tape reels to the Irish Traditional Music Archives in Dublin and Eamonn donated his photographs and drawings to the same Archive. Recently, I published an invited essay in the Irish literary journal Field Day that revisited the project and also contained a eulogy of his art work. Field Day, a renowned Irish literary journal, obtained copies of Eamonn’s photos and lithographs from the ITMA, They were never legally sued or banned for reprinting these images or my use of extensive quotes from the book.
However this aggressive and hostile letter is indicative of the reception I received from some sectors in Ireland deploying rumor and mendacity. Donegal fiddling is a border crossing transnational maritime and ’traveller" musical tradition communicating with the highlands and islands of Alba and the bothy culture of Northeast Scotland. The Northern fiddler Project itself crossed many borders in being a collaboration between a New Yorker and a Derryman; in its multi media approach to documenation; in its stress on the authority of oral history and material culture and in its foregrounding and eulogizing of a regional tradition that had been previosuly been marginalized by the cultural turn to trad music in the mid late 2oth century. These were novel innovatons in Ireland at the time of its publication. Border crossers attract aggression and are inherently threatening to some.
My second published book was “Formations of Violence: The Narrative of th Body and Political Terror in Northern Ireland ”( U of Chicago Press 1991). I have received immensely more slag, aggro and stigma about the Northern Fiddler publication than the resctions that greeted my second book which was about such ‘uncontroversial’ topics as ethnic-sectarian violence, paramilitaries, doorstep killings, the Shankill Butchers, torture practices and prison hunger strikes to the death in Northern Ireland. In hindset the Northern Fiddler was a far more politcized book in its reception than Formations of Violence. Go figure.
