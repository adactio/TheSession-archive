Traditionality

There are lots of tunes on this site which were written by someone, either someone in a well-known band, or a soloist, or are a composition by the person who submitted it. Some of these are good, some are very good, and some are crap.
Question is though, are they “traditional”? I remember seeing, in the comments section of one of these tunes, that somebody wrote something like “You’d better write in the composer, or else people’ll start thinking it’s traditional”. This made me think: what makes a tune “traditional”? Is it any tune that vaguely sounds like it should belong in this genre? Or is it only tunes that were written by someone who knows what they’re doing? Or do they only “become” traditional when people start playing them in sessions? Or do they have to have arisen from the ancient soil of the land, like turnips or something?
Are all tunes as traditional (or not) as each other, or are there degrees of traditionality, like with Drowsy Maggie and The Wind that Shakes the Barley at the top, and stuff like Catharsis and The Wizard’s Walk at the bottom?
What do you think is The Answer?

Re: Traditionality

It’s one of those enigmatic anomalies that seems to be the rule here at the sesh. “Traditional” doesn’t mean “traditional” as in style, but rather in the context of the tunes as they relate to who composed them and when they might have been composed. “Traditional” seems to mean “composer unknown.”

Take a tune like the Shaskeen: We know it was played by a piper from that townland in Sligo, and he might even still be living for all I know. We don’t know if he composed it or not, but since we don’t know -- the tune remains “traditional.”

Don’t ask me… it wasn’t my idea… and I don’t like it either, but now it’s a tradition to use “traditional” like that in this context.

Re: Traditionality

In Irish Traditional Dance music either will do.

The Shaskeen is a very old theme which is played today in common time. It does NOT have a composer or any record of one. Probably began as a ‘karant’ or song with a lilt to it.

There are tunes which are composed in the traditional style, eg Reavie’s or Wynn’s etc, but these all have fragments from older strains which the composers borrowed.

A great example today is a tune called “Otter Holt” and an old Co Roscommon ‘karant’ called “The Ould Doojeen” often misnamed “The Old Dudeen”. Explanation is really simple and a little humorous. A Doojeen is a clay pipe, and folks often so poor they would not be able to afford a new clay pipe with every wad of tobacco. So a Doojeen would be ‘left by’ = put away for later.

A visitor - isn’t it always somebody who arriving into a tradition think he/she can make it better by re-writing everything ie destroying it…see W Clare today for more on that one - hearing the old tune took the bits they could play and filled in the spaces making a clever tune out of it. On the fiddle the original when played even badly has great ‘draiocht’ to it and not a little humor.

They call this sort of thing ‘the folk process’ BTW.

So traditional often means just that, a history of some kind.

You must know OC that nearly all ITM is imported, Jigs are Italian, Hornpipe is English and Reel is Scottish. Of the rest I believe that the ‘Barn’ or Long Dance, the Air, the old Clan Marches as well as the Slides can claim a unique Irish origin.

That said, many of the melodies that comprise the lore of imported forms in Ireland are Irish or borrowed from Irish songs.

Consider for example ‘The Mist Covered Mountain’ by J Crehan, which the author admits he took from an old Irish Air.

Re: Traditionality

Sorry to take exception with almost everything said above.
“Tradition” is too broad a description for what I presume is being discussed. For the moment, I ask you to accept the term “folklore”, which includes
tunes, songs, stories, and plays.
Where folklore comes from is not as important as the fact that it has been introduced and accepted into a closed society. The litmus test is that it must be embraced and performed (oh, oh, that word again) and adapted FOR and BY the society itself, and passed down for several generations. Then it could be called “traditional” by that particular society - a village, or larger community, or in some cases, a country.
It is possible for someone to produce a tune, for example, IN THE TRADITION, that is, seemingly similar or for some, indistinguishable from the folklore; but it cannot be called folklore (or traditional) until “the folk” embrace it over time.
Even in the broadest sense of “tradition”, something has to be observed or performed a number of times in the same setting, or at the same time, to earn the description.
Of course, anything can be called anything, and by anyone; but if the language becomes too diluted, it means nothing. Start over with a fresh description.

Traditionality? hmmmmmm In an interview, Noel Hill said:

“And then my generation came along, born in the late ’50’s, and I started taking an interest in the music from the very first day I heard Willie Clancy playing pipes. before I ever started playing I had an immediate interest in this music because I heard a lot of Willie Clancy, hours and hours of Willie Clancy playing, so also the older fiddlers, concertina players, and flute players in my area. So I’d heard all of this around me as a child. I knew I wanted this music and I wanted to know more about it ….”

“Well you see, I didn’t set out to have a style. My style is just a consequence of the upbringing I had. Particularly having heard, at a young age, so much of the piping of Willie Clancy. Then later on when I moved to Dublin in the late ’70’s, I knew and became a good friend of Seamus Ennis who probably left the greatest mark on me musically, Seamus Ennis did. So the piping laid down certain ideas and shape and phrasing of the music, and ornamentation within the music. That was laid down in my subconscious. Then I’d heard the flute players say like Peter Lochlin, and Jimmy Kennedy, all those great players from around the area of Kilmaley, whose music was full of rhythm and full of punch and a very, very beautiful, comfortable rhythm. That was there. Then there were the concertina players of course, Mrs. Crotty. My uncle, God rest him, Paddy Hill. My mother who was actually a concertina player. And my father who played a lot of music on the concertina, particularly hornpipes and polkas and tunes that you wouldn’t hear at all. So there was that whole mix of the concertina music I had heard, and particularly the piping, the fiddling and flute playing, laid down in the subconscious phrasing and structure and taste and ornamentation . All of that left me with my style today. I didn’t go out to be what I am either musically, style-wise or any other way. If you listen to my playing, I hope that I describe it correctly by saying that its very rooted in the ………… TRADITION OF WEST CLARE.”

I think it’s interesting to note that he doesn’t say the traditional ‘MUSIC’ of West Clare, but simply the tradition, so, to me he’s saying that all that first hand experience with the environment, the community & the players etc etc gives him his musical sense & expression.

For me then, for someone like Noel to write a tune, & others of a similar age, like him, who have grown up surrounded by a similar tradition, in the broadest sense of the word, then I would be quite comfortable with calling that tunes traditional.

I think, where new tunes often fall flat on their ar$e, is when they are composed by young musicians who have learned their music in a bubble, perhaps even with a strong classical element to their tuition, from notation plus CDs & records & tapes or perhaps worse, from noodling in that, often clique ridden, environment we now call the session. They spend 4 or 5 years playing nothing but Jigs & Reels & then bingo, they know it all & start churning out notes & that’s what their tunes are, nothing more than a string of notes with no melody, no depth, no heart, no soul.

Please tell me the tradition is much much more than simply a bunch of fast tunes played by a bunch of self obsessed speed merchants, with a ‘Technique is GOD’ fixation!

“Traditional” does not mean “old”. Nor does it mean “dead”. Nor does it mean “forgotten”. A tradition, by definition, means it’s an ongoing process. Anyone else heard Peter Kennedy’s interview and recording of the genius Donegal fiddler Neilly Boyle? On it, he plays and talks about the reel Sean sa cheo, which is a very traditional, old tune. According to Neilly, the composers of old tunes like this one have been forgotten not so much because the music grows out of the soil, but because so much trouble for so long in Ireland made it very difficult to document and remember the real composer. Just a thought, but I think there must be some truth to this, especially since far back enough (before, I think, times became really hard) there was a harping tradition, with players like O’Carolan being credited and documented.
