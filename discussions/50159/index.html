Re: What your instrument says about you?

I didn’t choose many of the instruments I play, they often chose me.

The only reason I play Anglo Concertina is that Bob Tedrow traded me one of his concertinas for a flute I was selling at the time. I had never considered playing concertina before, now I’ve been playing for well over 20 years.

The only reason I play B/C box is that about 15 years ago I wanted to see if the iPad accordion apps I developed actually helped someone learn the real instrument, so I got a Hohner Double-Ray to test the theory and from that time I’ve played the B/C box. Before then I thought “what a ridiculous instrument, it’s like trying to put music on an air conditioner strapped to your chest”. Now I often lead the session I host on B/C box.

The only reason I play traditional Irish music in sessions at all is because 30 years ago I tried to bring a hammered dulcimer (had been playing/teaching for about a decade at that time) on a Southwest Airlines flight nearly 30 years ago and they wouldn’t let me put it in an overhead compartment.

Had a huge fight with the gate attendant who absolutely wouldn’t let me bring in onboard, finally they checked it as normal baggage (it survived the flight), but I had this thought:

“I need to play something smaller.”

and everything else that has happened since was a result.

At the time I loved the group Helicon (Ken Kolodner - Hammered Dulcimer (I’d taken his Swannanoa class), Robin Bullock - Octave Mandolin, Chris Norman - Wooden Flute) so first took up octave mandolin which lead to tenor banjo.

I already had wind experience from being a classical flute player in my youth so learned tin whistle, that slippery sloped into wooden flute, and then Uilleann pipes.

Eventually my wife and a friend took me to a pub where there was a session happening. Never knew there was such a thing. Now I’ve hosted my own for over 25 years.

If not for that fight with the Southwest Airlines gate attendant, I’d probably still be a hammer dulcimer player/teacher and would have never gotten into playing in sessions.

And yeah, I play too many instruments. Hopefully will help me avoid dementia in my sunset years (I’m 63).

“Could you spot a banjo player off the street?”

Be like Sherlock and Mycroft Holmes, peering out the first-storey window at the passers-by in the street below, guessing each persons’ occupation?

I worked at Disneyland (California) for 35 years and each day saw tens of thousands of people walking by. We played that game often. We got pretty good at it.

Many are gimmies with people wearing t-shirts or hats proclaiming their profession or hobby. Many others are more subtle. Without hearing their accents we could often guess what State or Country a person was from. Without obvious clues we could often guess a person’s profession. Since we often ended up having a chat with the people after our initial sighting and guessing we had the opportunity to confirm or refute our guesses.

So to the point, specific instruments no. But we could easily spot “folkies”, the sort of person who probably plays some folk instrument or other. Just as easy, of course, were rock musicians and Country musicians. Jazz musicians could often be identified too (soul patch etc).

Re: What your instrument says about you?

@conical bore - While I suppose it’s true that as a multi-instrumentalist my practice time is divided between different instruments, but on the other hand, I’ve been playing music long enough that I no longer find a lot of need to practice instrumental technique on most instruments - the piano aside, since I’ve been playing that for a lot less time than any of the others, and I’m still working on getting my hands coordinated. What I do spend time on is learning new tunes, and I find that what I learn about a tune on one instrument usually transfers in some interesting way to all of the other instruments. Each instrument lends itself naturally to certain sorts of phrasing, and if I learn a tune on the box that will color the way I play it on the flute, and so forth. Even instruments as similar as the flute and the whistle have subtle differences, for example I usually find that I play longer phrases on the whistle. Learning a tune on the flute that I already play on the whistle therefore forces me to think in shorter phrases, which gives me a whole range of new ideas. Similarly, not having to pause for breath when playing the banjo is a mixed blessing, so the imposed phrasing constraints of the flute or the whistle inform my banjo playing - but contrariwise, the banjo allows me to find ideas which I can transfer back to the flute.

I do think it’s interesting that most of my ideas about playing this music seem to come from fiddle players, since fiddle is one of the main instruments in this music that I’ve never really got to grips with.

↳ Posted by Jon Kiparsky on April 22nd, 2025.

Re: What your instrument says about you?

This is an interesting question. I play whistle and flute (mainly whistle), and I recently started Lindsay System SSPs. I think my instruments say a lot about my personality, actually.

Like many people, I took up whistle initially because it was an easy instrument to play simple melodies on. I stuck with it over the flute for a long time, because it was so much easier to learn to play tunes fast and flexibly on it. Sure, I could make flute my main instrument, but it takes much longer to learn tunes on flute, so I always end up going back to whistle. I also love that it’s in a higher octave, and I can always hear myself play. So I guess if it says anything about me, it’s that I like learning things quickly (which some people might call laziness, but I see as a kind of ambition), and I’m not afraid to be in the spotlight from time to time. I guess the stereotype of whistle players is, as someone else mentioned, that we’re too lazy to play a “proper” instrument. I want to prove that idea wrong, because I think I play a proper instrument, thank you very much. The fact that I was able to learn tunes on it faster than you learned them on your instrument doesn’t make it less of an instrument. It just means that I can learn more tunes and play faster and with more ornaments than I’d be able to on a more “difficult” instrument.

As for flute, the fact that I play it at all shows that I give into peer pressure. I was tired of people telling me I should learn flute, so I was like…well, why not? And now I’m glad I did, because I quite enjoy flute.

Lindsay System SSPs show that I’m not at all satisfied with conventional instrument designs and like buying instruments with fancy bells and whistles (no pun intended) that can do everything I want. Which is further demonstrated by the fact that I love weird, novel whistle designs, such as Morneaux’s 10-hole chromatic whistle. My favorite thing about folk instruments is the lack of standardization, because it means you can always find something new and unique.
