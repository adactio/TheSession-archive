Question to Irish Trad Musician/Mathematician

I’ve come across what’s called Shannon Number, and for those who don’t know, here is the Wikipedia page:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_number

Similar to this, I wonder if there is a mathematical way to estimate how many distinct Irish tunes can exist.

For example, just think of reels in G major.

Excluding incidental sharps and flats, there can be 19 notes: G, A, B, C D E #F G A B c d e #f g a b c‘ d’

A-part is made of 8 bars and B-part is also made of 8 bars, so a typical reel is made of 16 bars.

Each bar contains 1/8 notes x 8

So, a reel is made of 16 x 8 = 128 of 1/8 notes.

There are 19 powered 128 ways to arrange notes in 128 places.

But obviously, a vast majority of these arrangements/permutations will be just random notes everywhere and they won’t sound anything like a cohesive tune. So, it’s not a very elegant estimate.

Only a tiny fraction of them would sound remotely like a tune.

If there are mathematicians here, I would like to ask how you would approach this.

Re: Question to Irish Trad Musician/Mathematician

The naive approach, straight combinatorics, would give you a very loose upper bound. You could also try a more nuanced and analytic approach, something like the following. Consider reels only, for simplicity. Take the full corpus that you consider to represent the canon of Irish reels, and normalize them to eighth-note representations (eliminate triplets, break up quarter notes into pairs of eighths, etc). Then list all of the eighth-note pairs and assume that since each pair has appeared in at least one canonical tune, it is a possible pair. Then you can compute the number of combinations of those pairs, and you’ll have a tighter upper bound, but it’ll still be counting a lot of reels that nobody would consider “possible”. You can tighten that bound by repeating the process for pairs: all of the sequences of pairs are now possible sequences occupying four eighth-notes, so do the combinatorics again.

Essentially, this is reinventing the Markov chain, which I can tell you from my experiments is a great way to generate completely unlistenable and unplayable garbage, unless you set the length of the chain to about eight or ten notes, at which point you start overfitting, reproducing exact tunes from your training set because there is only one possible successor for each eight- or ten-note chain.

Re: Question to Irish Trad Musician/Mathematician

I think it would be helpful to identify as many rules as possible which define combinations of notes that would never occur in an Irish tune. For example:
- no more than five consecutive repeats of a note
- at least five different notes used in the tune
- b part different to the a part
- maybe a minimum of 25% of the notes must be adjacent to the previous note?
Then calculate the number of combinations remaining; listen to as large a sample of them as you can bear, as David50 suggests, to see what percentage of them sound plausible as an Irish tune; and extrapolate upwards to get your Shannon number.

Re: Question to Irish Trad Musician/Mathematician

The trouble with trying to do math about this is that you need a working definition of what constitutes a “tune”. If you had that, you’d be home free, but you’re not going to get that, so you’re stuck with heuritstics and proxies of various sorts.

I like the spirit of the “generate 1K note sequences and count the ones that are like jigs” as a probabilistic approach, but I suspect that you’d need a lot more than that to get even one acceptable tune. Again, it depends on your definition of “acceptable”, but the number of possible combinations of a jigload of notes is vastly, even astronomically greater than the number of such combinations which could even plausibly be considered jig-like. Estimating the latter number is again tricky, because definition, but if you consider a simple two-part jig there are 2 * 16 * 6 = 192 distinct 1/8 notes, and if we limit ourselves to the basic tin whistle scale (D major + c nat), you get 7 ^ 192 = a gazillion possible tune-shaped objects which differ from your original by one note, so good luck trying to work out which ones still represent a valid tune.

Re: Question to Irish Trad Musician/Mathematician

The salient point of the Shannon number is that it demonstrated the futility, at least with computational capacity in 1950, of calculating every possible outcome with the goal of training a computer to successfully play chess using only legal moves.

On the face of it, it seems likely that generating tunes would run into the same issues. Perhaps, however, the “legal moves” of intervals and phrases in traditional Irish tunes would add a helpful cap to the number of permutations.

Sometimes, when I hear the latest new tune, or when a session plays the same 50 tunes week after week, I wonder if we haven’t already hit the legal move cap. ;-)

Re: Question to Irish Trad Musician/Mathematician

“how many *DISTINCT* tunes”. this is what makes the problem difficult and interesting. not only you need to generate note sequences that are music, but you also must define a metric for “this is the same tune”.

for example, a 1 note change is “same tune”, a key change is “same tune”, reel changed to hornpipe is “same tune”, reel changed to jig could be “same tune, depending who you ask”.

then, as you generate a tune candidate, you must apply this metric and compare it to all other existing tunes to confirm “yup, I created one more variation of the Winster gallop”.

I would think this will reduce the number of possible tunes substantially (and somewhat increase the computational needs, this is an O(N^2) situation, check every new tune against every existing tune).

then screen tune candidates for playability on common instruments. it is easy to generate finger twisters like “the broken bow reel”, but will anybody play them, and tunes that nobody plays or nobody would play, do they really exist? this is single-hand clapping in the middle of an empty forest territory.

this further reduces the number of possible irish tunes.

(omit the filter against “this tune sounds Scottish” or “this tune sounds like bluegrass”, too subjective).

I think Calum is onto something. An Irish tune can only be defined as such if it’s known and played by a musician within the tradition - not necessarily an Irish national but someone with some knowledge of the form who plays an appropriate instrument.
Therefore maximum number of tunes = number of active musicians X maximum tune capacity of one musician.
I feel like 1000 is an acceptable approximation for the average theoretical maximum tune capacity per musician, but the problem is that to reach that capacity you’d need to learn a lot of the same tunes that everyone else knows, reducing the theoretical maximum hugely.
I’m also not sure what proportion of the world population to use as the theoretical maximum number of musicians, so still a bit of work to do…

Re: Question to Irish Trad Musician/Mathematician

Here’s a mathematical question that I find interesting. It’s a gross oversimplification of what happens in real life, but I think the answer would be a starting point towards answering the common beginner’s question: how many tunes do I have to learn to play in a session?

Suppose a group of people have a weekly session. They have compiled a tune book with T tunes that they all know and each week they randomly choose a set of S tunes from their book to play. A new player shows up and is given a copy of the book. (T will likely increase over time, and S will vary from week to week, but the new player has a rough idea of their size, so let’s say they are constant.) The new player wants to have a reasonable probability P of knowing at least K of the S tunes that get played. How many tunes should the player learn (as a function of T, S, P, and K)?

Abstractly, let X be a set of T elements and let Y be a subset of X having L elements. Let 0<P≤1 and K≤S. How big does L have to be for the probability of a random subset of X having S elements intersects L in at least K elements to be P.

I did a thought experiment some years ago.
Take just “jigs”.
Notes allowed in tune: D E F# G A B c d e f# g a b
Next note must be 0 to 4 scale steps away. So starting on G, you have D E F# G A B c d.
After just one bar of music (6 notes) you have a huge number (tens of thousands) of possible tunes, though many make no musical sense, at least in the context of Irish music. After two bars you have billions of possible tunes. Even removing those that don’t make musical sense (by applying some types of rules), just two bars of music gives so many possible tunes that I figured that there’s virtually no risk that all the good ones are already taken.

Yes, I think that’s the basic flaw in the mathematical approach to the problem - as I said above, combinatorics gives you a useless answer (effectively infinite) and there isn’t an actionable definition that you can use to refine that estimate.
