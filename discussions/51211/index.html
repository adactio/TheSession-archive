Re: Singing the notes as ear training

Singing is always good. Developing a sense of intonation takes time, but anyone can do it with practice. IMO it’s better to think about which note of the scale you’re singing/playing. Notes 1, 3, and 5 are the most consonant, and their neighboring notes tend to resolve to them. For example, 7 and 2 naturally pull toward 1.

Re: Singing the notes as ear training

I think most of us can sing fairly naturally and “whistling” or “humming” is just a slightly different form of the same process.. i.e. just an output of what is in our head at the time. It also works with your instruments too if you are familiar enough with the layout and scales etc. You just don’t really need to think about where you are putting your fingers.

That’s not to say we are all good singers, of course. Much has to do the timbre of our voices, breathing, pacing, control of pitch etc and…in my case… nerves. However, I reckon most of us can manage the basics.

If I’ve heard a tune or song I like, I will often sing or hum it first before trying it out on an instrument. That’s what I always did before the days of these “learning music by ear” workshops and classes. ….I’m not convinced that’s the best way really and/or any better than the dots in the long run. You only really know a tune when you’ve got it in your head after repeated listening either to others or your own playing.

As for singing or humming from the dots, I’ve found that’s a slightly different skill from directly locating the notes on your instrument. Trained singers can do this quite easily, of course.
However, I’m quite good now at looking at sheet music and being able to hum or sing a tune in my head. It’s quite good fun on long bus journeys and you can practise your sets without disturbing all the other passengers. :-)

Re: Singing the notes as ear training

> for a tune like Ships are Sailing where E is the tonal center but the melody has a C# instead of C natural, would you consider note E to be Re?

There are two approaches, and both have their arguments for and against. One is to say that doh is always the root note of the scale, whatever mode it is. You then sing whatever the altered form of the major scale syllables are (though there is also the school that says you just sing the alteration with the same syllable).

The other approach is to say that the pattern remains unchanged and the root of the scale is whatever syllable it happens to land on (this is usually referred to as la-based minor in classical-land).

The latter is easier to learn but you learn less about scale construction doing it, as the modes come “for free”.

As we all know, a lot of trad tunes can be modally ambiguous. The Ships are Sailing example is a good one; I think trying to apply doh to the E (or the B?) would be hard work, personally, though maybe the reward is worth it.

Re: Singing the notes as ear training

Lilting tunes has been a very helpful part of my tune learning since day one. I had the good fortune to be taught by Shannon Heaton when I was first learning, and she pointed out a few things to me:

1. It doesn’t matter whether you’re a good singer or not. In fact, I lilt tunes silently all the time.

2. It helps to use syllables that provide more information than just what note it is. Instead of singing “la la la”, you can provide more contextual information by using different syllables for different notes. Instead of going full solfege, many people (including myself) use syllables that are keyed on how high the note is. Most commonly “Dum” for low notes, “Dah” for middle notes, and “Di” for high notes, and of course, “Diddly” (or “Dahdly”, “Dumbley”) for rolls or triplets… And then you can fill in the connections between the notes with other syllables. When you lilt a tune this way, you’re giving yourself context of where the tune is going, without having to figure out whether a note is a Mi, or a Mi sharp. ;-) And that context can help you remember the contour of the tune better than “la la la la” will.

So, for instance, if I were to lilt the beginning of Maid Behind The Bar, it would be something like:

Dum ba - Dum ba da di - da buh dum ba - Dum ba da di - dah, da da - di da da da - dum ba da da - Di di da - da da da dum…

I don’t determine the syllable by what note it is, just by where it is relative to the other notes, so C# in one tune might be a “Dah”, but might be a “Di” in another tune…

Anyway, that’s what works best for me!
