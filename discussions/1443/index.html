Added by Nell on February 24th, 2003

What is accompaniment for?

This question was put to me by an excellent guitarist the other night. I was talking about singing, & recording, some songs with an accompanist, and he asked what I needed accompaniment for. In the discussion that followed, he described accompaniment as a ‘cradle’ which makes listeners and other players feel more secure. (I hope I’m not misrepresenting his argument - but this was in the very small hours and I’m afraid that some drink had been taken..) In trying to answer the question, I thought that my main motive might be that it’s more interesting for me, since it adds something I can’t do on my own; and listening to myself on a recording, unadulterated, is never going to be pleasant. All I hear is the weak spots and the faults. At least if someone else is doing something charming on a guitar, there’s hope that I might be pleased with the result and be able to bear hearing it…
This man by the way is a great tune-backer. But he didn’t play at all on the night in question, as there was already a backer, on keyboards, and just a few players at any time.

What do the rest of you think about accompaniment, of tunes or of songs? What *is* it for?

Re: What is accompaniment for?

Yes, and synchronicity is a lofty aesthetic consideration. To me, as a backer, synchronicity represents the ideal, both in selfish, personal concerns and lofty aesthetic ones. If I’m backing a fiddler and we wind up moving in a spontaneous direction because we’re both zoned in on “the whole” rather than “the parts,” it is such a thrill that it pains me to hear it broken down into an argument that trad tunes are “supposed to be” unaccompanied.
When a single melody player or singer steps into that zone all by himself or herself, the effect is magic, and listeners can be glad that, at that moment, there was no zouk player or guitar player banging away over the top of it. But if that melody player or singer and an accompaniest find themselves together in that zone, where the parts are greater than the whole, a sensitive listener can be equally glad that everything present was present and that everything absent was absent.
All too often, unaccompanied melody players -- and conversely, groups of melody players and/or melody players with backers -- must dwell in a more mundane world, where music is merely executed and magic eludes. But when it clicks, for whatever reason and in whatever combination, let us all rejoice, eh?

Re: What is accompaniment for?

I’m intrigued by the notion of this division between ‘Backers’ and ‘Melody players’. As a flute player I’m clearly in the latter group when playing tunes, but slip into the former group when accompanying singers, except during the instrumental break…
Are mandolin and guitar players who vary between playing ‘Rhythm’ and melody within a single tune backers? Are Fiddle players who occasionally play chords backers? I think that this question touches upon one of the major problems with session type music. The notion that one can be a ‘backer’ intruduces a two tier idea into the music, and it’s this dangerous notion that allows people think that they can play drum, guitar, bazouki or whatever without learning the tunes, or being astute enough to pick a tune up quickly enough. Even the much maligned bodhran is only maligned really because people have the idea that it is a ‘backing’ instrument and play it as though it had the same role as a rock and roll drumkit.
It reminds me of a wonderful experience I had a month or so back at a very ‘open’ session I go to, where I concocted what I thought was a pretty cool set starting with The Lads of Laios (E Minor(?)), going into The bag of spuds (Am), and making an interesting jump into A major with The Coast of Austria(!). It didn’t occur to me that the lady with the extra large jumbo guitar would even attempt to help us out, as I knew she didn’t know any of those tunes. Of course I was wrong. She played in G until someone managed to get through to her that the tune was in Em, into which she went just as we changed to the second tune, with an ‘Oh silly me’ look at us. Even she realised this was wrong, and switched back to G for The Coast of Austria. After a few bars of the musical equivalent of wading through treacle we ground to a halt and thanked her for her help.

I’ve always thought that harmonising was a particular skill and that people who can do it well are in a priveleged musical class of their own. What DC does is pure magic. In answer to Anny - in my own case, I was referring to singing - both performing and recording - but I was extending the principle to instruments as well. It’s tricky though, with the free timing in that style - it wasn’t made for accompaniment and it doesn’t sit with it easily. There aren’t many accompanists who either want to or can play well in free time.
This can make things tricky in a session. I prefer to sing unaccompanied, unless by someone who really understands the melody and can allow me to dictate the pace. When that happens, it’s great, I love it. But if they don’t and can’t, then it’s painful.
