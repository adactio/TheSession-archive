Guitar backing: moving up and down the neck constantly

I read in another discussion a member saying that he preferred moving ‘east-west’ than ‘north-south’, and that made me think about these two styles of approaching accompaniment. There are players who seem to remain down the neck for the first part of a tune and then go up the neck for some variation. Then, there are others who like moving up and down constantly for the entire tune, as the amazing John Doyle.

Which is your preference?

Re: Guitar backing: moving up and down the neck constantly

I imagine it’s going to vary depending on the approach to tuning, whether it’s Standard, Drop-D, or DADGAD. And also one’s experience, since beginners tend to stick to the more basic chords.

If you’re in Drop-D (what I use), there are some interesting variations up the neck on the A/Am and B/Bm chords and their “modal” forms (minus the third). I use those higher voiced chords when it seems appropriate for a tune. It might be as a variation the second or third time through, or it might be the starting chord if it’s something like a pipe tune in Amix.

DADGAD players tend to use more capo-shifting, and the better players will have chord variations away from the open position of the capo. Basically, you just play what works for ’ya, and that supports the music and doesn’t annoy the melody players.

Re: Guitar backing: moving up and down the neck constantly

I only use standard tuning, and never use a capo in sessions. I don’t intend to favour either up or down the neck, but simply play whatever is appropriate at the time (in a session). This could entail moving up or down “constantly” though the physical movement in itself ought to have little impact on other players. I play higher up the neck to take advantage of the alternative sounds available, whether it’s simply the higher notes, the combination with open strings left ringing or the ability to mute or partially mute the strings with the left hand. Although I may be fingering full chords, I often play a limited number of the strings. I prefer not to “strum” many of the standard chords at the first frets but may sometimes position my fingers for a full chord while using specific combinations of notes from within the chords.
When playing accompaniment in a situation where it has to be consistent, i.e. arrangements, I can’t say I have a preferred approach to where I finger notes/chords. I’ll start by using the techniques I’d use in a session. Again though, I’ll rarely use the standard chords at the first few frets, though they may provide an anchor for the fingers while playing or moving through a particular selection of notes. I may use a capo in arranged accompaniment for the usual reasons, either to shift into a singer’s preferred key or to make use of open strings in “odd” keys.

Re: Guitar backing: moving up and down the neck constantly

i like playing in eadgbe and have the option to both play in first position and moving across the neck. E, A and D keys leave you with a lot of possibilities since you can easily start a lot of different inverted chords and sostitutions. B and G i’ll most likely stay as near to the nut as possible, maybe halving the right hand pattern, and going up the neck only to steady up the thing. I tend to stay put on first position or to move on the neck depending on the tune itself, and most importantly the musician i’m playing with. Playing “open” chords in first position is more effective, in my opinion, to enphatize strong passages of the tune, while playing across the neck is better to give a rhythmic push. Certain soloists have a style that i would never accompany in a certain way, aswell as there are many tunes that in my ear sound best accompanied in a way rather than another. If you want an example of this, just try to play on youtube along a player like Kevin Burke, and you’ll see that strumming and flying on the neck is useless if not detrimental.

In general: If i want to swing it up, i go for the neck, maybe with 6 and 7 chords, walking the base strumming. You can do pretty much the same thing, walking the base i mean, on first position, giving it a more traditional sound, and i tend to do that on hornpipes and polkas, while i tend to swing up barndances.

Re: Guitar backing: moving up and down the neck constantly

I play standard in such a way that some people mistake it for drop D or DADGAD (all over the neck depending on key and different ‘voices’). In DADGAD you find people using a shape that would be the equivalent to a bar chord which can easily be moved up and down the neck and is easy to use either the major or minor third in it. This can give the illusion of someone being really busy when what their really doing is playing a bass line.

All In all, its personal preference and whatever sounds good
