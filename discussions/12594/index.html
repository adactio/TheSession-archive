How important is it to play a tune in its original or generally accepted style?

I thought about this after some of the discussion in the “ornament” thread.

Obviously, if you wish to play with certain musicians and/or in a particular type of session, it is important to adapt to what the general consensus is. So, you’ll want to play a tune in the same style with the same ornaments, techniques etc. Also, if you a have deep regard for a particular music and wish to “learn it properly”, you’ll understand this and make the effort.

However, if you are normally..say.. a Shetland fiddler and hear a Irish tune you like, do you learn it exactly as heard(or written, perhaps) or adapt it into your own style? The same, of course, would apply to tunes from everywhere. Do they have to be learned and played as originally intended?

You see, I’ve noticed endless discussions here about how important it is to play Irish music properly and so on but it doesn’t always seem to work the other way around; i.e. Scottish or other tunes still sound Irish when played by Irish(or Irish style) musicians.

Of course, as has been said here already, “the music” and “the tunes” are not the same thing and just because you might be playing an Irish or Scottish tune doesn’t mean that you are playing Irish or Scottish music.

Any thoughts?

Re: How important is it to play a tune in its original or generally accepted style?

I think it’s helpful to hear tunes played in diverse styles on a variety of instruments. If you’ve heard the same tune played as a slow reel on flute, on a BC box in a brisk ceilidh band, on a mandolin, on a fiddle a key up, or in a Scottish form, or whatever, you’ll be the more able to think laterally about the tune and adapt it to your own surroundings (or keep quiet if you can’t) - it can have any number of manifestations, but through them it’ll be recognisably the same tune. Learn how to keep in time and articulate a tune as your particular instrument can do it, and some way down the time-line you’ll probably find you’ve got a style of your own.

One “original” style seems to overlay another. The Bothies and other luminaries of that time created one sort (at least) of act to follow; some of their tunes were probably recorded by earlier players who sounded quite different. Either set of recordings seems to me a valid one to listen to and try to learn from.

Mine is an atomised, individualist etc. approach to learning and playing this music. I’ve the haziest of notions about Irish regional styles and am unlikely to spend much or any time in the places where these are or have been strong.

Heavens above, Northumberland just up the road from me is said to have four distinct Northumbrian piping styles! (For NTM anoraks - Tom Clough’s, Billy Pigg’s, Jack Armstrong’s, and Joe Hutton’s.) How many styles are there in Clare, e.g.? Which is the kosher-est of them all? And I mean for whistle and D/G melodeon, not for fiddle or pipes!

Re: How important is it to play a tune in its original or generally accepted style?

I have to disagree with you, John J. I reckon the original composers wrote with their own local ‘style’ in mind - the intention was that they play the tunes themselves, rather than that they would travel around the globe (or even just around the country).

The fact is that tunes can stand a hell of a lot of manipulation - even to the point where they’re almost unrecognisable. How many times have you been listening to someone play, and only realised on the second or third repetition that you know the tune?

I suspect the dots have a lot to do with morphing tunes - we all know how little information they actually convey. I’ve always played ‘The Blackthorn Stick’ with a slight lilt and a very even tempo, usually at a pretty fast pace. I recently heard a band playing it with a full jig rhythm - it sounded weird to me, but then I started to suspect that that was how the tune was originally played, and that generations of dot learners have smoothed it out.

And of course tunes can be played at vastly different speeds. Check out Brendan Powers‘ version of ’Cooley’s Reel‘ - he starts off really slow, then cranks up to full pace. Both speeds suit the tune. And ’The Otter’s Holt‘ slowed down by a factor of ten becomes the song ’Take Her In Your Arms’. It’s all good.

Re: How important is it to play a tune in its original or generally accepted style?

I see the tune as a constant, and the styles as its vectors in one place/tradition or another, and (more obviously, to me) on one instrument or another. And what I define a tune by is the sequence of notes that distinguish it from all other tunes. A player competent enough to vary spontaneously as he plays will still play enough of that sequence to communicate to listeners that he’s playing, say, “The Maid Behind The Bar” and not “The Wind That Shakes The Barley”. He would probably want to do this even if, say, he was playing to people who didn’t know ITM and would be happy with a hybrid tune.

Re: How important is it to play a tune in its original or generally accepted style?

With a classical composition, I believe that what the composer created & put down on paper must, more or less, be played exactly the same each time it is aired in public.

However, & it’s a thing that I love about traditional tunes, is the fact that when a trad musician writes a tune, he basically throws it out there knowing that every musician who picks that tune up is going to play it his or her own way & years later it may well come back to him in a very different form. So the tune, in a way, then becomes the property of each musician who takes the time, trouble & effort to learn it & pass it on.

So, in a way, a classical piece of music always belongs to the composer whereas a trad tune ‘belongs’ to all the trad musicians who play it & make it their own.

So, & this is where I disagree with Dow, if folks want to change the tempo or time signature, then they should, I believe, tear away, & if it works, then it will be adopted & played, but if it doesn’t it’ll remain in it’s original state.

So I don’t see any harm in turning Jigs into Reels too or speeding Hornpipes up into Reels, or vice-versa. After all, the original form is still there for all to see & play & the tradition, & time, will sort out which is the more popular form.

I love that Strathspeys can & have been modified into Flings & Reels over here - why not, they make cracking tunes & the Strathspey is still there, it’s not, I don’t think, been devalued in any way, in fact it has been honoured really, & shown that it has such strength of design that it can exist in different forms.

The way I see it, a Trad tune which only ever exists in exactly it’s original state is still a Caterpillar, while the one which is re-shaped over & over again by traditional musicians who love it, becomes the Butterfly! For years there were loads of Scottish tunes played on the Fiddle & Accordeon scene in Scotland which remained as Caterpillars but in recent years many have been taken out of those dusty old reference books & given new life & have actually metamorphosised into cracking tunes.

There is no need to worry, for the original tunes is still there, & always will be, in the dusty old book for the academics to ponder over.

Re: How important is it to play a tune in its original or generally accepted style?

I believe that in a session it is important that all players work together to produce a musically coherant version of whatever tune they are playing.This may mean setting personal preference aside for a while.
The tune may well be played differently at the next session if different musicians are present. I think this variety is a good thing.
Melodies, styles and arrangements always have, and always will, change with circumstances and evolve over time. “Original” is a fairly elusive concept for older tunes. “Generally accepted” is easier to pin down, especially in the context of specific musicians at a specific session.
No doubt many of us have an idea in our heads of how a particular tune ought to sound, but that doesn’t mean it’s the only way. I enjoy different slants on familiar tunes. Our session has grown into the habit of enjoying playing one particular reel as a hornpipe - we always warn any guests present of our intention and most of them enjoy it too.
In music, as in many areas, there are two kinds of unwise person - one says “It’s traditional, therefore it’s good.” The other would say “This is new, therefore it’s better.”

Re: How important is it to play a tune in its original or generally accepted style?

I’m sympathetic with just about every position expresssed here, at least partly.

I don’t want the music I love to be dragged off in this or that goofy lame direction as a result of somebody’s laziness, entrepreneurism or whatever, but poop happens. Folks will do what suits them. I moan about it like everybody else, but that’s like complaining that evolution has produced spiders and jellyfish and so I can’t enjoy bluebirds and blueberries. The folk process will continue processing and so will the capitalist process. Some of the results will be krap and some will be divine. Isn’t that how we got ITM?

People buy the pan flute pop music because they like it. I wish they also would try - and like - authentic traditional pan pipe music, but most of them probably wouldn’t like it.
