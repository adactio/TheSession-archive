The highs and lows of thesession reality show

Honestly you lot are ridiculous. You go from one extreme to the other - one minute you’re slagging the hell out of each other or coming out with the funniest off-the-wall humour, the next minute you’re at each other’s throats! You have to admit that some of what goes on here can be very soap opera-like - you’re such a bunch of drama queens. Of course I never get involved in all that stuff, I’m just so over it and above it all :-D

Maybe it’s cuz we’re arty-farty - I mean, to an extent you have to be emotional to play music well don’t you? I have to admit that I can be a bit up/downy. I’m the same when it comes to my progress with - and my approach to - the music in general. Sometimes I’m happy with how things are going. At other times I’m so peed off with it that I just want to quit altogether and sombrely and ceremoniously hoy my instruments onto a big bonfire. And then 5 minutes later I’m back playing tunes again.

Well today I’m on a high. I’ve had one of those weeks where something clicks music-wise, and I’ve broken one of those “barriers”, you know the ones.

I don’t know about you, but for me progress happens in sudden breakthroughs, and however much effort you put in between those leaps, nothing can happen for ages and you feel as though you’re not getting anywhere. Anyway, this week’s breakthrough has been one of those things which has had a domino effect. Fixing one thing has helped fix other things that at first seemed totally unrelated.

So I’d just like to say that I’m really really happy this week! There’s a session on Sunday and I’m looking forward to it. I just thought I’d tell you that because I have to tell someone :-)

That’s how it feels to me too.

But I suspect that *a lot* is actually happening and incubating between the leaps, and the leaps would not occur without the interim effort and “invisible progress.”

If there is such a thing as invisible progress, I’m not sure why we can’t see it or at least be more aware that some progress is being made, to ease our frustration. I don’t know what form that invisible progress takes--is it neural mapping? A slow erosion of excess motion until we hit some magic threshold of efficiency and the stubborn technique abruptly works? I don’t know.

I do know you can always blame your instrument (“I’d play better if I had a better fiddle/bow/flute/etc.”) and/or a lack of time (“I’d really improve if I didn’t have to work and could practice more than this measly 60 hours a week”). :o)

Re: The highs and lows of thesession reality show

Actually I don’t know that there is necessarily stuff going on that *causes* the breakthroughs. Obviously neurons are linking themselves as you practise during the “low periods”, and some of the time breakthroughs are going to be caused by the new links, but often my breakthroughs happen because of something I’ve thought about, often in the middle of the night, and it has absolutely nothing to do with my physical technique, even though whatever new thought process does ultimately improve my technique more than endless physical practice. It’s like: “what would happen if I did it this way, I’ll try that tomorrow”, and you try it the next day and all of a sudden you’re a better player without even having practised. It’s weird.

On the other hand, there always has to be a downside doesn’t there? I can’t just be allowed to be happy with my music as like this one individual emotion on its own. It has to be tinged with something sad or annoying. Today the bellows and my top B pad went west, so picture me taking my concertina apart with a screwdriver, bits and pieces everywhere. There’s no-one in Sydney to fix it and I’ve got no money to have it “rebellowed” anyway so I’ll just have to do it myself. I’ve decided to take drastic measures and go out and buy one of those glue gun things from the hardware shop tomorrow morning, so that the bits and pieces never ever come apart again, LOL! There’s no way I’m going to Sunday’s session without Tina because I have a couple of sets I’ve recently gone over and practised again. Ah well, such is life.

Deb, most of the fiddlers I’ve learned from have shied away from ever giving explicit bowing directions (Brian Conway being a notable and very worthwhile exception). So my experience is that us student fiddlers wander through years of bizarre and awkward bowing ideas before things start to click. I’d say it was a process of elimination, because one idea will get left behind for something new that seems to work better, except at some point in the process you find yourself doing a little of everything--nothing has been eliminated at all. In fact, it’s more a process of assimiliation--you learn to use every tool you come across, and make them your own.

Seems to me this process never ends, and a healthy sense of humor and wonder is the best way to survive it. :o)

Re: The highs and lows of thesession reality show

Heiki - excellent point! I’m convinced our brains (and bodies - seems the “brain” extends all the way to the nerve receptors in our fingertips, eh?) keep working this stuff out long after we put the fiddle back in its case.

I’ve read several sources that recommend working on a new skill only to the point of initial frustration. Then stopping for the day and not coming back to it till the following day. This gives our minds time to organize and assimilate the new information, so it’s “ready” for the fresh start. This approach works well for me--I use it to learn tunes in particular. Play a new tune for 5-10 minutes and then leave it till tomorrow. Do NOT skip tomorrow, but also don’t overplay the tune. By the third day, most tunes are cemented in place and ready to be explored, even at session tempo. My biggest frustration with this is when life interferes and I miss the 2nd or 3rd day--then it can feel like starting over.
