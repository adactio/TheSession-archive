Re: Who’s comfortable playing solo and who hides?

“If no one else knows the tune does that make the rendition a performance, or do the other players pretend not to listen?”

The other players will sometimes listen…and you feel that it’s a performance..which makes you nervous. Other times, they will ignore you and start a conversation between themselves. Again, this can make you nervous and, if you’re paranoid, you might think they’re ignoring or belittling your efforts. You might well be right, knowing some of the people I do ;-( .
However, in most cases, I think the other musicians just feel awkward and don’t want to appear to be giving you undue attention and making you feel as if you’re “on the spot”.

I don’t think we always have to stick to “known” tunes either. New and different tunes have to get an airing sometime but the advice to follow it up with a better known tune is sound.

Finally, and I know that I’ll upset the anti noodling brigade here, I really appreciate when one or more musicians are prepared to “busk along” or give some accompaniment even on a strange tune. As long as they are competent and don’t try to be too clever. I’d rather have some support, even although it’s not note perfect.

Re: Who’s comfortable playing solo and who hides?

This one is very timely for me. I recently decided to take a whole new approach to session playing. I’m very picky about the tunes I learn, and I’m tired of worrying about whether anybody else knows them. A proper session is not about the entire group playing one shop-worn tune after another. Listen to the session recording at Matt Malloy’s. Sometimes everyone plays, sometimes it’s a flute and a guitar, or it can be a solo singer. Now, how in the world can a traditional singer do a tune if EVERYONE has to play? The tune ain’t done that way! So if I want to play a tune that likely will not be known by many or any, I pair it with something everyone will know. The effect of going from a solo tune to one where gradually more and more musicians join in sounds great, everyone gets to hear a new (to them) tune, and the session is all the better for it. I think a session should be as much about listening as playing. Again, listen to “At Matt Malloy’s” If that isn’t the ultimate session, I don’t know what is.

Re: Who’s comfortable playing solo and who hides?

I see Matt’s session is being used as an example here. I’m sure they knew what they were going to play before they started. It doesn’t sound to me like they’re stopping at the change and waiting to see what the next tune is before jumping back in. But that’s just a session style: knowing what tunes are coming.

I’ve seen this session style in action where they will decide the tunes by first playing snippets. Once they’ve determined what the tunes are going to be -- off they go. That’s why there are no awkward moments on the changes. I prefer this method myself and can easily determine before I start playing whether or not I’ll be supported or left on my own to play a particular tune.

Since I like the sound of everyone playing together I can make sure this will be the case using this method. Sometimes I will start out with a tune that no one else knows, but I can make sure others know the tunes that follow.

The interesting thing is that when I first tried to introduce this concept in my local session it was met with strong resistance. People would interrupt me and say things like, “Just play the feckin tune, Jack!” It seemed that they preferred the crash and burn tune changes that were a common feature in the local modus operandi. But eventually some of them recognized the advantages and started doing it themselves when they realized it reduced the likelihood of suddenly being the soloist in a session.

Another interesting thing is that people who seem to enjoy being soloists at sessions were the ones that protested the most when I tried to introduce this approach. Other people just seemed to think it was too much of a bother and didn’t care if the plane crashed now and then. And still others seemed to think it got in the way of the spontaneity. At this point we’ve settled on letting the person suggesting the tunes decide what approach to use when it’s their turn. It’s all good as long as no one attempts to impose they’re particular preferred session style on the overall proceedings.

Re: Who’s comfortable playing solo and who hides?

Playing as part of the group is definitely easier than solo playing. If you’re playing on your own and have a memory lapse the other players will keep the ball rolling. If you’ve somehow slipped into a different B-part or something, the other players will help you out there as well. Also, if you don’t have all the tees crossed and eyes dotted it won’t be as obvious with everyone else playing, whereas when it’s just yourself all is lying naked. I tend to get self-conscious and become a little nervous when I suddenly find it’s all down to just me. This will cause me to bear down on whatever instrument I’m playing and I’ll loose any finesse I might have had up until that point. I suppose if I were a brilliant player it wouldn’t matter so much.

Re: Who’s comfortable playing solo and who hides?

The question is, Jack, do you find yourself more self conscious when there’s a big audience of non-musos, or when it’s just a group of sessioners? Perhaps that’s a whole thread on it’s own.

I think your idea of playing snippets is sound, but does it break up the momentum? When a session is really rolling there’s hardly time to draw breath between the tunes (or take a quick sip of the black stuff). The trouble with naming the set, for example ‘Merry Blacksmith into Rattigan’s into Silver Spear’, is that not everyone knows the names of the tunes or even knows them by the same names. I’ve found this even in sessions where you’ve heard people play a tune before; say the name and they give you a blank look - play a snippet and they go ‘OH - that one…’ (and usually look at you like you’re a bit slow) ;-)

If I start a tune and no-one joins in I think it’s fair play to do three repetitions. If I change to a tune in the middle of a set and lose the other players I usually play it twice to allow people to recognise it, or just once if it’s particularly long or complicated, then go on to something else I’m pretty sure they’ll know.

I’m always trying to fill in the gaps in my repertoire, so I like to hear multiple repetitions of a tune I don’t know to give me a chance to pick them up, especially if it’s rare or obscure (a modern composition or Middle Eastern, French, etc.)

I have observed some people who get very impatient with people playing anything they can’t play along with. They’re usually the ones that start a new tune the very second the set finishes, treading on the end of the last note, so that they actually get to play something. Personally I think that’s one of the rudest things you can do at a session, but perhaps that’s just me…

A slightly different tack to my own reaction (above)..I’ve got a friend who is a professional muso and brilliant fiddle player and guitarist who was getting back into fiddle after a long injury induced break. We were at a wedding, and a piper had everyone up dancing, but he’d had a few too many and couldn’t remember the ends of most tunes, and the dancing was faltering. So my friend grabbed his fiddle and joined in one of the tunes, at which point the piper looked grateful and sat down, leaving fiddler going solo. And the music lasted just as long as the dancers continued to dance. As soon as a sub group of dancers decided to sit and listen….he stopped. So I reckon he was comfortable without the wall of sound whilst there dancing that needed tunes, but as soon as the balance shifted and there were more people listening than dancing…he became self conscious. That was my take on it anyway.
