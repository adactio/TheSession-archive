Here’s to the accompanists!

So I’ve generally taken somewhat of a purist attitude toward accompaniment in Irish Trad. I do believe that the music can be considered “complete” without any accompaniment. After all, the melody players are defining both the rhythm and the melody.

Don’t get me wrong, I have the pleasure of playing with several accompanists that I highly respect, and I do enjoy it! (And I have also played with accompaniment that really destroyed the lift and drive of the session…)

But due to various circumstances, I have recently played two sessions in a row without any accompaniment. And I was absolutely surprised at how much I missed it! Good accompaniment has a way of filling out the sound, and producing a more textured and unified feel for people that are listening. Last night’s session was filled with good players and good tunes, but I could tell that the crowd, which is normally very enthusiastic about the music, was quite a bit more subdued in their energy level. And I tend to feed off the energy of the crowd, so I really missed it! (Ironcially, our tip jar had about double what it would on an average night there -- a fact that I will forever hold over the accompanists’ heads, of course ;-))

I started in this music doing accompaniment (poorly), so I know how hard it is to do well. And I know accompanists don’t always get the respect that they deserve here, so this is my shout out to them: Thank you for spending endless hours of your life learning the music and learning how to complement the melody players in a productive way! Sometimes, it’s just not the same without you!

And I’d love to hear some stories from other players about times that accompaniment has been a big part of your musical experience… (No reason to post your horror stories, we can easily search the archives for plenty of that!)

Re: Here’s to the accompanists!

“the #1 thing as a melody player (that I want from a backer) is ROCK SOLID RHYTHM.”

I have the opposite view, or maybe a different way of expressing it. Of course a backer with shaky rhythm can be a nightmare, but for me it’s more about who is establishing the rhythm pulse -- the backer or the melody players. My ideal backer is someone who follows the rhythm pulse of the melody players wherever it goes. It’s the opposite of Americana genres where the “rhythm section” establishes a steady bedrock and the melody players sit on top of that.

I started playing this music as a guitar backer before moving into melody with mandolin and flute. At one time my S.O. and I co-hosted an intermediate level pub session where I would often back on guitar. One night, a friend was visiting who was a bass player in an OldTime jams (she wasn’t playing with us, just a visitor). After the session ended, she made a comment about one set of tunes that got a bit feisty with the tempo as it progressed, and she asked why I didn’t try to “hold them back” on guitar. I responded that in this style of music I didn’t see that as my job. If that’s where the fiddlers wanted to go, my job was to *listen* to what was happening, then support and follow them.

It doesn’t surprise me that people have different views on this, both the backers and melody players. I’ve been in sessions where a rock solid rhythm would have been an asset, because the melody players were drifting apart. It’s just my ideal to be in a session where the melody players themselves have locked into a tight groove, and the backer is just along for the ride, adding texture more than a metronome function. That’s the kind of backing I’m happy to celebrate in this thread.

Re: Here’s to the accompanists!

Conical Bore wrote: “…it’s more who is establishing the rhythm pulse -- the backer or the melody players.”

Agreed. I suspect there are reasons for differences in this among various genres.

In bluegrass and Appalachian music, for instance, typically only one tune at a time is played, not sets. So it makes sense to play that tune with one feel, tempo, pulse, degree of swing, etc. Guitar and/or bass can set the groove for the melody players. In bluegrass, the melody players alternate in taking solos, so of necessity the bass usually provides the constant pulse.

In contrast, in Irish trad, stringing tunes together, those same qualities (less so tempo) often want to flex slightly to suit each tune. I would go so far as to say that it’s less the melody players setting the feel and groove but the tunes themselves, and each tune wants it’s particular groove. Sets work best when melody players and backers alike are tuned in to the subtle character of each tune.

Of course, you sometimes hear a melody player trying to shoehorn tunes into awkward grooves—trying to play a flowing, lyrical tune with mega drive and punch, say. A talented musician can almost make it work. :-)

Back to my one of my favourite types of tune: slow airs. Yes they can be magical unaccompanied, but so too - even better sometimes - with a truly sympathetic and gentle accompanist. I can think of a couple of tunes where perhaps you have the same note repeated several times: a great accompanist will expand on that by perhaps doing a walk-down bass accompaniment or a different chord/arpeggio for each of those “same” notes making them “unsame” and thus adding to the whole texture and feeling of the piece. It’s a technique I use when accompanying on piano, and would have, in the past, on guitar.

Re: Here’s to the accompanists!

Whether or not we have a tip jar at the local Sessions usually depends on the location as well as who is hosting and/or leading the Session. Since we moved to the Hibernia Irish Tavern several years ago, we haven’t had a tip jar. However, audience members rarely, if ever, give us any money anyway but we don’t worry about that because we are there primarily to have fun playing music.
The owner of Hibernia is also the bartender and the waiter on the Sunday afternoons when we have our Sessions. Since the local law requires that he make a certain amount of profit from selling food so he can also sell alcoholic beverages on a Sunday, Hibernia does have a full time cook there on Sundays to comply with the law. Myself and some of the other musicians will usually order food to go. We take the food home with us after the Session has ended to eat it.

Re: Here’s to the accompanists!

Speaking as an accompanist, one thing which I believe is important is to keep the volume down low enough on my genuine ersatz fake imitation piano (a Roland FP-30 Digital Piano) so I can hear clearly what the other musicians at the Session are playing. I am not there at the Session to drown anyone out or to play loud enough to cover someone else’s mistakes. Making music at a Session is supposed to be a cooperative effort where everyone works together to make that organized noise called “Music”.
When I play for the local contra dance society, however, I do have to turn up the volume slightly because the dances are held in the Fellowship Hall at a local Presbyterian church and this Hall has such a high ceiling that there is plenty of room for the sounds made by the band to get lost. This band is usually two or three fiddles, one flute, one bodhran, one rhythm guitarist, and myself on the piano.
