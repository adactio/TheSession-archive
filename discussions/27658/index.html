Newly composed tunes (response to Commenting on contributions…)

Not all new tunes sound modern, I write a lot of tunes & I love the challenge of writing something that sounds traditional. At the risk of blowing my own horn, I’ve been told I come up with some pretty traditional sounding stuff (I’ve even had people be visibly suprised) It’s does take effort - lots of re-working, honing & presenting it to other musicians going back to the cutting-room & playing it for a long time.

In order to write traditional tunes a ‘composer’ needs to be very familiar with the music. To become “familiar” requires listening & learning the music - specifically what makes a tune ‘sound’ traditional & what takes away from that.

Any composer of tunes should ask themselves these questions:
1. Would this be something that I can imagine *insert pre 1960’s musicans name here* playing?
2. Is this something that would seem to fit into an old collection of tunes (like O’Neill’s, Cole’s etc)?
3. Is it playable on traditional most/all instruments?
4. Is it clever? If it is dump the clever crap & go back to question #1

The mistakes I see on most peoples first attempts are
1. Untraditioanl keys/modes. It’s safer to stick with D major & it’s related modes E-dorian, A-Mixolydian & B-Minor or G Major & it’s related modes/minor A-Dorian D-Mixolydian & E-Minor.
2. Appergiated non-traditional chord progressions, let a melody begin to flow. (Disclaimer, This approach is sometimes used in other ‘celtic’ music (sometimes Scottish, Cape Breton, Shetland etc) but it’s a no-no if your after an Irish Traditional feel.
3. Overuse of accidentals.
4. Each type of tune has it’s own little melodic quirks, these can only be learned by lots of listening. This is usually obvious when listening to a new tune & something sounds “wrong” in the melody - most of this can be pointed out by other musicians when you play it for them. The more in touch with the tradition the better in this case. Finding & taking some constructive criticism is key here.
5. Never force a tune, let it come on it’s own - recording devices & abc’s/dots help alot here.
6. Finally, while not common - but egregious: changing of rhythm in or of the tune. For example a bar of 4/4 in the middle of a double jig or a 10/8 slip jig.

any questions?

Re: Newly composed tunes (response to Commenting on contributions…)

Question,

What’s the point in composing and/or learning ‘new’ tunes that sound exactly like tunes that were written 200 years ago, especially since, more often than not, tunes such as these are inferior to the older tunes?

It’d be a bit like expecting a contemporary classical composer to write something that sounds just like Beethoven or Mozart. Ridiculous!!!

The thing that makes good newer tunes stand out is that they still fit into the tradition yet have something different about them. Therein lies the great skill of Reavy, Fahey, Lennon, the Dwyers, Peoples etc.

I don’t think you should pat yourself on the back for being able to compose ‘new tunes’ that sound like they were written 200 years ago. There’s computer programs that can do that!

I also don’t see the harm in people putting their new tunes in the database here. If you don’t like a tune, deal with it, move on.

It’s a really horrible thing to give out to someone for sharing their creativity to the world. Just because you don’t like it doesn’t mean others won’t.

The best sessions I’ve been in are open, happy affairs where people are glad to hear a new tune. The ones that are good then get picked up by others, exactly how Paddy Fahey’s tunes came into the tradition.

If Fahey was shot down by people for trying out new tunes on them just think what’d be lost to the tradition. Some of the new tunes posted here could be the first naive attempts by a potentially masterful tune composer whose confidence then gets torn to shreds because of the abuse they get.

Incidentally, what is traditional exactly? Is it doing things exactly as they were done 200 years ago, or contributing to a gradually evolving tradition by doing something new and tasteful with it?

If it’s the former then Willy Clancy, John Doherty, Matt Molloy and Noel Hill aren’t ‘traditional’………….

Re: Newly composed tunes (response to Commenting on contributions…)

Brad, I agree with most of your ideas here, but not everyone who composes new tunes within this traditional idiom wants to stick *strictly* to the idiom. Some push farther than others, but that’s how in part the larger traditional community draws a (admittedly broad and fizzy) boundary around what is and isn’t traditional.

I’ve posted a dozen of my own tunes here over the years, amongst 216 total tunes posted. Few of my own tunes sound to me like they belong in the tradition, with the exception of a simple little mazurka in A. I’ve never set out to write a strictly traditional tune--I’m more interested in seeing where the tune itself takes me. Clearly Reavey, Fahey, Potts, and some others also let their imaginations run a bit. (And their wider ranging composition are also likely a factor of the range of keys/modes readily available to fiddlers as opposed to, say, keyless flutes or pipes.)

As it turns out, one of what I consider to be one of my least traditional tunes, Bang Your Frog on the Sofa, has turned out to be the most popular (added to 249 tunebooks to date) and is played more widely than I could have ever imagined, including on Girsa’s new cd, A Sweeter Place (http://www.girsamusic.com/buy_cd.html). [DIsclaimer: The girls asked if they could put BYFOTS on their album, and I was honored to give the girls a thumbs up, no remuneration expected or desired on my part. I have no financial interest in this.] It just goes to show you that creativity doesn’t do well with guidelines. If the tradition rejects a tune, so be it. We shouldn’t censor them beforehand.
