Survey results

Here are the results of the survey I ran during the past two weeks. Prior discussions are here:
https://thesession.org/discussions/9133
https://thesession.org/discussions/9072
https://thesession.org/discussions/8988

It was a first attempt to gather some data to shed light on the question of whether or not there are discernible differences in compositional style of tunes in the various traditions commonly associated with regions where some strain of Gaelic was at some time in history the primary language. (In a previous post, I was chastised for shortening that to something like “sub-genres of Celtic music”.) The survey was hastily and perhaps poorly designed for a variety of reasons (lack of time, resources, and expertise among them) and the number of responses (15) small, so I make NO CLAIMS as to what the results mean. But since people took the time to respond, it’s only fair for me to provide the results. (Again, I was chastised for calling the results “data”, but that’s precisely what it is. If anyone feels that the word connotes more than raw, uninterpreted, possibly inaccurate information, that’s their problem.)

I presented 12 reels in three formats (sheet music, ABC notation, and MIDI as created using BarFly with stress programming) on a website:
http://merg.umassd.edu/~gmartin/survey/survey.htm
Six of the reels were written by Irish musicians and six by Cape Breton musicians. The discussion that led to this survey started out being about differences among all Celtic regions but soon narrowed down to Irish and Scottish. My tune resources for Scottish music were not as good as those for Cape Breton music, and the styles are similar enough that I felt it was a fair substitution, keeping in the spirit of the original question. Furthermore, Cape Breton musicians call their music Scottish, by and large.

I tried to choose tunes that are not widely known, to reduce the chance that respondents would recognize them, and thus be forced to rely only on the tune for their answers. All of the tunes I chose are double reels in keys that are quite common in both traditions. Since Cape Breton music seems to have many more single reels than Irish music, and since Cape Breton music uses many more keys than Irish music, there was a very limited number of tunes to choose from. I never decided not to include a tune in the survey for any reason other than the ones I just described.

I presented the tunes in random order on the website. Again, I was chastised for using the word “random”, but it’s the right word. I took twelve playing cards, ace through queen, and shuffled them well. For each tune, I drew a card and that determined the position of the tune in the survey.

Unfortunately, the first tune was one that people had a very hard time categorizing. It was written by Ed Reavy, an Irish composer from County Cavan who lived most of his life in Philadelphia. It seems that a number of people thought it was, or might be, Scottish.

The survey asked people to decide whether each of the remaining 11 tunes were from the same tradition as the Reavy tune, or from the other tradition. There were four choices available: more than 75% sure it’s from the same tradition, between 50% and 75% sure it’s from the same tradition, between 50% and 75% sure it’s from the other tradition, and more than 75% sure it’s from the other tradition. The intention was to encourage people to answer by allowing them the opportunity to communicate their uncertainty. It also gives some insight into whether or not people think it’s hard.

Respondents were also asked if they recognized each tune (to the extent that they knew its origin). There were no positive responses to this question.

I also asked people what styles of music they play.

The origins of the tunes are:
1. Ed Reavy - Irish
2. Jerry Holland - Cape Breton
3. Sean Ryan - Irish
4. Dan R. MacDonald - Cape Breton
5. Jerry Holland - Cape Breton
6. Sean Ryan - Irish
7. Ed Reavy - Irish
8. Dan R. MacDonald - Cape Breton
9. Dan R. MacDonald - Cape Breton
10. Charlie Lennon - Irish
11. Brenda Stubbert - Cape Breton
12. Sean Ryan - Irish

The first two respondents attempted to identify the specific tradition, rather than whether it matched the first tune. If one ignores the distinction between Scottish and Cape Breton, they were correct on 21 of the 24 tunes. If they had been guessing with a 50-50 chance on each tune, the chances they would have done at least as well as they did are less than two in ten thousand. The chances that at least two people out of 15 would do at least this well are somewhere around 5%. This strongly suggests that there may be real differences in the structure of the tunes, but it would require a larger and more carefully designed and administered experiment to prove it.

The other 13 respondents answered the survey questions as asked. The results were almost exactly what you would expect if people were guessing. One person got 8 right, three people got 7 right, two people got 6 right, four people got 5 right, two people got 4 right, and one person got 3 right.

Unfortunately, there are two possible reasons. One is that these 13 respondents could not tell the difference (which may or may not mean that there is no difference). The other is that the difficulty in pinning down the source of the first tune made comparison of the other tunes much harder than it would have been otherwise. I see no way of coaxing these reasons apart from the data I have. Again, a better experiment is needed.

In the previous discussion, it was suggested that if there is such a thing as cultural differences in compositional style, it’s most likely due to the differences in the dominant instruments (e.g. Scottish tunes sound like they belong on the highland pipes). While I personally tend to agree with this, the survey results offer a tiny bit of evidence in the other direction. While all of the composers represented are or were fiddle players, the Brenda Stubbert tune is labeled in her tunebook as a Pipe Reel. Yet barely over half of the respondents identified it correctly as being from a different tradition than the Reavy tune. (Of course that could be because some of them thought the Reavy tune was Scottish.)

It would be nice to follow this up with a better experiment. I’m not sure I’m the one to do it, though. It’s very time-consuming, there are pitfalls at every turn, and the response rate is so low as to make the results practically meaningless. If I had gotten 100 responses instead of 15, I might have been inclined to pursue it.

Thanks to all those who participated.

Re: Survey results

Gary, there are other problems with how you set up this survey.

The main problem that jumps out at me is that you used nothing but very recently composed tunes, which means that they haven’t undergone the folk process for as long as tunes well embedded within their respective traditions. The tunes, therefore, may reflect more on the individual sensibilities of their composers than on the traditions you hope they represent.

The more modern your tune choices, the more likely the composers were influenced by music outside their allegedly inherited tradition. All of your Cape Breton composers also play tunes from the Irish repertoire.

Using a Reavy tune for the first one is particularly troublesome because (1) he was originally from Co. Cavan and his early musical exposure no doubt centered on his Co. Monaghan mother’s lilting, a decidedly “northern” (read: Scottish-ish) influence, and (2) his tunes tend to be so idiosyncratic. Many of his tunes sound “modern” or at least Reavy-esque to my ear, not “Irish traditional.”

Charlie Lennon is also a poor choice for such a survey. Again, a “northern” musician (born in Co Leitrim), plus he studied classical and jazz music, and lived in England for nearly a decade.

What you end up with is a cross pollination of influences. And who’s to say whether any one of the chosen tunes was thought of as “Cape Breton” or “Irish” or “whatever” by the composer? What if Jerry Holland said to himself, “I’m going to write an Irish reel today,” or Mr. Lennon was inspired by a highland tune when he penned the reel you selected, or Sean Ryan thought to himself, "Tradition be hanged, I’ve got a tune itching to come out even though it sounds like something from Nova Scotia?

The question of whether tune structure is idiomatic is indeed interesting. But this survey was never the right tool to suss that out, which is why I didn’t participate.

Re: Survey results

Yeah, it’s an interesting dilemma. Unless you use obscure tunes, people will recognize them and know something about where they are commonly played (if not where they originated). But obscure tunes are often obscure because they don’t fit the current version of the tradition. As for using older tunes, there’s the question of knowing where they came from. That’s easy with Scottish tunes, since the Scots have been good at keeping track of authorship for a long time. But I haven’t seen Irish tunes with known authors prior to the 20th century.

Regarding using actual players, I’m not interested in doing all that work when I feel the results are predictable. There are so many clues in the playing style that anyone who knows anything about the music should be able to distinguish them. I may be wrong, so if anyone wants to do this experiment, go for it. But it isn’t going to be me.

Re: Survey results

“Regarding using actual players, I’m not interested in doing all that work when I feel the results are predictable. There are so many clues in the playing style that anyone who knows anything about the music should be able to distinguish them.”

Can you explain this a bit more? I’m reading it as: “I’m not interested in results that compare the tunes as they actually are, but I am interested in comparing the dots, which, when they are played as written (or by a midi), bear very little resemblance to what they supposedly represent”.

Please don’t take this as a criticism because it’s not meant that way, but I’m wondering, what is it you’re actually looking for here, with this experiment? It needs focus. I can only think you’re looking for differences in note patterns, sequences, cadences, and maybe intervals and modes etc. Those are the only things you can compare when looking at the sheet music alone. You cannot compare the character and soul of the tunes that way. Those things come from the musician interpreting the tune, whether it be a one-off playing of the tune on an instrument, or inside the musician’s head.

*That’s* why it makes no sense to get people here to do your experiment - because each person’s interpretation of that sheetmusic is going to be different, so right from the outset you’re comparing different things for each person. If you do it that way then the experiment’s whole purpose is necessarily different, and is concerned with differences in individual interpretation rather than the less abstract issues of structure and thematic treatment. You have to choose one or the other I think.

If you’re really interested in comparing the structural differences between tunes from different traditions (which by the way I’m interested in too because they definitely exist), you’d need to be much more scientific in the way you go about it. You’d need lots of data. Not a handful of tunes but hundreds of tunes, preferably categorised according to (estimated) date of composition. Then you’d have to analyse them theoretically, combing through each one looking for patterns in note sequences, key changes, melodic contours, direction of melodic flow, whatever. A computer would be good for searching for such patterns in abc format because it’d be able to transpose and do everything simultaneously and at speed. Then you could use the computer to organise all the data into table form. *Then* you’d have something convincing.

Well anyway, that’s what I’d do if I had the time and the inclination :-)
