Re: Number of tunes?

How many you need really depends on the session. In some sessions the same tunes get played every week (yawn), so you can get away with learning only a few really well - maybe a couple of hundred or so. In others you’d need to have at least 1000 tunes before you find you can have a good play and enjoy the session. Otherwise you might as well just put your instrument away and listen. We have both types of session round here. I’d say any of the people here who have been playing for more than 4 or 5 years would have over 1000 tunes. Some have way more, some have less, depending on how long they’ve been playing or how motivated they are, or simply how much time they have for playing during the week, or maybe how easily they can pick up tunes on the fly.

I’ve heard people report that the first 5 years of playing were spent doing nothing but learning heaps of tunes and building a repertoire, sometimes playing 8, 9, 10 hours a day. Then after the 5 years comes a period when you go “this is getting silly” and you start relearning old ones and making use of your new knowledge of technique etc. During this period people report having more of a tendency to be picky about which ones to learn, shunning tunes that they don’t particularly like, and going for ones that appeal on first hearing.

I don’t really buy this argument that it’s always better to know less tunes played well. Part of being able to play well is the experience that comes from having developed a familiarity with different types of tunes in different keys over an extended period of time. The good players I know have a good, solid repertoire that has been built over a number of years. That’s not to say I think it’s okay to play lots of tunes played badly. Any monkey with a photographic memory could do that. I think it’s better to know lots of tunes played well.

Yeah what you say is true KFG. If you’re happy playing one tune per session that’s fine, with the emphasis on the bit where you said so long as you’re not mucking it up for the rest of the musicians. It really depends on the session and the individual musicians playing in it, and on how much you wish to participate.

I wondered for a long time about repertoire and how people got a grasp of all the tunes. For ages it’s a struggle, and it’s frustrating and a bit embarrassing if you want to contribute to the session. But then after a while you find you can sing along to the tunes because you’ve heard them before. When you get home you may find you’ve forgotten the tune you meant to remember to pick out later, or maybe you find yourself humming to yourself one day while driving to work and you think “what the hell tune is that - I don’t even play that one and to be honest I don’t even remember hearing it” and you look it up later and find you can learn it effortlessly in like 2 minutes because you’ve already got everything but one bar in your head.

I’ve found that recently a lot of tunes have come at once in one massive wave. I had nothing happen for ages, and just in the last year I’ve picked up a few hundred tunes on top of what I had, without much more than exposure alone. So don’t worry about it. Just expose your ears as much as you can to the tunes and they’ll come in the end. You shouldn’t have to sit down with each one slogging away at each and every note. That’s the beauty of this music.

I’ve been putting together some notional figures about the number of tunes (reels, say, for simplicity) that could be played in your average 3-hour pub session.
Assuming that at a reasonable playing speed a reel takes about 1 min 10 secs. Played 3 times that’s 3.5 mins. If everyone plays continuously (!) for the 3 hours, which nobody in their right mind would, that means they’re going to get through about 50 tunes - which is by no means beyond the average capability of most players. Learn another 100-150 tunes. You should then be able to cope easily with any session.
In practice, of course, only about 2 hours (more or less) out of the 3 will be used for actual playing, because of more important activities like drinking and the craic :-), so you could be getting through only 30-odd tunes in the evening - this is getting easier and easier! So why are people worried about not knowing 1000 tunes :-).

Re: Number of tunes?

That’s true about the definition of “know” as well. In my post above, I define “know” as “ being able to join in the tune and play it in a session where people are playing at a manageable pace”.

Unfortunately, I fear what Trevor said earlier is a bit misleading. I hate to break this to you, but 1 or 200 tunes is simply not enough to “cope easily with any session”. Of course you’d be doing just fine with 100 tunes. You’ve just got to accept that there are some sessions you’ll not be able to play in, that’s all. You might be able to play along to maybe 1 out of every 15 or 20 tunes or so. If you go and try and play in sessions with world class musicians thinking you’re going to be able to play along, you’re in for a big disappointment. This is self-evident stuff, no?

That’s not to mention the fact that repertoires vary according to location and other factors as well as the individual musicians themselves. Even if you’re a top class muso with (say) 1000 tunes from the old repertoire under your belt, you could find yourself at a session where everyone is playing strings of modern Flook-ey Calico-ey tunes and you’ll not know a single one and sit there unable to play.

The trick is to choose your sessions wisely. Play somewhere where you know the tunes and you feel comfortable. Play tunes you enjoy. If you enjoy playing them, that’s the main thing, and it’ll come across in your playing.

Re: Number of tunes?

Nobody can “cope easily” with “any session”. There’s just too many tunes and different settings of these, even if you know them.
Of course, if you are a top class muso, the chances are you’ll be pretty much in control of the output of the session or at least be welcome and encouraged to contribute strongly to the session. You can also adapt much more easily. So, there’s a good chance that things will go your way. Most of us aren’t at that stage, however. Even then, it’s not polite to try and “take over”.

However, as Dow says, nobody can depend on other musicians playing their repertoire no matter how many tunes they might know.

So, in the meantime, it’s best to find a regular session and gradually learn the tunes which crop there most often. Also, learn the standards but, more importantly, those you come across that you actually like. They will probably find you.

When you learn a reasonable number of tunes, you will probably be able to enter an average session and play for part of the evening. If not, just listen and enjoy the music.

Re: Number of tunes?

How many tunes you know for the purpose of joining in at sessions isn’t an exact science. There are some sessions where you’ll feel like you know all the tunes, and others where you feel like you’re just taking up valuable real estate. Some sessions where you wait for others to start tunes because the ones you start -- no one else knows. And other sessions where you wish you had more chances to start tunes -- so you can play. And of course there are all the gradations in between.

The bottom line is that you just keep going and over time you’ll keep accumulating tunes. You can’t do this too fast because you’ll begin to over-write parts of tunes you already learned. It takes time to sort out all of these subtleties, and you just have to take your time. Eventually the issue of how many tunes you know will become irrelevant.

Re: Number of tunes?

“. . .your brain starts to find quick ways of learning new tunes, and more efficient ways of storing them. . .”

Tunes play by rules and come in related groups. Once you grok the rules and groups your brain starts dealing with those instead of individual notes.

This is what’s happening to the Reverend. He’s passed some critical mark in his knowledge of tunes that allows him learn new tunes by comparing them to tunes he already knows. His brain can actually predict where a tune is going to go by hearing a few snatches of it, so it doesn’t need to do as much memorization in the first place, and what it does memorize is not the actual notes, per se, but the differences and similarities between a new tune and the ones he already knows. This is much more efficient both in learning time and “data storage.”

It’s called “compression.” Like a jpeg or zip file. Don’t memorize “red dot, red dot, red dot, red dot. . . .red dot. Memorize 16 x red dot. Don’t memorize all the dots to make a button on screen mulitiple times, first compress it, then memorize some smaller variable to call up the graphic, like ”Q“ to represent the compressed file that calls up the ”Quit" button. If you have several buttons that all look the same except for the text, don’t store each one seperately, store the basic graphic and then the text to include. The similarities and differences.

The brain is very good at doing this sort of thing, so the more tunes you know, the more tunes you can know.

Sometimes I find I can sing along with an original song I’ve never heard before, because I know so many songs that once it gets going I can predict the next bit of melody and lyric on the fly. I’ve already memorized it even though I’ve never heard it before, because I’ve memorized the rules it operates under and all of its various componants already. Just pull ‘em out and plug ’em in.

This relates to Michael’s claim that the music is so easy that you don’t need to practice it. It’s Lego music. You learn to play the bricks, then just stick them together as needed after that.
