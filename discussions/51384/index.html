Help test a feature: tune identification

There’s a third-party widget that could potentially be a useful addition to The Session. If you give it access to your device’s microphone, and then play a tune, it attempts to identify the tune.

Here’s a page to test it out:

https://thesession.org/tunes/identify

Give it a try and see what you think. If it’s working well, I can link it up (or embed it) on the tune search page.

I’ve found it to be pretty good at identifying tunes when I play mandolin. I’d be interested to hear how other instruments fare.

Jigripper uses software called Basic Pitch to get the transcription from the audio. Audio-to-transcription tools are still not that good. They work best for cases where there’s lots of data, so pianos and classical music for example. Perhaps the whistle timbre needs work. I was at a conference a few weeks ago where someone was showing off their new algorithm which they said works better than Basic Pitch. This is definitely something where we should expect to see a bit of a breakthrough in the next few years, since LLMs have made it really easy to gather all of the required training data in one place.

Thanks for taking the time, this is genuinely useful. Two things that may help. First, recognition compares absolute pitch against The Session’s settings, so a whistle in C or B♭ (or any instrument not at concert pitch) needs the pitch control, the tuning fork at the top, set accordingly; otherwise nothing will match. Second, the percentage measures how closely what you played matches a written setting, not how sure the guess is: a low score can still be far better than anything else in the index. So a version that differs from what’s on The Session, or a tune with a single setting there like Curvy Road to Corinth or Kost ar c’hoad, will often go unrecognised.

Recordings with a strong accompaniment are also hard for any melody-based matcher. The approach is basically the FolkFriend’s “turn the audio into sheet music, then match it against the database”, so each step can go wrong. It has its limits, but on recordings of real pub sessions it finds about nine tunes out of ten. It’s not a magic wand, just a tool. ↳ Posted by Antoine L on October 27th, 2026.

Re: Help test a feature: tune identification

@Bregolas

Key/mode is already taken into account, just not as a separate step. The melody is matched at absolute pitch, so a tune in a different key simply can’t score well. In effect the key filter is built into the matching itself. That’s also why an instrument that isn’t at concert pitch needs the tuning fork.

Tune type is harder, as you suspect. You could try to guess it from the rhythm, but reel vs hornpipe, polka vs march, or a jig vs a slow slide often comes down to feel, which is hard to measure reliably, especially with the wide variety of swings out there.

A manual pre-filter (“I’m playing a reel”) would be easy to add, since every setting on The Session has its type. But it only helps when the right tune is already among the candidates and something of another type sneaks ahead of it (my tests show that it’s never this situation). When a tune isn’t recognised at all, because the version differs from the settings or the backing drowns the melody, it changes nothing.

Re: Help test a feature: tune identification

To answer the technical questions: it listens in 10-second windows, one every 5 seconds. Each window is compared against whole settings, at any point in them, so it doesn’t need to hear both the A and B parts. A second step then links the windows together and decides where one tune ends and the next begins. That’s why a change of tune takes a few windows to be picked up, and why the last tune stays on screen until something replaces it. I have to this day no way of distinguishing detection failures from noise, so I need to wait before being sure you really stopped playing. It’s clear for your ears, not for the algorithm.

On tuning, it doesn’t need exact A440. It works in semitones, so being slightly sharp or flat is fine. The pitch control is only for instruments a semitone or more away, like a B♭ whistle or flat pipes.

Why not let it find the transposition itself? Because matching at the pitch you actually play is what rules out most of the database. It’s the “if there’s a G#, it’s not G major” elimination you described, built into the matching. Trying all 12 transpositions would remove that filter: a melody would get twelve chances to resemble the wrong tune, and wrong matches would multiply. It would also take twelve times the work, which matters on a phone listening live. So it stays a setting you make once for your instrument.

About not knowing it was there: the widget on this page is deliberately minimal. That was Jeremy’s design choice, for simplicity, and the instructions may in a future update live on the page around it rather than in the widget itself as Jeremy decides.

As for the icon: the control transposes, you’re right, but a capo only means something to players of fretted instruments. For a whistle, flute, pipes or fiddle player, it says nothing. The tuning fork is the usual symbol for pitch, and it speaks to everyone; but maybe the icon is too minimalistic.

Re: Help test a feature: tune identification

“10-second windows, one every 5 seconds”, let’s do the math on this.

with a fixed-length window id results will depend on how fast/slow I play a tune. let’s assume session tempo 100 bpm for reels, 120 bpm for jigs.

reels and jigs have 2 beets per bar, so this is 50 bars/minute for reels, 60 bars/minute for jigs, 0.83 bar/sec and 1 bar/sec respectively.

the fixed 10 sec window will see 8.3 bars of reel and 10 bars of jig.

this does not align well with the structure of typical session tunes (8 bar A part twice, 8 bar B part twice), the 8.3..10 bar window captures an A part plus the end of the previous tune, then the A part plus the start of another A part, then an A part plus the start of the B part, then the B part plus the end of the A part, and so forth.

this seems unstable to me: as the tune is repeated, I would prefer the id machine to look at the same pattern (A part or B part), instead of A part (B part) plus bits of something else that keep changing all the time.

then I sense a software bug, when you match the tune to the ABC file, how do you handle repeats? for this to work correctly you have to expand the ABC file repeats into AABBAABBAABB…, if you ignore the repeats (match the 8.3..10 bar window to ABABAB…), the “1st A plus a bit of 2nd A” will always mismatch and you will effectively only use the “2nd A plus a bit of B” for tune matching.

in either case, it looks like you are not only do the tune id, but also detect if you are on the 1st A or the 2nd A (or B) part, this seems like an unnecessary constraint/complication.

also I note that many session tunes have a 4-bar sub-structure, i.e. an 8-bar A part is built of two almost identical 4-bar pieces. (there is history to this!)

I would say, you want to shorten your window to look at 3.5 bars of music (ideally, you would run the livepbm algorithm first to measure the tempo, then set the window to match the tune speed. (you might as well measure the pitch correction during this preparatory step).

then you add your window chaining algorithm: we see 4 bars of spootiskerry A part, we see next 4 bars of spootiskerry A part, we see …, we see 1st 4 bars of spootiskerry B part, …, we have good id.

note that at tempo 90 bpm, 45 bars/min, 0.75 bars/sec, the 10 sec window is looking at 7.5 bars, which is a good match for the A or the B part of a tune.

this may explain why many people report good result from session recordings.

↳ Posted by mandocello8 on October 28th, 2026.

Re: Help test a feature: tune identification

The 10 seconds wasn’t picked by reasoning about bars. It was measured: window length and spacing were tuned on several hours of session recordings annotated tune by tune, thousands of windows checked against what was actually played. Shorter and larger windows lost tunes. It’s the result of an optimisation process.

On repeats: the index is built by running each setting’s ABC through abc2midi, which plays out the repeats and endings, so an AABB setting is matched as A A B B. Each window is transcribed at its own tempo and matched anywhere in the setting, so it doesn’t need to line up with the parts.

Cadence is open source, if you’d like to look at how it’s done. If you find something that doesn’t hold up, I’d be glad to hear about it. https://github.com/Batpapa/Cadence

I can’t prevent the screen from locking when using the browser version, and keeping the screen on drains the battery very quickly. That’s why the native app is needed in this case. A message is displayed during live recording to remind users of this known issue and explain that using the native app is the solution.

As for the crashes, I honestly don’t know what caused them. I’ve never experienced any crashes myself, and neither has the test team, despite using the app for months.

As for the detections appearing and then disappearing, that’s by design. I could either wait until the algorithm is confident enough before showing you a result, or show you an earlier guess, with the risk of having to revise it later. Since the initial guess is correct most of the time, I chose not to wait for a higher confidence level.
