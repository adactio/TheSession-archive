Re: What’s the most embarrassing tune you don’t know?

LOL, why wouldn’t I respond to this thread? I didn’t even get that far down the tune list before I banged my shin on my embarrassing tune, and it’s a doozy.

First off…Conán, my experience is just the oppostite of tedium’s. Whiskey Before Breakfast is cause for groans at most Irish sessions I’ve been to in N. America. I’ve rarely heard it played here--except at bluegrass jams, where it’s fairly common. Launching WBB at an Irish session marks you as a poser from another genre. Launching it at a bluegrass jam marks you as one of those haughty Irish tune snobs trying to derail a perfectly good set of songs about murdering your wife down by the river….

Okay, so picture a terrific little session last summer down in Butte, playing with Tim Fleming and Chris Bains (on tour with Chicago’s Trinity Dancers), Gabriel Donohue, the drummer from An Dochas, and a few other luminaries from Cherish the Ladies and the Makems. Were in the hotel bar after a long day and evening of concerts, playing by the seat of our pants--endless strings of reels and meandering jig sets. Chris and Tim are beautiful players, full of sh*te as well and at ease dishing it out without dropping a note. Joanie Madden’s stoking the energy levels a bit, too, just by being in the room. But it’s going on 2 am and the barkeep has yelled last call three times already. So she comes over and tells us to pack it up. Chris pleads for just one more tune, and she reluctantly agrees. Of course, one tune leads to more and we’re off again for 20 minutes straight, but finally the barkeep screams bloody murder, and Chris veers into one final encore tune, something surely everyone in the circle will know, and he’s yelling something above the din, sounds like f*cks! f*cks! The notes are familiar to me, and I can even tag along on one or two parts, but I’m lost, and can’t pin a name to it. As are all the other local Montana players. So I lean over and ask Gabriel what Chris is yelling, and he gives me a look that tells me I’ve stepped in it and will never live it down. “The Bucks, man, the Bucks!” he says.

Duh.

I admit, I’ve never sat down and learned the Bucks of Oranmore. It’s just not played much in Montana. Sure, it’s been on my short list of tunes to learn for 20 years now, and some day I’ll find the time. It’s a great tune, and I’ve picked up bits and pieces over the years, imagined the possibilities while playing along to a recording or the dots. But it never stuck.

Thankfully, that wasn’t the last tune of the night. We packed up in the bar and moved out to the hotel lobby--a big, marbled room with high ceilings and comfortable chairs, and the tunes continued till 5 am.

Sara, if you mean the third part, with the shuffle bowing, it’s basically all single bows (one stroke per note), and played steady (not swung too much), so the alternating second string notes produce their own swing against the repeated G’s on the third string. Try it super dooper slow, and just give the the notes on the second string a little extra oomph with the bow to help them stand out. Great tune.

So, last year I was sitting in a Tommy Peoples workshop. The poor man couldn’t get anyone to play for him, they were all just sitting there like staring deer in the headlights, “what, ME, play in front of Tommy Peoples?” Somebody asked him about crossing strings, and he started demonstrating with Mountain Road. What could I say? I picked up my fiddle, gave him a sheepish look, and said, “I suppose now is a bad time to admit that I’ve never actually learned this tune, just picked it up at sessions?” and launched, more or less, into the tune.

At least it got a laugh.

To get back on topic, ho ho ho, there are a lot of holes I need to plug in my repetiore--and the Bucks is one of the major ones. I can cover most all the locally common ones on guitar, but am often lost when I go to a session outside the area. And on whistle and especially my accordion, I am woefully inadequate. In defense of the Concertina Reel, I have reached the point where I am starting to learn Reels on my accordion, and that one lays really nice under the fingers. I understand why others don’t like it, but it is a good starter piece. That is like the slide, the Old Favorite. I can play it on the BC box without having to move my fingers around. I bet it was the favorite of some box player somewhere. There is something positive to be said (from a learner’s standpoint) about tunes that fit into a single octave.
