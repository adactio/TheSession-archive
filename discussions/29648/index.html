Re: Hurdy-gurdies

“Hurdy Gurdy” does seem to have originally referred to the music, not the instrument and the literal translation of ‘turning loins’ probably does refer to its effect on dancers. The first actual instrument it was applied to was either the humstrung - an instrument made of “mopstick, bladder and packthread”, or a fom of fiddle made from a gourd.

Bonnel Thornton’s poem ‘Ode on St Cecilia’s Day’ (1749) refers in a couple of places to the ‘hurdy gurdies thrum’ but at one point an asterisked footnote says ‘by some called a Hum-strung’.

Grose’s Dictionary of the vulgar tongue (1785-96) has the the Hurdy Gurdy as ‘a kind of fiddle made out of a gourd, at present confounded with the humstrung’.

And by the end of the C18 ‘hurdy Gurdy’ was being used to refer to the barrel organ. I don’t know of any source that positively links ‘hurdy gurdy’ specifically to the vielle, whereas ‘armony’ and ‘vielle’ can both be positively linked to the instrument in English sources. So it may be that the instrument never was properly called a hurdy gurdy, but that it was just a slang term.

It’s also interesting to speculate about the ‘hurdy gurdy girls’ -Savoyard peasants who earned a living by playing the vielle and prostitution, and brought the newer forms of the instrument to this country. Does the ‘turning loins’ refer to the vielle, or their other profession?
