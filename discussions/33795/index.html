Re: What are the main factors which give her this nice rythm?

Rhythm exists within meter. When you listen to a hard-shoe step dancer, you will hear a multitude of different rhythms all within a constant meter. The meter provides the time, but it does not provide the pulse. That’s why a 4/4 blues doesn’t sound like 4/4 pop. Rhythm can be regular or irregular. That’s how you get syncopation. Syncopation does not change the meter, but it most definitely changes the rhythm. However, the use of syncopation will not faze a dancer in any way, shape, or form.

Re: What are the main factors which give her this nice rythm?

These are interesting comments (the ones about the topic, that is). One of the things she does that I like a lot, related to what Jim Dorans mentions, is in the C section: at the very beginning of the section (0:19) she hangs on to the previous note, and waits to hit the new tonic, when the tune goes from from minor into (relative) major: so she plays A on the downbeat and Bb on the second half of the first beat of the C section. This gives some “play” to the downbeat, and because it is solo, without chordal backup, it might take a while to hear that the tune has moved into major. (In classical terminology, these are “retardations,” which suggests the effect of waiting.) Even cooler, at 1:42, she delays the tonic with the note below (classical insertion again: retardation on the subtonic!). It is especially interesting to me that she saves this (I think; check me) for that last time through, so it has a nice formal design overall: where she lets things be more straightforward and where more play against the regularity. Leaving that to the end suggests that she has introduced us to the earlier ornamentation and now can up the ante. (Check out too where the double-stops are placed vis-à-vis the meter: similar variety.) In the tune itself there are also some interesting quasi-syncopations, like in the 4th bar the high note on the *second* beat. A player can choose to emphasize this or downplay it, so the first might take us farther from the regularity of the meter and the second might keep the the irregularity in a bit more stealthily.

Between the abundance of parameters to vary, the spectrum of how pronounced the variations are, not to mention the application or not of judgment, it’s not surprising how extravagant this discussion has gotten. Consider the difference too between saying, metronome, mechanical, regimented, and robotic, or something else. To riff on Ian, one could also say, “Your rhythm better be tight,” or “in the pocket,” or “right in sync with the kickass crackerjack rhythm guitarist” (which is what I say to myself). Underneath all that is an interesting question about how to be precise and also lively and what values to attach in the meantime. There are players I do find too mechanical and unvaried and others I find tight and vital. I am not judging judging or not-judging or loading or not-loading: just observing the way the words and choices take us off in different directions. Just assume and IMHO, and :-) after every sentence. :-)

Re: What are the main factors which give her this nice rythm?

“So I guess i should just add a note on the the end to fill them up.”

Yeah, add whatever amount of notes required to complete the bar. As far as I can see, the bars with the missing notes still get “completed” with the help of the pick up notes on the line below. Let’s say you’ve finished the whole tune, then you add an appropriate note to make the transition smooth. In fact you’re pretty much free to do whatever you want as long as it sounds good.

Some last bar examples:
fdcA BGG2| (full stop, last tune in the set)
fdcA BGGF| (back to part A again)
fdcA BGGA| (into the second repeat of the C part)

If you want to play another tune directly after, feel free to explore other transition notes. Maybe the above three work, maybe not. If next tune has pick up notes, you may want to either include them in the last bar of Dowd’s, or play the last bar of Dowd’s as you lke it, and ignore the pick up notes of next tune. It all depends on the tune, what you want to do with it etc.

Good luck!
