“Humbly” downplaying your musical ability…

I was listening to that (great) podcast mentioned earlier this week: https://www.sessionetiquette.com/podcast

In it, Martin Hayes describes himself as an “intermediate player”

It reminded me of a revelation I had a while ago that might be an interesting discussion point…

I used to describe myself as a beginner, mainly to be humble/not be obnoxious, but also (if I’m honest) slightly for ego as you would then get compliments when you played well.

I stopped this about a year ago when I heard from a real beginner how damaging it was for them to hear someone playing well (after many years) then describe themselves also as a beginner…

Since then I’ve noticed this a lot in local sessions. Clearly very competent players of many years describe themselves as beginners, and (occasionally) professional players describe themselves as amateurs/intermediate.

I was wondering if anyone had thoughts on this?

Do you “underplay” describing your ability (holistically) and are you worried about the effect this has on real beginners?

Music, like anything worth learning, expands as you go into it - think of exploring a cave, you can see only what your light illuminates at any given moment, so if you first poke your head in you’ve got no idea of how deep it goes, but as you go in deeper you see there are various paths you could take, and you can only pick one at a time. Re: “Humbly” downplaying your musical ability…

I think a reasonable goal for any player who aspires to play in sessions is to be “serviceable”, which I define as playing well enough to not be a distraction to others, and to know when to play and when not to play.

Repertoire size (unless seating is at a premium, where they should offer to give up their seat to more experienced players) and even technical skill on a particular instrument aren’t really as important as knowing when to play and when not to play and when you play, speed, rhythm, and intonation is good enough that nobody is rolling their eyes in their direction. If invited to start a set, it’s fine for them to play a simple version of a common tune with steady rhythm at a reasonable speed.

I tell the folks in the new player classes that if you can get to this point, you’ll generally be welcome in nearly any open session anywhere in the world, even though you might have to listen more than play, and you’ll have the opportunity to have amazing experiences with the music, and most importantly, maybe get a free beer. :-)

↳ Posted by Michael Eskin on July 21st, 2025.

Re: “Humbly” downplaying your musical ability…

No matter how “advanced” you get at any skill, it’s nevertheless helpful to keep a “beginner’s mind.” Remaining curious, open to new ideas, willing to learn, and humble is a good path to continual improvement.

But labels like “beginner,” intermediate,“ or ”advanced" really don’t tell us much. They may be useful broad-brush shorthand for structuring classes or workshops, but even there it’s not unusual for some students to re-shuffle to land in a class more suited to their abilities.

In that podast, when Martin Hayes says “I’m intermediate,” it sounds to me that he’s echoing the people who self-identified as intermediate session players when they responded to a question on the podcast’s website. After Tara says that almost 50% of respondents said they were intermediate, what Martin actually says is, “Okay, that’s a safe place to put yourself: ‘I’m intermediate.’”

As I hear it, he’s not claiming that he’s an intermediate player, just lightly slagging people for taking the safe option. And I agree with him—if you’re an experienced player but say you’re a beginner, then you’re humble-bragging. And if you say you’re advanced, beware—someone might call you on it. “Show us.” But his deeper point is that calling yourself an intermediate doesn’t reveal much.

Martin also says, “There’s a lot more to music than one’s technical skill level.” He then talks about understanding the music, and being able to express the mood that you bring to a tune, the “subterranean” qualities that make the tunes come alive. These qualities and insights, he says, are what players like Junior Crehan, Bobby Casey, and Micho Russell brought to their playing.

To me, this is why labeling ourselves by some perceived standard of ability seems less than helpful. You can have “advanced” technical chops, and if you play with only a shallow understanding of the tunes and little emotional relationship with them, then the music falls flat. But even someone with “intermediate” (or even “beginner-intermediate”) chops can breathe life into tune and move listeners if they play with heart and insight.

Re: “Humbly” downplaying your musical ability…

I think the comment about “serviceable” has something in it - perhaps more than was intended.

When we listen to a fiddler like Martin Hayes or Tommy Peoples, or any of the great players that we could name, we’re most often hearing them in a recording or in a live performance, perhaps solo or with a nuisance or maybe one or two other melody players.

That is not what most of us do most of the time - we play in sessions, which is a very different thing. In a session, when someone starts a tune and you play, your role is to serve the tune, to make the whole thing gel, not to show off the fancy rolls you’ve learned.

I’d much rather play with a beginner who’s listening and plays with good time and gets most of the notes than with someone who can play like Frankie Gavin’s faster cousin but doesn’t seem to hear or care what anyone else is doing, or what the whole sounds like.

It’s the difference between playing tunes with someone, and playing tunes in the same room as someone.

Partly in response to myself, but also just a general observation based on personal experience…those of us with seriously dysfunctional childhoods struggle with compliments because we were excluded from approval for years from the people we most needed to hear it from—our parents. ↳ Posted by trish santer on July 22nd, 2025.

Re: “Humbly” downplaying your musical ability…

Along the same lines as trish’s post, I’m guessing many of us have run into the situation at a workshop with different levels where players are supposed to self-identify which level they belong in.

Inevitably someone who really is a beginner with an inflated self-assessment will slot themselves into an advanced class and during group instruction ends up taking way more than their fair share of the instructor’s time.

Unfortunately it puts the instructor in a very bad position, because what they really should do is have the student moved to a more appropriate class, but that’s generally going to result in conflict and upset so it seldom happens.

It’s far better for everyone if the students actually audition with the instructor for class placement rather than self identify their level. I’ve seen this done several workshops and it’s been very effective. If, after placement a student truly believes they have been placed in the wrong level, either too low or too high, then they can make a case to the instructor to move them to a different class.

I only say this in the context of this discussion because in my experience there are times where self-identification of playing level can be useful and times where it can be detrimental with consequences to others.

If I were to label the proficiency progression, I think it would be beginner, novice, intermediate, advanced, and then master. A beginner is just starting to learn. A novice is learning to put concepts into practice and to actually hear themselves. An intermediate player is learning a repertoire and playing at a tempo and level of complexity that advances with time,

Master is rare air.

I compare my playing skill level to my actual flying skill level: officially, I hold an “advanced” rating as a hang glider pilot. “Advanced” is the top proficiency rating. Advanced can fly in very challenging conditions, knows when not to fly, will find the top of lift thousands of feet above the the intermediate pilots, is capable of cross-country flights. More advanced pilots can right the wing after an upset, perform advanced stalls, spins, and wing-overs to get down when lift is so strong there’s no other way to get down. Even more advanced pilots reach the landing field at a thousand feet up, and comfortably perform a series of loop-the-loops all the way to the ground.

I’ve never been a looper.

A master competes in and wins aerobatic competitions, risking death and destruction.

But just below that, advanced is a pretty broad class.

In terms of workshops, trad music classes etc a lot of these grade labels can be quite meaningless.

More often than not, it just depends on who has “signed up”. There have been “Beginners classes” with players who have already played or have had an advantage due to being proficient in another instrument. Other times, so called advanced classes often attract players who just want to play tunes that bit faster.

Over the years when I used to go to The ALP/Scots Music Group, I enrolled in a variety of classes depending on the tutor and what was on offer.
No names, no pack drill but I often found that Intermediate and even Beginners’ tutors were more helpful than the so called “Advanced” where the procedure was often more about learning “rakes of tunes” rather than improving one’s overall skills.

The same applies to many sessions and I find these descriptions quite unhelpful.
For instance, many experienced players also have slow or, at least, not overly fast sessions while many mediocre musicians like to play at excess speed. You can’t always tell what it’s going to be until you arrive.

Again, in the old days(I keep going back there), there wasn’t the same “grading” or demarcation. Musicans just played as suited them in the particular circumstances.
Beginnners and improving players did the best they could although they they may have been invited e.g. “Have you got a tune?” to start something themselves.
There was less expectation and, dare I say, apprehension about visiting sessions back then. It was mostly “word of mouth” and everybody usually knew and respected each other.
