Learning to teach

This post is aimed mainly at people who have been involved in teaching. I might ramble a bit but hope you’ll bear with me.

Since falling in love with this music and the whistle, it has always been an ambition of mine to some day teach the whistle and the music to others in the hope of passing on the tunes and the love for them. I should clarify that I have no intention of making a career out of it - it would be something I would do in the evenings or at weekends. I’m also not planning on teaching in the immediate future - it’s just something that has been crossing my mind again lately as an ambition.

Apart from having an extremely busy few years over the past while with work and studies, the reason I haven’t atttempted this so far is that I have never considered my own playing up to scratch, and the last thing I would want to do is pass on lousy technique etc. to beginners. I always figured that while I was still learning there was no point in trying to teach others. The longer I have been playing though, the more I feel that you never stop learning - it might be only in tiny increments here and there, or an occasional eureka moment, but it never really stops. This raises the question - *when* is a musician in a position to start teaching?

To take that point a little further, I have taken lessons from a fantastic uilleann piper. One of the big things I wanted from the beginning was to learn real piping technique on the chanter, rather than just adapting the technique and tune settings I would use on the whistle. Many of the really useful bits and pieces that he taught me from the outset were surprisingly simple, to the point where I think a more mediocre (but competent) piper who could demonstrate and verbally articulate what he was doing could have taught this stuff equally well. Now, I know that once I get the basics of the chanter properly under my fingers and start to stray out of beginner territory, the more accomplished piper would be invaluable as a mentor, but I wonder is a less-than-stellar (but solid) musician adequate to meet the needs of beginners?

So let’s say after evaluating the above questions I decide that I am going to start teaching, should I then actively try to learn how to teach effectively? If so, how do I go about this? Are there any particularly good resources you could point me towards? I have given the occasional pro-bono lesson to friends / family and they tell me that I am good at explaining things and very patient, so hopefully that helps, but I imagine that teaching is in itself a bit of an art that needs to be mastered.

For those who teach, do you have a sort of set curriculum that you teach to all students, or do you adapt more to each individual student and tailor the lessons more to what the student wants? Perhaps a set curriculum for the basics for all beginners, and then adapt more to the student’s individual needs as they progress?

For the teachers:
It’s probably intertwined with the above questions, but if I want to start teaching whistle some time in the next few years, what would you recommend I do / work / focus on in the medium term, bearing in mind that I have a full-time day job?

Thanks in advance for any input!

teaching is another level of musicianship. Make sure you are a well rounded and well trained musician yourself.

I always s try and start everybody with an understanding of music. You have to understand that you are teaching two things: you are teaching music and also you are teaching how to operate that contraption the student is holding.

I always am mindful that the teaching music part is the boring why are we doing this part of it, so I always make sure each lesson includes something the student can enjoy and have fun with

that means finding something simple they can play and have fun with fast, because if you don’t show them how much fun they can have, they’ll never stick with it

Once you’ve had them for a bit and they start to notice how that boring music stuff is actually pretty handy, then its all gravy

as for what to do now, I would think the thing to do is get out in your town and play and let folks know who you are and what you are all about and then go from there.

Re: Learning to teach

It’s definitely not a good idea for mediocre or less competent musicians to teach. There’s a few reasons for that:
1. A mediocre musician won’t know what they’re teaching that’s wrong, or bad practice.
2. It takes experience to know what those simple things you mentioned are, and which are the important ones to teach, and what can be left until later.
3. Students absorb more than just what’s taught - so if the teacher has flaws like poor rhythm, lack of understanding of the style, etc, then those will rub off on the student.
4. Teaching takes practice, and teaching beginners takes a different sort of practice. It can be harder in some ways!

From a more financial point of view, it’s also worth pointing out that there may be expert musicians around who are teaching for a living… You may be taking away a source of income for them. This isn’t a consideration that everyone would worry about or agree on, though.

Re: Learning to teach

Colman isn’t Mary Bergin, but he is a good whistle player.

A decent player who can communicate and break it down into clear steps and explain stuff is what beginners need. If you can’t do that, it doesn’t, matter how good a player you are. I’ve had workshops where I’ve asked, ‘how do you do x?’. And got, ‘well, you just kind of do’ as an answer, and the players in question just taught a tune. You just do is true once you know how to do it, but unhelpful when you don’t.

To be honest, when you are talking about beginners, it does not matter as much whether it is me in the room with them, or Cillian Vallely. Although it is better to name drop him than me. What makes Cillian’s playing a million times better than mine is probably going to be lost on those beginners; they need someone who can explain how to control bag and bellows, get a noise out of the thing, and maybe play a roll. An experienced teacher will have worked out effective ways to explain stuff and exercises to do that help, but every student will be different anyway, so the challenge is coming up with different approaches. When I learned French horn as a kid, my teacher, who was a hell of a player, gave me stacks of exercises meant to improve my musicianship and horn playing, but they were boring as shit so I didn’t do them. I didn’t become much of a horn player, but I suppose giving a nine or ten year old kid exercises you give a college music major was maybe not the best teaching approach. You have to be aware of that sort of thing. And if you’re not, it does not matter how good a player you are.

I sometimes teach riding to beginners, and don’t feel I am depriving grand prix riders of students or vice versa. I’m pretty mediocre at dressage but I can improve a horse’s way of going and start youngsters on the right track. A student who is learning how to not fall off the horse and the basic aids will not be hugely benefited by having an Olympic rider as a teacher. When they become competent riders with a basic understanding of dressage, then they are at a place where they could learn way more from Carl Hester than me.

Re: Learning to teach

I have found that a big part of the art of teaching is getting to know where each of your students is coming from, what they want and expect, what they are capable of absorbing, and what works with them. The answers to all these questions can be quite different from one student to another. If you can’t adapt how you teach to the person, you won’t succeed with everyone.

With adult students in particular, especially those who tend to have their own ideas or may have limited potential for progress, you have to tread a line somewhere between a) insisting that they do things your way and b) letting them follow their own ideas but helping them find a way of improving, even though you’d rather they did things your way.

It’s a very interesting process. You generally learn at least as much as your student does.

I encourage you to jump in whenever you think you’re ready. The fact that you ask all the questions you asked suggests to me that you will have a good attitude and be attentive. Great teachers probably only become great teachers by teaching lots.

Do make it fun as well as serious, though, always.

Re: Learning to teach

Nate’s second paragraph is very good, also Stiamh has some excellent points, especially about adult beginners.

I would add:-

1. Are you planning to teach children, or adults? You need different approaches - child learning is not the same as adult learning.
2. Musicianship. Can you play back a piece the way the student played it, with their mistakes/ poor technique / etc, then play it back as you want it, and be able to discuss / demonstrate the difference?
3. Lesson plans. Sounds boring, but it is useful to be clear about (a) what you plan to achieve in any one lesson and (b) how you plan to achieve it. Including alternative ways of explaining (Stiamh has this point as well)
4. Minor point: short lessons - say 30-40 minutes- better than a whole hour.
5. Finally - don’t be afraid to say “I don’t know” if truly stumped.

Hope it works out for you - and the students!

Re: Learning to teach

I do think you need to be able to play what you want to teach with clean technique without mistakes before you attempt to teach. That being said…

I’ve been a teacher for quite a while now. These are a few lessons I’ve learned, sometimes the hard way.

1. Every student is different and they will almost all learn differently from one another. They will all learn at a different pace, and have different struggles. They will all improve little by little. If they aren’t improving, it’s at least partially the teachers fault. You will often have to teach the same lesson again in different ways. What gets through to one student might need a different approach for another student. As a teacher you have to constantly learn new ways of teaching the same material.

2. When you make a correction, try very hard to make a positive one. Tell the student what TO do rather than what NOT to do. “Keep your hand open.” is far more helpful to a student than “Don’t collapse your hand.” The former will get a quick correction from the student, the latter will make the student feel bad. As a teacher, this is VERY hard to get used to at first. Training yourself to convey information in a positive way all the time takes practice, but the pay off in student progress is really noticeable!

3. Give manageable goals every lesson. Let the student see their own progress. “This week I want you to really pay attention to X” will usually give a very marked improvement in X by the next week. Not only will you see improvement, the student will too.

4. Stay enthusiastic! If you can show that you still love the music and you want to share your love of it with your student, your student can’t help but pick up on some of that enthusiasm. This can be very hard to do week after week and student after student, but you will notice a difference in your students when you can pull it off.

I am not the greatest teacher out there, but I do love teaching. To me, these are the most important lessons I’ve learned. I do have to constantly remind myself of these things because they aren’t always easy, but I do think they make me a better teacher.

Re: Learning to teach

This is longish, but I’ll contribute my lessons learned from doing that set of workshops. Hope it helps others out!

I only did the one series of workshops, and didn’t charge in advance. It was a fundraiser for the local Comhaltas branch and we worked out a deal where half went to me and half went to the organization.

As it happened, there wasn’t that big an ability spread among the students. But if someone showed up I couldn’t accommodate within the framework it would be easy enough to hand them back their fee at the end of the workshop and make other arrangements that are more suitable. If there were enough of them I would have arranged a second workshop.

The structure was… I only had an hour or so. So I’d introduce a concept, then teach the tune. A basic two parter. Anything more than 2 parts and I’d run out of time.

I’d hand out sheet music, but usually at the end of it, AFTER everyone had the tune.

Then we’d go right into a ‘slow session,’ and I’d emphasize the tunes we did in the workshop at a slow to moderate tempo, and then the regular sesh would happen after that.

I picked tunes that were fairly standard but just off the beaten path. Maybe toward the bottom of Dow’s 50 or so. Tunes that were not really hackneyed but if they went to a new town and started one of these tunes there would likely be a couple of experienced musicians that would join them and model for them without the “clown car” effect. Also ones that were just good tunes on their own, that they’d get a lifetime of value out of. So I was trying to thread that needle with the repertoire.

I got really good feedback from the regular participants. A few people who had classical training dropped out early, because it was maybe too basic for them or not what they anticipated.

Another positive effect was it broadened the repertoire of the local session, as well.

I did not attempt to include non-fiddle instruments, because then it would just have been a tune-learning session and my participants needed some help with technique. I did not attempt to include backers, etc.

That was a plus, because the workshops had a focus. If we tried to be too broad in appeal and bring in other instruments it would have been musically less successful.

One funny thing was I could not predict in advance which tunes would be easy for them to pick up and which tunes they would have a harder time with! I pulled out The Famous Ballymote once, thinking it was a gimme, and we broke our teeth on it! But The Faerie Hornpipe, which I thought would be trickier to play and commit to memory, they picked right up on and made it a session standard within a week!

Another thing that was a positive was we got their noses out of sheet music when they were sessioning. Things sounded much better after that, with more flow, and people could think on their feet.

Another plus was social media. The night after teaching a tune I’d post a video of great players playing the tune we learned that day. Several versions if I could find them, and tag the workshop participants.

For the slow air workshop I just had everyone pick out a slow air that they liked, and learn everything they could about it. Where it came from, who played it, what the words meant, if any, in Irish, the history it referred to, and share what they found with the group. We didn’t have anyone play it if they didn’t want to. I didn’t want anyone’s case of the shies get in the way of their enjoyment. If I knew the air I’d play it, or we’d find a recording. Invariably the recordings were much better than mine! But that was, I think, our most enjoyable and maybe our most productive workshop.

Things I would have done differently would have been to have been more diligent about having a sign in sheet and collecting the fee. The Comhaltas group here was originally going to take care of the collecting and I was just going to do the teaching but we didn’t communicate well enough on the administrative part.

I’d also reach out more to local schools, etc. I was hoping to get more young people involved but almost all my participants were adults maybe 1-3 years into the fiddle, though some had music backgrounds on other instruments. The time to do that, I believe, in my community (South Florida) is DURING the school year, as many kids take off on summer plans, vacations, etc. The workshop series I did was a summer workshop, so players were local (not during tourist or snowbird season) and lots of kids were out of town for the summer.

That’s just my two cents on the workshop format! Since then I’ve had the privilege of taking a two day workshop with James Kelly, a master not just of playing but of teaching as well, which is a rare combination. He really has his pedagogy wired. I would very much recommend that experience as well, because James has put a lot of thought into explaining some concepts that I was struggling to get across or didn’t fully grasp at that point. So I’d probably organize it about the same way with the same scope but I’d have been better armed with pedagogical ideas and techniques and devices for explaining different concepts, adapted to the time available and the people in attendance.

If you’ve read this far, you can also see this thread, where I asked for some advice and ideas for how not to make a hash of it. https://thesession.org/discussions/31899

Many thanks again to all those who contributed their wisdom!

Hope that helps!

Incidentally, James Kelly had a huge disparity of technical levels and abilities in his class for two days.

It just didn’t matter. It wasn’t an issue. That was one of the amazing things about it. James had the musical and pedagogical understanding to rope everyone in coming in make it inclusive and challenging to all. I’m a former schoolteacher myself. High school band, of all things, so I was very interested in how he did it.

The closest analogy I have was this… everyone came into the workshop playing checkers. Some people were much more accomplished checkers players than others. But next to James we were all playing checkers.

James comes in and says “let’s talk about chess.”
