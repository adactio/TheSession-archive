Re: Celtic Melody in Lydian Mode - PLEASE?!?

irish_em,

If you listen to the earliest recordings of Irish flute and whistle players (such as those re-issued on the Topic cds edited by Reg Hall and available on the web on various archive sites), you will hear a lot of very sharp Cs (or even true C#s) in tunes with a G tonic note. But even clearer examples will be some tunes in G tonality played on D melodeon (such as the jig The Banks of Newfoundland or the reel The Bunch of Keys = Paddy on the Turnpike, both recorded by P.J. Conlon). A lot of the one-row melodeon players would skip over the fourth degree of the scale in G tunes, but not always -- and where they play it, it’s a C#. When listening for these tunes, be sure to make allowances for the fact that some melodeons were/are pitched in C, rather than in D, and/or not in A 440.

Re: Celtic Melody in Lydian Mode - PLEASE?!?

A tune ostensibly in Lydian mode immediately loses that feel if it’s backed with the subdominant chord of its root note. If you play a C major chord in the backing to Gillan’s Apples, for example (a tune in G major if you like but G Lydian to those of us who enjoy whimsy), then the Lydian feel is lost because the C chord includes C natural, which G Lydian forbids. Nowt wrong with doing it, but you lose the “option.” As has been stated many times before on these threads, it is common in Irish tunes to be unable to ascribe a particular mode to the whole tune. A bit of the tune, half of it, all except a couple of bars… You could say that Shores Of Lough Gowna is an Aeolian tune, but what about the start of the B part? Similarly with Brendan McMahon’s, which you could say is Aeolian but which “temporarily” shifts to another (major-sounding) mode in the B part, though not requiring any notes that are not already available in the Aeolian mode. Intriguing stuff.

Pete, could it have been A-flat, or possibly that trick note between G and G#? :-)

Seriously though, once you start bringing the Celtic quarter-tone intervals into the discussion (and they are certainly around in unaccompanied Irish music) that would seem to negate useful discussion of the modes, certainly as far as tunes containing those quarter-tones are concerned.

My opinion is that the modes you see in today’s music theory texts are a centuries-old scholarly construction designed to help organise the singing of ecclesiastical chant. The classical music modes generally don’t have much of a formal relationship to traditional folk music, which is largely unaffected by other types of music. It does work the other way, however - classical composers for centuries have been going to folk music for ideas.

When I say “quarter tone” I’m referring to a broad spectrum of pitched intervals between two semitones, and not necessarily mathematically halfway between. The Czechoslovakian composer Alois Haba for instance wrote a large number of works for string quartets, solo violin, solo cello, and a specially made microtone piano, not only in quarter tones but in fifth and sixth tones. These works are not too difficult to listen to, the ear soon gets accustomed to the non-classical intervals, and occasionally you can pick up references to folk tunes in his music. Haba was a professor at the Prague Conservatory in the 1930s.

Steve, of course that C# gives it a different feel. It’s an accidental… which doesn’t make it lydian.

You can’t judge a mode simply by what notes are present in a tune. Different modes are like different “moods” or “colours” of the keys. It’s something you have to get a feel for. You can’t just look at the melody line alone - you have to see the thing as a whole.

As for the idea that tunes have nothing to do with chords - that’s rubbish. The tunes are based around suggestions of chords. Just because those notes aren’t played at the same time (unless you’re double stopping) doesn’t mean the music has nothing to do with harmony. When the brain hears a sequence of notes, it often makes sense of them in terms of notes that go together in scales or chords common in Western music. It doesn’t just “hear” a bunch of discrete notes that bear no relation to each other. Take the 1st part of the Athole Highlanders as an example:

|e3 e^cA|e^cA B^cd|e…

e^cA outlines an A major triad. That’s why the notes go well together. Don’t make the mistake of taking the triad for granted - it’s a very powerful thing. The tune might start with an “E”, and it has notes in that also appear in the Edorian scale, but the reason you don’t “hear” this part of the melody as E minor is because of that strong A maj triad. This has nothing to do with being a melody player versus backer. If a melody player were to play this part of the tune and a backer tried to back it using an E min chord, you can bet the melody player wouldn’t be very happy! That’s because we all have an awareness of the underlying harmony present in Irish music.

Where we often differ is whether we like that harmony to be filled in and made more explicit or not (thru backing) or whether we like it to remain suggested through the contours of melody (unbacked).

Glad to provide a better definition, particularly since hacker’s working definition helps to explain his position and what the problem is with it. Here’s a snippet from Wiki about chords:

“Three such pitch classes are needed to define any common chord, therefore the simultaneous sounding of two notes is sometimes classed as an interval rather than a chord. ”

This definition is supported in the Harvard Dictionary of Music and doubtless in other places too. The term “pitch class” implies all octaves of the same note. Hence G G’ G" B is not a chord, rather it is a series of intervals formed by G and B. G B D is a chord. Three pitch classes.

Thus the problem with hacker’s discussion of pipes as chord producing instruments…generally speaking they only produce two pitch classes at a time: The one from the drones and the one from the melody note. (Fifth drones in a minute). So what the drones can do is imply a harmony not require one. G B implies either Em or G major but doesn’t require either one.

So, what about the pipes that drone on fundamental and fifth (say G and D just for an example we can talk about not necessarily from the real world of pipes). That would seem to provide the needed third note wouldn’t it. Well, not really. The function of the fifth drone (the D in my example) is to reinforce a basic overtone of the home key of G (in my example). It really isn’t an added note in the sense of implying harmonies. I know that is a sticky thing to explain, but that’s the best I can do.

In sum, setting aside the importance of pipes early on for the moment we can only say that the piping tradition would supply implied harmonies not complete ones.

OK Miss L. I’ll try to give a little comment about the Wiki harmony article. It is too long to discuss in detail here, but perhaps these comments are sufficient.

1) My favorite part was the Frank Zappa quote “The creation and destruction of harmonic and ‘statistical’ tensions is essential to the maintenance of compositional drama. Any composition (or improvisation) which remains consistent and ‘regular’ throughout is, for me, equivalent to watching a movie with only ‘good guys’ in it, or eating cottage cheese.” It doesn’t really apply much to Irish trad except in the extreme edges of it. Most of the harmonic material used in Irish trad would fit nicely into Zappa’s cottage cheese category. This is not to say one can’t spice up the harmonies, but rather that mostly they are not spiced up, just as they aren’t spiced up much in traditional bluegrass. Both those traditions depend on structured improvisation around melodies, so the harmonic material itself is not so important.

2) The discussion of acceptable consonance moving from fifths, fourths and octaves to later embrace thirds is historically accurate but doesn’t really deal with the “whys” of that whole issue. It probably was not the case that early musicians didn’t hear thirds as consonant (i.e. their ears probably would have accepted the consonance), but rather that the old studies of music placed it as part and parcel of the quadrivium (music astronomy, geometry, and arithmetic). In that tradition intervals were described as divisions of a string into parts by small whole number ratios. 1 to 2 is an octave 1 to 3 a fifth etc. The ratios describing thirds were not small ratios and so were deemed as dissonant. It was many years before theorists were willing to let go of that mathematical definition and accept what their ears were telling them. And the article is correct that the practice of singing in “parallel second inversion chords” in the British Isles was instrumental in the change of definition. If you want the definition of parallel second inversion chords we can do that later. Suffice it to say that each chord, like G c e, is made up of three intervals: a fourth (G c ), a third (c e) and a sixth (G e). If the next chord is A d f you still have the same intervals (yes the thirds and sixths have changed to minor) The sound of these parallel chords is so very sweet that theorists were sort of forced into accepting the consonance. Interesting too is that though the “British Isles” style (called fauxbourdon if anyone really cares) was known the development really moved forward with little regard for vertical harmony until 200 years later.

In most cases my complaints about the Wiki article are less with the content than with missing detail (like that above), and with confusing language which is almost always a major problem in talking about music theory. Most of us would be lots better off with one of the “Theory for Dummys” books I think.

David’s post above, https://thesession.org/discussions/24333#comment508972, puts me in mind of the mediaeval method of singing known as “organum”, which we were taught about at school. Church plain chant was originally monophonic (it still is), but monks with deeper voices would naturally sing an octave below or perhaps the interval of a fifth or fourth below. The fifth, in particular, would generate a very deep difference tone which must have been very effective in the acoustics of a stone church. The chant would always end on the unison. It must have been a natural step to start singing in thirds to the main melody, and thus the beginnings of true harmony as we know it. And then the harmony part would develop its own melodic line and thus polyphony came about.
I’ve been doing a little digging around, and it appears that one of the earliest writings on harmony, “De Harmonica Institutione”, was by the 9th century Benedictine Saint Hucbald. You can download the relevant 10MB PDF from here: http://www.documentacatholicaomnia.eu/02m/0840-0930,_Hucbaldus_Sancti_Amandi,_De_Harmonica_Institutione,_MLT.pdf
It is in Latin (and probably has never been translated), but there are several diagrams and tables which give an idea of what was going on at the time. I don’t suppose for a moment that S Hucbald invented harmony; he was just writing a reasonably comprehensive treatise on the musical practices of is era, which must have been going on for some considerable time.

Actually, TWH’s point can be relevant when applied to the fiddle. If you play the chord D-A-f# (struck or as an arpeggio) you should flatten the f# by a small amount so that it equates to an f# harmonic on the D string. If you don’t do this it won’t sound quite right - probably slightly harsh in fact. All this improves the resonance of the instrument and is one of the factors that singles out the better players.

Pastoral pipes -- the predecessor of union/uilleann pipes -- had one regulator and a foot joint, which gave them a bottom C. In the late eighteenth century the foot joint was abandoned, which enabled the chanter to be played closed style, on the knee, and two more regs were added.

It’s then pure supposition to theorize why regs were increased from two to three, or why the pastoral pipes had them in the first place. That’s the thing… no one wrote down *why* these instruments developed the way did. All we have are surviving instruments and collections of tunes that were written for them. All we can do is make conjectures based on those two things. For example, it’s evident that foot joint and non-foot jointed pipes existed concurrently because tutors for union pipes include tunes which use the note below the bottom note of the chanter, which you have on pastoral pipes but not union pipes. The pipes were also not ONLY used to play Irish music, as late 18th century and 19th century tunebooks contained Irish tunes, Scottish tunes, English tunes, and even Baroque tunes. They were also being made in Edinburgh, Newcastle, and London.

It’s unlikely to be the case that Irish dance music as we know it was based on the capabilities of the union/uilleann pipes. There was dance music in Ireland before there were union pipes. Seamus Tansey suggested in his book, “The Bardic Apostles of Innisfree” that playing the fiddle so it sounded like pipes was something pioneered by Sligo fiddler Kip Scanlon in the 1850s and 1860s. In his Traditional Music in Ireland, Tomas O‘Canainn(who is a uilleann piper) cites Neales’ A Collection of Most Celebrated Irish Tunes, published in Dublin in 1726. According to O‘Canainn, Neales’ features a number of Carolan (harp) compositions as well as other airs and tunes (such as Limerick’s Lamentation). They are not cited as instrument-specific settings.

O‘Canainn also describes the two-volume Burke Thumoth Collection published in 1750, which, O’Canainn writes, “contain many interesting airs that are still well known in the tradition. The tunes are supplied with a set of variations and a bass, neither of which, from a traditional point of view, do anything for the music.”

Bunting’s first volume, Ancient Irish Music, published in 1796, consists, says O’Canainn, of “66 tunes collected in the main from harpers at the Belfast Harp Festival of 1792.”

More of relevance from O’Canainn’s book:

“Until the middle of the 18th century the uilleann pipes consisted of a chanter and two drones. At this stage a further drone was added, an octave below the other two, so that the three drones were tuned to a D with an octave between each, the highest one being at the same pitch as the lowest note of the chanter.”

Later in this same chapter on the uilleann pipes, O’Canainn writes:

“It comes as something as a surprise to realise that the extended harmonic sections of the pipes (that is, the set of regulators) is what is generally regarded as the most significant Irish contribution to pipe development. The irony of it is that the extra regulators were added to provide a harmonic accompaniment which would satisfy nineteenth-century ears and they are, in general, an unsuitable accompaniment for the highly sophisticated melodic tradition that we associate with uilleann pipes.”

Later still (same chapter):

“It has already been pointed out that the harmonic accompaniment provided by the regulators, particularly in the hands of an insensitive player, is a very mixed blessing indeed. The layout of the keys is a direct invitation to the piper to put the heel of his hand across all three regulators and obtain block chords which tend to obscure the melody, not only because of their chordal nature but because of the lack of balance between the three regulator reeds and the single chanter reed. Many pipers do not seem to be aware of the musical satisfaction given by the interplay between a decorated melodic line and a steady drone which provides a large number of higher harmonics. Such a system is self sufficient and has been the basic formula for much of the world’s folk music for thousands of years.”

Don’t know about anyone else, but O’Canainn’s sense of piping and its place in this tradition strikes me as reasoned and reasonable, well researched (he’s engineering faculty at University College in Cork), and clear.
