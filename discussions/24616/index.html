I heard it again

This is a common myth which is very persistent. It goes like this, “If you have learnt to play bluegrass you have what you need to play Irish tunes.”
The good news is I am playing with a bluegrass guitarist who seems to have a knack for Irish music. Despite the fact that even though he was providing a wonderful upbeat for the polkas someone actually said to give it an om-pah om-pah beat. Polkas have a great rhythm & he seemed to be able to find it instinctively.
I really like this backer. He very freely admits that he has not grasped the rhythm for slides or slip jigs. He is better than he thinks. But the simple fact is that he cannot get these rhythms from playing bluegrass.
Has anyone else come across the attitude that since bluegrass has Irish influences that everything one needs to play the tunes is to know how to play bluegrass? I doubt that most of the crossover tunes will get you very far in a bluegrass or an Irish session. These may be more familiar to more players, but they barely touch the repertoire of either genre.
I’m only referring to bluegrass. There are other genres (New England style in particular) which have more of the Irish & Scottish dance rhythms. I know a number of bluegrass players who have learnt to play Irish traditional music, though it has taken them years to learn certain things, like the upbeat, slide rhythm . . .
I have brought this up before & received a bit of flak from my mates. I am sure I will receive more this time around. Thanks, & apologies, in advance.

Re: I heard it again

I don’t think experience of playing bluegrass equips a backer with the skill of *following* a tune. Bluegrass tunes tend to follow more or less fixed chord progressions; Irish tunes do not necessarily follow any chord progression at all - it is up to the backer to find a sequence of chords that (i) fits with the melody at any given point and (ii) follow on from one another in a way that makes musical sense.

A bluegrass guitarist who happens to have a very good ear - probably like your friend, Random - might be able to pick up this skill very quickly. But it is not a skill that is as fundamental to playing bluegrass as it is to Irish music.

Re: I heard it again

It is not just about tunes the traditions have in common, it is how they are played. The same tune can morph from Irish Reel to Scottish Fling to Cape Breton Strathspey to American Barndance. Go to the long discussion appended to the tune database for The Road to Lisdoonvarna to find a discussion of what makes a slide versus a single jig versus a double jig.
I remember when I first learned to play the whistle from the little Clarke lesson book, and then learned some Irish tunes and brought them to a session. There was a bunch of tonguing in my playing, that was inspired by the New England fife music I had heard all my life. Fortunately, a lot of the flute players in the area are conversant in both traditions, and were able to nudge me in the right direction--the articulation is so completely different, even though the traditions share many tunes in common.
Bluegrass players do not necessarily have the tools to play Irish music. A mule has a horse for a parent, but that doesn’t mean it can run in the Kentucky Derby. (Oh dear, that analogy isn’t really flattering to bluegrass players, isn’t it?)

Re: I heard it again

I have some very good evidence on this issue. When Junji first walked into our local with a guitar and sat down to play, his skill was obvious, but he would get off tonally and his rhythm seemed a bit confused here and there. We didn’t know it at the time, but Junji was the one who started the bluegrass music craze in Japan and the band he and his friends put together went on to record 30 albums on the Toshiba/EMI label and toured successfully for the next 20 years becoming Japan’s most popular bluegrass band. But as I said--we didn’t know this at the time. Everyone liked Junji and we gave him recordings of great trad players and he went home, re-tuned his guitar, and over the next few months made astonishing progress. (it should be noted that Junji’s astonishing progress was due to his extraordinary focus, discipline and talent) After playing in a few different bands with yours truly, Juni went on to play, and tour with some of the biggest names in Ireland.

So the conclusion for me is: yes… you have applicable skills as a bluegrass guitar player, but not everything you need to play trad without first learning the modes rhythms and tunes.

Re: I heard it again

Tunes are tunes. The bluegrassers will play them their way because they’ve learned to hear them that way. The irish trad musicians will play them their way for the same reason. The contra dance musicians will do something else to the tunes. So will Cape Breton players, or Scandinavian musicians, or whatever.

Folks in each tradition will grumble that they have it right and that you have to learn their way because that is the only way. Far too few really can crossover among the traditions.

Truth to be told each tradition has good reason for the way it does things. But all should respect the other and recognize the worth of “the other way.” And that respect should include realizing that the other tradition has a different approach and not just an extension of the approach they are familiar with.

Of course we all get to grumble when another tradition “steals” our tunes :)
