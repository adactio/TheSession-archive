Ok guys and gals. Who’s to blame ?

Who were your biggest influences, gave you the most inspiration when you first started playing ITM ?

Personally, there was so many but let’s try and keep it down to one or two for bands and individual instruments.

Here’s my list of who helped get me hooked.

I play banjo so I’ll kick start with the frying pan.

Banjoists : Giving my age away here but in the 60’s/ early 70’s there wasn’t a lot of choice…..so credit has to go to Barney Mc Kenna and the banjo player with the Johnstons also caught my attention. I believe that was Mick Moloney ?

Later inspiration from notable players were Kieran Hanrahan and Charlie Piggot. Less well known…..Ciaran Duffy, Downpatrick and Jimmy Fitzpatrick, Belfast. Then along came the likes of Cathal Hayden, Brian Mc Grath and Gerry O’Connor, what a threesome !

Fiddlers : I’ll be brief here. Maurice Lennon and Frankie Gavin were the kingpins in my formative years and you can put Paddy Glackin and Kevin Burke in there too.

Fiddlers of less repute but who gave me great encouragement were….Dennis Heaney (RIP), Derry and Brian Sutherland, ex Killyeagh but then moved to Derry.

Other big influences were :

Tom Mc Gonigle (concertina) Newcastle, Co.Down.
Tom Traynor (fiddle) Downpatrick.
Seamus Quinn, Derrygonnely (Gerry Donnelly to those that know the place :)

I’ll draw the line here or else my list will be endless but I’ve got to mention two more guys who gave me a mountain of tunes, encouragement, inspiration and great pleasure….Jerry and Lawrence Mc Elhinney, box players, Muff, Donegal and Glasgow.

Top, “ace” band has got to be The Bothy Band.

If any of the aforementioned are members of this forum, I apologise for any embarrasment but a big “thank you” is in order.

S/foyle.

Re: Ok guys and gals. Who’s to blame ?

I first heard the Chieftains on the radio in 1975, and on that same show they played recordings by Planxty and the Bothy Band. That made me want to find out more about this music. Then the individuals who had the greatest influence began to pop up. After becoming aware of ITM, my desire to play it evolved over the next few years. But I can easily narrow it down to my initial influences.

whistle -- Mary Bergin
concertina -- Noel Hill
flute -- Matt Molloy
bodhran -- Johnny McDonough

For the next couple of decades (80s and 90s) there were many other major influences, but the one’s that stand out are; Frankie Kennedy, Harry Bradley, Micheal O Ralghallaigh, Kevin Crawford, and Josephine Marsh. Each of these people had something about their technique that is outstanding and inspiring for me. The most unique influence was from Josephine Marsh on account of her playing an instrument I don’t play, (all though she did once pick up my concertina and play it in a way that forever changed my approach.) The thing about her playing that struck me was the feeling she puts into it. In Jo’s case it wasn’t so much her technique necessarily, but rather it was what she showed me about the spirit of the music that I have her to thank for.

Of course I’m still trying to put this all together into my playing, but I feel like these folks were the most important influences.

Oh… and I should add that I’m forever indebted to Patrick Egan (concertina player) who showed me the Phantom Button last summer.

Re: Ok guys and gals. Who’s to blame ?

Well, only concertina players would understand, (or be at all interested) in the Phantom Button, but that’s the name I gave it after having it be a mystery for so long. It’s a technique that allows you to get that three-note triplet on buttons that you’re playing on your left hand. I had seen that triplet achieved by drumming your fingers in such a way that they all hit the same button, (Noel Hill showed me this for the low A on the top row of the left hand,) but it’s limited to notes where you can afford to end up on your index finger. If the triplet includes a forth note you’ll end up back on the finger you started with, but that still limited it’s usefulness to certain notes in certain tunes. (The Oak Tree was the tune in question on that night in Cruise’s Pub.) I was hearing the players using this tight 3 and 4 note triplet a lot. Like Micheal O Ralghallaigh, Aogan Lynch, Tim Collins, etc., and when I would watch these people play up close and in person the activity on their left land didn’t make sense at first. They were only using 2 fingers, but getting 3 or 4 notes. I did notice them hitting some button on their right hand, but I couldn’t tell which one it was. I experimented when I got back home, and was very close, but I couldn’t find the button to make it sound right. Then Patrick showed me the button… but it didn’t exist. He was tapping a place where there was no button, hence the name -- Phantom Button.

Re: Ok guys and gals. Who’s to blame ?

I’m really a converted GHB player, so I still count that influence as the primary one in my flute playing. I’ve had exactly one “technique” lesson on flute, and that came almost exactly a year after I started playing.

GHB teachers would include Roxanne Fargason, Jerry Finegan, and Jimmy McIntosh. The 78th Frasier highlanders for showing the possibilities of creativity in what is normally a very conservative genre of music.

For strictly Irish stuff in a general sense. . . the usual suspects from the 70’s revival - Chieftains, Planxty, De Daanan,Bothies, etc. The winner in the “general influence-individual” category would be Fiona Richie, for simply playing all that stuff on the radio.

For flute-playing influences specifically - that’s a tough one. I generally listen more to UP players than fluters, so Jerry O‘sullivan, Leo Rowsome, Liam O’F, and Paddy Keenan were big.

For actual flute players, it’s still so new to me that picking one or two as the “primary” influence (after listening to ITM and playing pipes for two decades) is kind of silly. Nonetheless, if I could play like anyone on the planet it would be Paddy Carty, probably followed by MM, circa “Heathery Breeze.” In terms of nitty-gritty issues of style, the guys and gals in the local sessions probably have as much influence as any “big name.”

I’d like to thank especially, the members of the academy, my producer, my family, and my lovely wife for making all of this possible.

That’s a great nickname, Jack.
