Why do some musicians like to race through a reel ?

Added by flauta dolce on July 27th, 2005

Why do some musicians like to race through a reel ?

There is a trend amongst some musicians to play extremely fast in a session and thus the quality of their playing diminishes, if they’re not up to playing at speed.

In the meanwhile, those who are attempting to follow the tune are unable to do so and often make a mess of things.

So I don’t see the purpose of playing at top speed.

I would be interested in hearing what people think about this.

↳ Posted by flauta dolce on July 27th, 2005.

Re: Why do some musicians like to race through a reel ?

I firmly believe that no musician should play a tune faster than he/she *can* but, in too many cases, many of them play as fast as they *think they can*.

So, there’s nothing wrong with playing really fast if a player is “up to it” and doing it properly. In these cases, I am happy enough to take a back seat and listen if I can’t keep up. It iritates me though when some people play at speed just for the sake of it either to “show off” or exclude other players and miss out half the notes etc in the process.

I’ve no problem with *good* fast players, though. It’s just up to me to get better.

Re: Why do some musicians like to race through a reel ?

It’s easy to complain about the speed at which tunes are played - but listening to a recording of yourself (particularly in a session environment) can be very revealing - for the most part I think you’ll hear yourself playing faster than you thought you were.

2 major contributors I feel are:

1. I think the comment has been made before that it is more difficult to play slow. Try it sometime with a tune you are really comfortable with…not just slowing down the tune, but also adjusting the timing of your ornamentation accordingly. Not so easy.

2. I also think that it has everything to do with what you are listening to and who you are regularly playing with - a tempo invariably ingrains itself in your head with the tune. Think about having the Bothy Band’s rendition in your head of the two tunes The Enchanted Lady & The Holy Land, as opposed to sound of the same two tunes played by Ben Lennon & Friends on “The Natural Bridge”.

Cheers.

Re: Why do some musicians like to race through a reel ?

“Seamus Ennis is supposed to have released an album which is meant to be really good. I’ve forgotten the name of it, would you know which one I would be looking for.”

I’m writing a biography so have some of this information ready to hand.

SE’s readily-available recordings:

* Forty Years of Irish Piping: assembled by Patrick Sky; one of the first Green Linnet releases; songs and tunes on whistle and pipes; some great recitations; good place to start.

* The Bonny Bunch of Roses: re-release of his first LP (1959). He was at a difficult place in his life but the plping (more than the singing or tin whistle) is titanic.

* The Pure Drop/The Flax in Bloom: re-release as single CD of two late (1970s) LP’s. His playing is not the best here.

* The Return from Fingal: Harry Bradshaw-remastered collection of recordings from all periods in his life, going all the way back to acetates recording in the late ‘30s, which make clear he was already a fantastic player in his late teens. To tie this back to the thread, there’s a live set recorded in Miltown from ’59, including the Silver Spear, where he’s just blazing the tempo. Flauta, “Fingal” is probably the recording you’re thinking of.

chris smith

Larsheen, you beat me to it. Of course, speed is relative. It is relative to your capability, and it is relative to what you have heard. Some people around here think that I play fast in the session, but it is pretty mild mannered in comparison to Belfast sessions, or Cork sessions.

From a beginner’s standpoint, I think that it is difficult to play jigs or reels at dance tempo. So, “acquiring” speed is a good thing. It is a goal. You want to be able to play up to speed for a good dancer. You want to sit in and play for a ceili.

This thought gets stuck in some people’s heads. The thought that the faster they can play, the better they will sound. Some people have not been playing long enough to know when they are playing too fast.

↳ Posted by Proinsios on July 27th, 2005.

Re: Why do some musicians like to race through a reel ?

Music should be interesting to listen to, fast or slow. I’ve heard all sorts of tempos from old musicians, some of these musos were born in the 1880s, on one occasion they might play bolts of lightning, on another they’d poke along. But most always they had an interesting way with a tune, some nice little catch in their phrasing. It’s like speech - who would you rather talk to, Jimmy Cagney or your local newscaster?
If you poke along you can humour the notes that much more, you’re not just struggling to get the notes in. I think this was more the policy in the old days with so many old people around, wanting to get in on the dancing and not having the gusto anymore. Various accounts mention old people keeping a lid on tempo, or wanting them nice and easy - Michael Coleman, for instance, wasn’t popular with dancers because he was too fast.
Seamus Ennis is the most “interesting” musician I’ve ever heard - modern pipers might as well be playing cheap Casio keyboards compared to the sound he got out of the chanter, even before the ornaments kicked in. Now, his pipes are a bit out of tune on the Bonny Bunch - and what do you expect his singing or whistling to sound like? Seamus was a fine singer and whistler, if you don’t like his voice too bad but trad singers weren’t showing up on stage at the Met or anything. Also note that the jig he plays here was good enough for Grey Larsen to transcribe.
The Return to Fingal is the Ennis to own, though. Not sure if it’s available in the States right now, try CelticGrooves.com. If not that 40 years, then Bonny Bunch.
You should hear some of the stuff that’s not been released! Folktrax in England have some vintage Ennis, too.
What’s this bio you’re writing? Pat Mitchell and Jimmy O’Brien-Moran were writing a book analyzing his music in detail.
