Traditional Irish Tune Key and Mode Ear Trainer

I’ve put together a new Traditional Irish Tune Key and Mode Ear Trainer for anyone working on hearing keys, modes, and backup chords.

When I first learned to play backup, one of the things I used to do was pick random tunes, listen to them, and try to work out the chords by ear. This trainer is basically a more structured version of that exercise.

Each session picks 10 tunes completely at random from the current collection of 90 traditional Irish tunes. You listen to the tune without seeing the notation, identify the tonal center and mode, and then submit your answer. It’ll tell you the correct answer, show some common accompaniment chords for that mode, and let you hear the relevant scales to help reinforce the difference.

At the end of the 10 tunes, there’s also a review showing how you did and which tonal centers and modes gave you the most trouble.

Right now there are 90 tunes in the collection, and I’ll probably add more over time.

It’s meant primarily as a practical ear-training tool for people learning or improving their backup playing, but it may also be useful to anyone who wants to get better at recognizing Major, Dorian, Mixolydian, and Minor tunes by ear.

I hope people find it useful!

Based on a suggestion by my wife Linda as well as Frank Herrmann on the O’Flaherty Retreat Facebook group, I’ve updated the ear trainer to also include identification of tune rhythms.

I’ve put together a Traditional Irish Tune Rhythm, Key, and Mode Trainer for anyone working on listening for tune rhythms, keys, modes, and backup chords.

When I first learned to play backup, one of the things I used to do was pick random tunes, listen to them, and try to work out the chords by ear. This trainer is basically a more structured version of that exercise.

Each session picks 10 tunes completely at random from the current collection of 100 traditional Irish tunes. You listen to each tune without seeing the notation and can choose from five different exercises: Rhythm + Key + Mode, Rhythm + Key/Mode, Rhythm Only, Key + Mode, or Key/Mode. In the options with a +, key and mode are answered separately; with Key/Mode, they’re selected as a single combined answer.

The rhythms currently represented in the collection are Reels, Jigs, Hornpipes, Slip Jigs, Slides, Polkas, and Barndances. For key and mode exercises, the trainer works with Major, Dorian, and Mixolydian tunes.

After you submit an answer, the trainer gives you immediate feedback. For key and mode exercises, it also shows some common accompaniment chords and lets you hear the root note and relevant scales. If you get the key or mode wrong, you can directly compare the root notes and scales for your answer and the correct answer.

At the end of the 10 tunes, there’s a review showing your overall score, including which rhythms, keys, and modes gave you the most trouble.

Right now there are 100 tunes in the collection, and I’ll probably add more over time.

It’s meant primarily as a practical ear-training tool for people learning or improving their backup playing, but it may also be useful to anyone who wants to get better at recognizing traditional Irish tune rhythms and hearing the differences between Major, Dorian, and Mixolydian tunes.

I hope people find it useful!

Re: Traditional Irish Tune Key and Mode Ear Trainer

I’d make the case that even Julia Delaney is ambiguous. Could be Dorian, could be Minor. Commonly played versions don’t have a B or Bb anywhere, which would be the note that makes the difference.

Same thing for other ambiguous tunes like “The Musical Priest”, they are missing the one note (G or G#) that would distinguish between Dorian and Minor.

This is why I chose not to include any Minor tunes in the ear trainer in favor of Dorian. For these ambiguous tunes, the Dorian chord patterns also often work without modification.

There are some tunes that, in some versions, are truly minor, for example “Dunmore Lasses”:

X:1
T:The Dunmore Lasses
R:Reel
L:1/8
M:4/4
Q:2/4=90
K:G
|: E2 EF GFGA | Be e2 Be e2 | E2 EF GABG | AcBA GEFD |
E2 EF GFGA | Beed e2 ef | (3gfe fd eBdB |1 AcBA GEFD :|2 AcBA GE E2 ||
|: gfeg fedc | Be e2 Be e2 | gfeg fedB | AcBA GE E2 |
gfeg fedc | Beed e2 ef | (3gfe fd eBdB |1 AcBA GE E2 :|2 AcBA GEFD |]

This version clearly has a tonality of E, and has C naturals, so that’s E Minor, not E Dorian (which would have C#s).

The trainer certainly could be extended with additional true Minor tunes for more advanced-level players, but that’s not something I’m actively considering at the moment.

The trainer is primarily for newer player who are challenged with distinguishing even basic tune styles, keys, and modes, but I’m sure even more experienced players would find it useful.

Traditional Irish Tune Name Ear Trainer

Based on the same framework as my Traditional Irish Tune Rhythm, Key, and Mode Ear Trainer, I’ve just released a new Traditional Irish Tune Name Ear Trainer.

This time the challenge is simple:

Can you identify the tune just by listening to it?

Each session randomly selects 10 tunes. For each one, listen to the tune and choose its name from a list of possible answers.

You can choose how challenging you want to make it by showing 2, 4, 6, 8, or 10 tune names.

At the end of the session, you can review the tunes you missed and practice them again.

The built-in collection has over 100 traditional Irish tunes, originally drawn from the San Diego Comhaltas chapter tunebook.

You’re not limited to those tunes.

You can load your own collection of ABC tunes from an .abc or .txt file and use that in place of the built-in collection.

So if you have a particular session repertoire, tunebook, or collection of tunes you’re trying to learn by ear, you can turn it into your own personalized tune-name ear trainer.

Thanks to Tim Arbon for suggesting the idea.

I grew up listening mainly to rock and folk (and classical and jazz) and, in those pre-internet days, with very little written sources, I basically had to learn stuff by listening repeatedly to parts of LPs (vinyl discs, if you like) over and over again by lifting and replacing the stylus. So my ear probably developed there, before I got into trad in the early 2000s.

I didn’t actually start seriously playing the melodies of the tunes until 2020 when I gave up the accompaniment chair at a local session to let someone else in who had to give up melody playing because of physical problems. But, by that time, I had heard some tunes so many times that I knew them already and just had to find where the notes were on the mandolin.

People do seem to think I learn tunes pretty quickly - maybe I just spend more time on it. But I probably spend just as much time finding different ways to accompany tunes, as I’m not that fond of accompanying too much on the fly. But when I am expected to accompany a tune on the fly, I’ll be starting real simple and listening for necessary (chord) changes. At a simplest level, that could mean playing the root chord (or root note) until it doesn’t sound right and changing to the dominant or sub-dominant or whatever. But I’m also trying to anticipate where changes might occur and what they’ll be. A fairly good knowledge of harmony and how tunes are generally structured helps.

Because I’ve not been doing so much accompaniment of late I think I’m probably getting a bit rusty on that side of things and actually, if I’m being honest, I often find it more enjoyable playing the tunes than back up. It’s a lot easier for one thing.

As an aside, years ago, as an ear training exercise, I used to watch the Eurovision Song Contest, with a guitar in hand, and have to have every song worked out before it finished - key and chords.

Re: Traditional Irish Tune Key and Mode Ear Trainer

“I wonder if you noticed anything changing about the way you accompanied tunes as you started to play more of them.”

That’s a VERY interesting question and one I don’t know if I can answer fully here without a lot of further thought, as I’ve never really thought about it before.
But I guess one obvious thing is that, playing in a restricted palate of keys, I became acquainted with many more voicings of common chords. Also, the diatonic nature of trad encouraged me to think more of bass runs within the chords. And I eventually realised that it was just as much about what notes I could remove from the chords as what I could add.

Undoubtably, my playing became idiomatic as I began understand more and listen to the backing masters. But I also like the idea of individuality - I’m good at sounding like me and I’m not so great at sounding like other people. (In my pre-trad life I spent years trying to sound like other people - it was an epiphany when I decided to be me and play to my strengths.) I like the individuality and personality of players and I’m not so taken with the generic university taught sound.

And I’ve just noticed - I’m on all 4’s.
