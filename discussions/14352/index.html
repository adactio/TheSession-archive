You are very wise to ask, as the bluegrass/folk style is not entirely the same as for ITM. Definitely listen, although there are ground-breaking bands on record who mainly now concert who, by necessity, use arrangements for accompaniment that do not work so well in the hurly-burly of a spontaneous session.
Also be aware that there is no absolute and agreed chordal arrangement for almost any tune.

Re: Looking for strumming suggestions

Come on now fellahs, lets not jump down this bloke’s neck for asking an innocent question. The antiquity of the guitar in traditional music isn’t the question, I tend to think of Willie Johnson (1930s) in Shetland as the instigator of the modern techniques used in standard tuning. The ligitimacy of the instrument isn’t really relavent either, it would appear to be here like it or not - much in the manner of mandolin-family instruments and, a few hundred years earlier, the fiddle.

With regard to the question asked….weeeelll, it’s not all about strumming “patterns”. It’s all about a sense of rhythm, and this isn’t a guitar specific thing. Generally, each beat for the dancers is two beats for the musicians, so tunes in 4/4 (ie reels and some hornpipes) aren’t normally accented 1 2 3 4, it would be something more akin to 1 (2) 3 (4) - i.e DuDu. However, it’s not uncommon for accompanists to drop the up strum in favour of a down strum over partially muted strings - resulting the the Shetland boom-chick style of guitar playing. This, however, is usually done in conjunction to fairly fast changes, often with extended chords chosen to give chromatic runs.

For single jigs, many people advocate the DUD DUD approach. Others suggest playing DuDuDu, it’s a matter of personal taste - I find DUD DUD too in conflict with the rhythm of 6/8, I hear it as double time (which given there are only 3 playing beats to the bar, and so 3 dancing beats per 2 bars, it sort of is).

That’s the “mathematics” of it. In terms of developing a good right hand technique, there are a few things. Firstly, I use what I think of as a “funk” action - the motion comes through the rotation of the lower arm however it manifests at the wrist (no doubt I’ll have awful carpal tunnel in a few years) - many people lock out the wrist and play from the elbow which I find stiffens up the action and makes motion less natural. A few play from the shoulder which I can’t understand and generally people with this approach sound forced.

I’ll post this little lot now and finish off in another post, it this vanishes into the ether I’ll not be chuffed!

Re: Looking for strumming suggestions (pt. 2)…….

Right, continuing from above then……… :-)

In order to develop the sense of rhythm it’s important to listen to a LOT of traditional music, some of accompanied, some unaccompanied, some of it “un”arranged, some of it over-arranged. Get a feel for what other people do in terms of the rhythms used in guitar playing, listen to the DADGAD school of guitarists, the drop-D-ers, and those using standard tuning. There are subtle differences in the rythmic effects that are as a result of the different tunings, even if it’s not in the tuning you use the exposure to it will always give you something to think about.

In terms of non-guitar exercises, tapping fingers/feet when sitting on the metro/tube/bus in the morning is useful, particularly the fingers just using thumb and forefinger to simulate down and up strokes - try keeping to the rhythm as well as crossing the beat, and creating tension and release with rhythm. It’s not going to come in all that much in a session, but these sort of things are good skills to have. Similarly simply tapping out rhythms is useful when listening to music, traditional or non-traditional.

Somewhere between non-guitar exercises and guitar exercises lies the horrible one of “learning to find the off beat”. This always manifests itself unpleasently at jazz gigs when the non-musical audience are asked to “clap along to the off beat” (personal favourite was a Courtney Pine gig at the Barbican, bloody hilarious) it’s not 1 2 *clap* 4 - it’s 1 *clap* 3 *clap*. I got to this by practicing quaver scales over a metronome starting the tonic on beat two. Somehow the combination of the resolution from sub-tonic or sub-medient to the tonic seemed to drill it home. Not that you’ll use the off beat much, but it’s very useful to have a feel for it - particularly when playing hornpipes.

Re: Looking for strumming suggestions

A brief aside to have a shot at Michael (who I normally agree with……) With all due respect, every bugger who doesn’t play the guitar says “learn the tunes” when ever a question comes up about guitar accompaniment. That IS, of course, true (there, I DO actually agree with you), however, I think I’m in a position to impart some technical advice on the subject. Your approach would be rather akin to telling a novice fiddler to learn the tunes and not bothering to teach them about the the wrist movements in the right hand.

Having got that off my chest I’ll resume what’s turning into rather a hefty tome……

Finally, there are practice techniques to apply on the guitar. Firstly (presuming you use a pick) muting the strings with the left hand (or weaving a couple of 1 inch strips of cloth into the strings at about the 10th fret) and playing along to recordings. This means that 1) you don’t need to focus on harmonic changes and 2) you can’t hide behind chord changes or left hand damping, the development of right hand control is focused on. Some players (myself included) use front end of the fleshy pad on the palm of the hand just about where the thumb meets the hand as an extra device to impart a slightly more percussive effect however this is a matter of taste.

Finally, the acid test is tune the guitar to an open chord, weave a very thin strip of fabric (<1cm) into the strings and push it up hard against the nut. Then, find a tune in that key (with the fewest chord changes) and “play along”. If you can control the rhythm and suitably enhance the tune then you’re pretty much there in technique at least.

Naturally, these exercises take time to become profficient. If you already practice 4 hours a day, and devote 45 minutes to these exercises you’ll develop a lot faster than if you do them for 10 minutes about once a week (where you won’t develop). That said, like with all exercises, pain is bad. A slight ache is just about ok, sharp pain means STOP, normally for the day.

There are other ways to do it, but this is probably the most effective use of time and doesn’t annoy people at sessions. But as is said above, listening is the start and end of learning how to accompany. It’s just the middle bits where technique and knowledge can be used.

Re: Looking for strumming suggestions

Absolutely no question, you have to learn the tunes, it’s the only way. Of course you need a good knowledge of the instrument first, so you can learn the tunes. So get the basics under your belt first.

What no one ever explains, since the music is not written out for guitar, is the HOW of how you are supposed to learn the tunes. The only way, is to have a good basic knowledge of what the keys are for starters, then sit and play to CDs, or tape sessions and practice to them at home. Of get some instructional DVDs by someone whose playing you like and work with them too.

You learn the tunes by playing along with them so much, you memorize them. They deviate from a pattern so often, there is no generic “this is how you play with a tune in D” etc. If you do it enough you will (hopefully) come to know the music so well, you don’t have to stop and think about what comes next. You really don’t have time for that when you’re playing anyway. You also learn to hear the structures (or lack thereof on some tunes) of the various keys, with hard work you should start to hear most of them almost immedately.

The real key to it all, pardon pun, is you have to love to sit and practice for hours, and not notice the time has passed and still thirst for more. You start to recognize tunes, and ultimately get to know them as well as the melody players, so well in fact that ultimately you can maybe join in bits of the melody where it fits if you want.

As for strumming patterns, I don’t strum, so have no input to give you. I play strictly fingerstyle and don’t think about chords so much as complimenting the tune, do a lot of noting, droning and partial chords if you could call two noters that…. and subtly…. always remembering it is all about the melody player, the tune, and never bashing away. That’s not to say you can’t be a strong and steady player with a good solid “attack” but it needs to be secondary to the melody. Guitar players who want to be the star, they doom themselves too many times by competing with the melody.

Another thing, some people pick guitar because they think it will be easy. It’s pretty complex, unless you are content to bash out three chords in any given key with no consideration given to the twists and turns of each tune, and no effort put into learning the tunes. If you really want to learn to play it right, it takes a lot of work. Being both a melody player (guitar and in the past mandolin) and a backer, I have to say I find backing/accompanying way more challenging to do right and tastefully, but it’s the reason too, that I love it so much.

Just don’t go getting frustrated if you don’t get it right out and sound like some of your guitar idols. It not only takes lots of work, but if you are just starting out there are little hand and wrist muscles that need to get trained before you have an ease of playing. As these build up, you will find you loosen the death grip on the neck. Guitar has a much longer stretch than other instruments, you need to ultimately be loose enough and have enough hadn and wrist strength to fly all over that neck really fast. Time brings you there, but if you love it, every small step will be a happy improvement. I’ve been at it 44 years and should be a lot better than I am considering the time put in (!) but I stil enjoy getting lost in it and discovering new things all the time.

Good luck!

If every person who picked up a guitar and took it to a session had the same attitude as Iris the world would be a better place. Although what on earth we would ever find to talk about on here however, might be rather less vehement.

Just a brief note on actually playing tunes on the guitar - firstly that’s not really what “learn the tunes” means, being able to sing it is usually sufficient (although in the cases of some particularly devilish tunes, having a known set of chords is going to be vital). A good example is Reel Beatrice, if you can sing the tune you’ll know that the B and C parts are in different keys and should be able to establish the interval and feel that on the guitar - if you get it wrong once you won’t the second time - lesson learnt! On the other hand, some tunes behave in damned odd ways, so you need to know (for example) that in the third bar of the second part of the Old Lancashire Hornpipe there’s a C natural and that playing an A major at that point will result in a train crash.

Secondly, don’t try to play in box pattern scales like you would in jazz or rock - it isn’t fast enough. Use open strings, particularly when effecting position shifts.

There’s a lot of talk about strummers having to have some sort of mythical predictive powers. This is nonsense of course. It’s simply about knowing the music.

However, I cannot stress enough that it should be a two way conversation. The tune player should be listening and responding to the strummer in just the same way we are advocating that the strummer be listening and responding to the tune player.

We know that terrible music happens when the tune player has no option but to try to blank out the strums of the unsympathetic. But the music is just as terrible when an arrogant tune player blanks out someone who, under better circumstances, is an excellent strummer.

Re: Looking for strumming suggestions

Agree that someone coming from bluegrass guitar has much to learn, but I think that a GOOD bluegrass guitar player is equipped with most of the skills required to back up Irish reels. Of course Irish tunes are much more unpredictable, and newbie guitarists often find themselves lost harmonically.
But speaking to the issue of STRUMMING PATTERNS (as opposed to chord patterns), what will be new and challenging are jigs and especially slip jigs.
What sounds worst to me is guitarists strumming only twice or four times per bar in a double jig (that is, a jig in 6/8 time which has six eighth-notes per bar). To me the music requires strumming five times per bar (strumming on the 1, 3, 4, 5, and 6). There are various ways to do this:
#1: Down-down Up-down-up
This is what I see most guitarists doing. But a friend who is a good fiddler, tenor banjo player, and guitarist always uses:
#2: Down-down Down-up-down
which has much more rythm and drive to it. It’s much more difficult to do well and is much more strenuous. One other advantage this strum has is when you play slip jigs. Using #1 above puts the downbeat on an upstroke every other bar when playing slip jigs (that is, jigs in 9/8 time):
#1: Down-down Up-down-up Down-up-down /
Up-up Down-up-down Up-down-up
Whereas his strum goes:
#2: Down-down Down-up-down Down-up-down
for every bar.

A few days late but I just wanted to respond to Iris(Nevins).

My comments were a little “tongue in cheek”. Of course, I think the guitar is a real instrument and I’m not deriding those who play it or even *good* percussionists. It is very hard to do this very well and does require a lot of work.

However, as I’ve commented, I don’t necessarily think that knowing the tune is the same as learning every note. At least, it shouldn’t be necessary to be able to actually play the melody on the guitar or another instrument. It ought to be “in your head” though and, as you suggested, you could even be able to hum or “sing it”.
Of course, to work out really good arrangements you could learn the melody “note for note” before working out a backing. This would be particularly useful for performances and recordings.
However, I don’t see that it’s vital to dissect very individual tune before “backing it” in a session. It would be an impossible task as there’s so many. Being familiar with a tune and knowing its structure is, of course, a different matter and I certainly wouldn’t advocate people just “playing blind” or guessing a chord sequence as often seems to happen.
As Michael says, you can only really predict or guess the patterns with the really simple tunes although I’d disagree that such tunes were necessarily “crap”.
