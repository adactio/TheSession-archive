periods of discouragement

I’m sitting here listening to ‘A Tune for Frankie’ off the Blackwater CD… it’s just so sad… I miss him, though I never met him….

Anyone out there have lulls? Stories about how sorrow (or some other Strong Emotion) has either disrupted and/or enhanced your playing? & how do you bide your time until the next upswing?

Re: periods of discouragement

“Lulls?” Youbetcha, except mine are so prolonged that they stretch into plateaus and then into great plains and whole horizonless continents of lassitude and musical ennui….

I allow myself time off from the instrument (not necessarily the music) when my playing stagnates. Sometimes it just sounds fresher when I come back. More often, some strong emotion drives me back to pick up the fiddle and crank out some rousing reels or weep through an air or slow somber jig. Though I don’t typically enjoy the results of playing when I’m angry….

When my 86 yearold Dad was clearly in his final decline and I was taking care of him, I remember thinking that I was just too tired and depressed to play. But I had my fiddle with me and somehow it ended up coming out of its case late one night. I played for a couple of hours, about 2 percent of my brain conscious that I was playing much better than usual despite two weeks away and being so far down in the dumps.

In my experience, great sorrow and joy bring many emotions to the surface, and you can clearly hear it in the music. But for me even then the music is still its own experience--not an expression of some other experience or emotion, but an even and upwelling all of its own.

Re: periods of discouragement

Helen, it seems I swing through that same tug of war between the mundane and the sublime several times every session. For my own personal reasons, certain tunes are forever linked to peaks or valleys in my life, and they pull the emotions right up to the surface. And then we go into tunes that don’t have any baggage yet, and I’m just sailing along, wondering why my eyes are wet, or trying to decide if I can make it to the next lull before visiting the loo…. Overall, it seems to be a cathartic experience--life almost always looks good after a session.

I wonder if part of what makes Frankie Kennedy’s loss seem so personal to so many is that Mairead and the lads kept going, grieving and playing joyfully nonetheless, bringing his inspiration to thousands of people, to us, all in a way that makes you feel like your sitting in their living room. Like we all participated in an extended, really healthy wake for the man.

Wow, great responses.

Helen, I really get what you’re saying about stepping into a different mindset whilst performing. That’s something I definitely need to work on. As a classical player during gigs, I would use my sheet music as a crutch, but also as a panacea & safety net to nerves and/or emotion (esp during weddings, funerals, church gigs etc), something to focus on, perhaps even notes written to myself to help ensure a good performance. But a session is really different, you have so many things going on & you need to find that headspace to be able to retrieve what you need when you need it. When I started to fill in for my aunt in our old band, she was also a classical flute player who was morphing over to Irish, I was always amazed she didn’t use any sheet music during gigs, & questioned her relentlessly, mostly in disbelief. She used to say it was like a file drawer in her brain that she sort of unlocked, & now I know exactly what she means, but it definitely takes more skill, or something, to navigate this in session setting. Well at least for me. So that’s a big thing I’ll work on in Gaelic Roots, finding that headspace to keep it all together. Mighty.

As for the Altan thing, thanks Domino for the great recommendation, I will look to get it. Believe it or not, even though I’ve known empirically about Frankie for a long time, I only had the early CDs, so I just got Blackwater in the mail (ebay purchase *grin*) & I made the mistake of putting it on in the newborn nursery at work, & I just couldn’t believe the emptiness without the flute. Finally, as for periods of discouragement, I don’t think I’m in a bonafide one per se, just a lull between craic, a little withdrawal before heading into Gaelic Roots. As many of us I think are perfectionists, it’s sometimes hard to face the reality of your playing, when so many months ago, I was hoping to be a better point in my playing, but at least I’m getting more comfortable with my personal learning curve. Last night I learned Castle Kelly, the setting off the Murphy Roche CD, & half of Cup of Tea, but obviously neither tune will be really ready for Boston. But oh well. Re: periods of discouragement

It’s kind of like having a kid, Mark. You can never actually afford to one, but you always somehow manage to get along anyway, so no use waiting for the right time, just go do what makes you happy. If you want all the creature comforts and don’t like stressing about the rent, get a day job that doesn’t interfere with the playing. Get rid of all your debt so you can go do what you want to when you want to. Figure out the base level of income you have to have to be happy with your life outside of playing. And either find a significant other who is a player too, or who is very VERY understanding.

I was an actress for five years, and a theatre costumer for quite some time before that. As a result, I have probably had more, different jobs than most people have. But I was always an actress. One of my friends once recalled to me a statement I made at a party (I don’t remember saying it), that someone had remarked how many jobs I’d had, and apparently I said, “No, not really, I was just always an actress.”

Eventually I gave up the theatre thing, mainly because I didn’t need to do it to be happy, and there were so many other people who did. (Or who thought they did.) It didn’t seem right to take jobs from people who honestly wanted to do that more than anything else when I was mainly doing it because I simply liked it even though it was neither a vocation or avocation.

Now I’m entirely lucky and blessed to have a husband who is willing to get by with making less money and still be able to teach stepdancing and make stepdancing costumes and have a part-time job at a photography studio (that’s a new one!), AND play out a lot. It’s awfully generous of him.

Anyway, do what you need to do. The rest follows, generally. :)
