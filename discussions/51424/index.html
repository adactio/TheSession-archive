Value of recording?

Modesty would usually prevent me from making such a comment but for the purpose of this discussion it is important for me to say that I would be generally seen as a strong player.

That said, I am always open to comments and feedback and I am always aware of the need to improve. So I went for a lesson with a very well known Great Fiddle Player. She gave me a number of useful pointers, one of which was to record myself playing, and then use this as a tool to analyse strengths and weaknesses.

At first I was wary. I know of the reliable research for all musicians that you only hear a percentage of what you play. You cannot concentrate fully on listening as so much of your concentration is taken up with actual playing. I am also aware of a number of fiddle players particualrly, in traditional and classical circles who refuse to listen to themselves. I have heard it said that when you hear yourself playing it can have the same off putting quality as hearing a recording or your own voice.

So I was cautious. But thinking I had nothing to lose I did record myself and, you know, I have to say, in all honesty, the sound was awful. Tone was poor, intonation was poor and there was an awful ‘wooden’ quality to the playing. I was really not prepared for this. As I say the usual feedback I get is positive. This recording made me sound like an unmusical chimpanzee with broken fingers. The first time I recorded myself I was genuinely depressed. I tried again at the weekend and it was perhaps a little better but not much.

As someone who has no understanding of recording technology I assume this might be a factor. I am recording myself on my phone and playing it through stereo speakers. But even so. Ouch!

Any thoughts?

I also record myself to help improve.

Listening back can be brutal, but cut yourself some slack – of course it’s going to be a surprise if you never usually hear it like that. Red light syndrome can affect your playing, but it disappears once recording becomes routine.

Try switching the recorder on and playing the same tune half a dozen times through. Record it on an app that shows a waveform, so on playback it’s easy to delete silences / false starts and locate sections you wanted to listen to. If you’re working in something new, try cutting and pasting the best take into a new file and compare it with a recording made a few weeks later.

↳ Posted by WhistlingFionn on November 4th, 2026.

Re: Value of recording?

I’ve recorded myself, a lot. I’ve also recorded a lot of other people. For myself I spend a lot of time looking for interesting tunes in old collections. When I find something I want to learn I find the quickest way is to record myself playing it from the dots, then learn it by ear from that recording rather than just memorising it from the dots as a classical player might.

With other people, these are often people who are ready to make their first CD. They almost always have the same reaction of horror that you had when they hear the first recording played back. There are two factors at play: one is the way your brain applies filters and cleans things up, so what you sing or play really does sound better to you than to others. You can’t do much about that other than learn from it. The second factor is ‘microphone shyness’ (I prefer ‘microphone awareness’): when you first start recording yourself just being aware that you are recording will increase the nerves and make your playing worse than normal. One way to get round that is to not keep starting and stopping the recorder, reminding yourself that it is there. Just start it running at the start of your practice then try to forget about it and leave it running for the whole session. When you come to listen back afterwards, if you use a DAW or editor like Audacity you can easily see where the tunes are and go straight to them without having to listen through all the guff in between.

Re: Value of recording?

I find great value in recording myself - precisely for the reasons you describe. Yes, it can be very disheartening hearing yourself back. But what you are hearing is the truth. That is what you sound like. It’s tough love!

(OK, we can console ourselves by remembering that we are not playing in a fantastic acoustic space, and we are also inhibited by nerves and ‘red light fever’, the self-consciousness of knowing that we are recording ourselves which severely effects our playing)

Recording yourself is brilliant for educating yourself on deficiencies of tone and intonation. The more you do it, the better a musician you will become. I do it for both fiddle playing and singing. I will never cease to be horribly unpleasantly surprised about how off my singing frequently is, and how my violin tone and pitch is scrappy.

But it’s made up for by the occasional time you listen back and think: “I’m getting a lot better” and even sometimes “Actually, that didn’t sound too bad…”

I think it’s a particularly bitter pill for us fiddlers to swallow. I also dabble in guitar, banjo and concertina. And it’s a hell of a lot easier to sound acceptable on those instruments than on the fiddle. As long as you hit the right frets or buttons on those instruments you’ll never sound absolutely godawful. Whereas the violin is spectacularly unforgiving of even the smallest discrepancies of tuning, and nuances of tone. Hence the value of recording yourself…

Re: Value of recording?

I definitely don’t like listening to raw recordings of myself, but I do it anyway, because the benefits outweigh the discomfort! There are a few factors at play here. First off, just like listening to a recording of your own voice, the sound is different than what you’re used to. It’s not just that we have a different mental picture of what was just played, but the physical sound is different, especially if you’re playing a fiddle right next to your ear, or you’re playing a flute, and feeling the vibrations as much as hearing them… A good recording setup can help a bit with this, and then adding a little bit of reverb can instantly make a raw recording sound more natural.

People have also brought up “red light syndrome” -- the fact that your awareness of being recorded can negatively affect your playing. But, as mentioned, the more you record, the less impactful it becomes. I suffer from red light syndrome, and it bothers me that I still have it after nearly 30 years of playing. But over the years, I have developed some ways to combat it…

One thing that makes a difference for me in listening to my recordings is to put them aside for a while before listening to them, which removes some of the emotional connection (either good or bad) that you have with the recording. If you have red light syndrome, and you immediately analyze a recording after making it, I find that you re-live the anxiety that you had when recording it, and start to anticipate the places where you made mistakes or the playing got rough. If you give the recording some time, like a week or two (or longer), you’re not as connected to the act of recording it, and it’s easier to be more analytical about what you hear.

I am also considered a strong player, but during the course of recording my two albums, I have been fairly well convinced that I am a horrible player. A lot of that comes from the editing and mixing processes, where you’re digging deep into the details of your playing. I find that process very painful. But it has also helped me realize that a lot of the style that I bring to the music makes the most sense when it is in an ensemble setting, and also points out to me just how much more fun I have playing with other people over solo playing.

Re: Value of recording?

I can offer a few observations you might find enlightening:

1. I get a much better perspective when someone else is recording me than when I record myself.

2. When I listen to older recordings, I have the advantage of having forgotten what I was thinking at the time, so I am far less judgmental when I come back to those recordings.

3. When I listen to formal recordings I have made dating back as far as the early 70s, I can listen to the overall recording as if it was not me playing. It’s a variation of my second point, with the added advantage of being in a more formal setting with better recording dynamics.

“Improvement” is a very subjective term. There are technical and artistic aspects that can always be improved. Technical improvemt encompasses both what you need to do and what you think you should do. Your perspective on the latter is not dependable. Some are simply never satisfied. I think that’s a great pity.

My suggestion is to take your impression of your recordings with a hefty grain of salt. If you like what you’re doing at the time you do it, it’s probably good. If you doubt that, it’s likely because you need to work more on your internal sense of yourself and your abilities than anything you can learn from a recording.

Re: Value of recording?

I’ve done a lot of recording over the years, from tape and cassette recordings in the 1970s and 80s to phone mic recordings and Logic laptop recordings in the last few years, interspersed with recordings in professional studios.

What recordings do (apart from acting as a record, obviously) is highlight strengths and, especially, weaknesses. In some ways, that’s the easy part. The hard part is addressing the perceived weaknesses (whilst also remembering that perfection is not the ultimate goal).

I’ve certainly learnt a lot from self recordings (and the actual recording process) over the years. When idly playing through a tune I could think to myself, “Not bad - only two mistakes” when a recording would have revealed perhaps a dozen.

But the real place where recordings help is in phrasing, timing and communication. Too many beginners/improvers just think about the notes and not how they work together and tell a story. Imagine Martin Luther King’s “I have a dream” speech read by a six-year-old in a monotone voice. It wouldn’t have quite the same emotional impact.

Session mates often ask me to replay a tune so they can record and learn it. I never give it much thought in the moment—it’s not my playing they’re after but the tune itself. I’m happy to oblige.

What catches my ear is the difference between those recordings and ones I’ve done for self-evaluation. More than once, someone has played back a tune I recorded for them months or years ago, and I’ve said in all earnestness, “Oh that’s lovely, who’s the fiddler?” But the self-made clips always sound horrid, like strangling cats, no matter how much time has passed since I made them. Beyond simple self-consciousness, I cannot fathom why the difference.

↳ Posted by Arbo R. Doughty on November 4th, 2026.

Re: Value of recording?

It’s interesting to consider that many brilliant players of the past did not have ready access to recording equipment, and yet somehow managed to hone their sound and technique without routinely listening to themselves other than in real time as they played. I’m thinking the likes of Michael Coleman, Paddy Killoran, James Morrison, John Doherty, Willie Clancy, Johnny Doran, Junior Crehan, Bobby Casey, Paddy Canny, Micho Russell, and so on. I’ve often wondered what they thought when their records were released and they heard themselves that way.

A good mentor can provide helpful feedback as you’re learning, but at some point we all have to suss out how to hear ourselves in real time. Advice that helped me early on was to play in a small, low-reverb room. The idea was to hear every scrape and scratch, every hiccup in the timing, every “off” pitch. Without the external fullness of reverb. That was brutal at first, as ugly as recording oneself. But I could hear every flaw loud and clear, as well as what worked to fix them. It also taught me to manage my volume, which greatly helped with bow control.

Like yourself, I took lessons from a well-known “name” fiddler, and that was almost his first piece of advice: “record yourself a lot and listen to it”.

I think it was great advice. Personally, I feel like it makes practice more fun and addictive. It gives you an objective, measurable goal for each practice session. When you’re practicing without ever recording yourself… it sometimes starts to feel a bit like you’re playing a difficult arcade game without ever being able to see the score, and without knowing whether you’ve beat your personal best.

It does help to get a halfway decent mike/pre-amp and to experiment a bit with microphone placement-- no sense in making yourself sound worse than you have to. I draw the line at adding reverb/EQ and other frippery, though.
