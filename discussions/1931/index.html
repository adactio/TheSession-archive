Re: horrid tunes?

We had this discussion here a while ago, and it seems that people fell into three camps: those who hate certain tunes, those who like almost every tune (and for whom “hate” is too strong a word to apply to a piece of music), and some who try not to think about it.

At one point, someone said something to the effect of “There are no bad tunes, just poorly played attempts.” Maybe that’s reaching a bit, but I’d generally agree that a good player in a generous mood can transform plain or trite tunes into honest to god music. And, as with most things, one person’s ex-spouse is another’s true love….

Personally, there are darn few tunes I don’t like. I enjoy playing music, and it’s *all* music. Now, I don’t go out of my way to play Devil’s Dream, for example (or Atholl Highlanders), but even after 20 years I genuinely enjoy the Kesh Jig and Drowsy Maggie and all the other old war horses. And I know how to have fun playing even those tunes that for whatever reason haven’t yet weasled their way into my heart.

All depends on what ‘horrid’ means…
(a. a pain in the neck to play on given instrument.
(b. inducing a trip to the bar, bathroom, home etc

Paddy Ryan’s Dream is ‘horrid’ (a) although it’s one of favorites. Playing the Pinch of Snuff in 4 different keys with a smug look on your face is ‘horrid’(b) In fact playing the same tune in different keys in a set is just ‘horrid’(b) IMO. If your one of those people who plays the Foxhunter’s Reel in G than in A as a set - you need to cut that out.

If you get tired of those bread & butter fav’s, try them in C or F. You get a fresh approach to them. You may not realise that your just going through the mechanical motions till you go & learn it in a different key. This applies to those first couple of hundred tunes that you’ve learned, lets face it after the 8,000th time you’ve played the Kesh Jig you could be doing long division in your head while yuor playing it - but put it in F or Bb & your actually thinking about the melody & ornaments, rhythmic stuff etc.

Not for sessions, but a good exercise for when your at home.

Re: horrid tunes?

Locally, our players recognize that some tunes speak more to some people than to others, so we expect certain tunes to come out of certain players in the course of an evening. Most folks join in, even if it’s not their personal favorite, because that’s part of what makes a session fun--helping the other muso’s enjoy their night out.

I’ve also seen the phenomenon of a good tune spreading from two or three core players to the whole session circle, and what was once a break from the wall-of-sound brawl of a loud session becomes just another simultaneous belch from 16 instruments. Tha can be especially disturbing on an air or other slow tune, where it’s unlikely all 16 players will be on the same groove. At one point in the life of our sesh, this happened to Inisheer. Sometimes I sit out those tunes, not because I dislike them, but to give the up and coming players a chance to hear themselves in a smaller ensemble and make their own magic.

I’ve always thought it unrealistic to say that it is only down to the way a tune is performed. There’s good art and bad art. Tune composition is art, so Irish tunes are no exception to that - there are well-written tunes and badly-written tunes. The well-written ones are always balanced in form and aren’t too repetitive or unimaginative. Having said that, I do think that a lot of the tunes people tend to dislike are hated because of bad associations or because they’ve been overplayed. There are quite a few tunes which I personally don’t like, but I’m lucky because I never have to hear them or play them - they never get played at the sessions I go to. You’d never hear the Irish Washerwoman or the Kesh or anything like that, and if you tried to play them you’d most likely be asked to stop. Even some of the session standards like Drowsy Maggie don’t get played anymore.

Re: horrid tunes?

Tunes that repeat parts ad nauseum would be low on my play list. Seems like there’s a glut of hornpipes where the B part is just four new bars with the A part ending tacked on again, and that gets old. They’re hard to play more than once through because you feel like you’ve already played them twice, heh.

And there’s a difference between a tune with a simple melody and a simple-minded melody. I’ve never cared much for Boys of Bluehill because it does that repeat thing already mentioned above, and the phrases are mostly straight scale runs. In contrast, Kitty’s Wedding has a similar form, and follows a fairly simple melody line, but the intervals between notes are more interesting, and so the phrases are more interesting.

That said, if someone else starts up Boys of Bluehill or some other less than stellar tune, I usually join in because I can still have fun with it. Kinda like fingerpainting--even if it produces “bad art,” the sheer joy of mucking about with your hands and watching the colors bounce off each other is worth the mess.

By horrid I didn’t mean tunes that are hellish to play on your instrument. You probably like them and get frustrated with the technical problems - I do often enough.
I was trying to get at some Hate Level Formula apart from simple over-repetition, so far comprising key, type of tune, structure of parts, intervals/melodic movement. So a hornpipe in D major with two simple parts and a rather foreseeable melodic line has a good chance of being hated. Simpleness alone does not determine the HLF, see Kittys Rambles which is considered easy but acceptable.
Now on the other hand something (listening habits?/ tradition?/ musicality?) probably points the more experienced players towards the more valuable, interisting, unusual tune simply because tunes with a high hate level factor get overused.
So my point is: without silly, over-used, flogged-to-death tunes we might not get the good ones.

Re: horrid tunes?

I’m fascinated by what makes me like or dislike tunes, and I’ve thought about it quite a bit actually. Tunes with high HLF for me all have one thing in common (and before anyone misunderstands me, I have to emphasize that this applies to me personally). They all have a totally inflexible harmonic structure. Whether a tune is backed or not, it has a harmonic structure because the notes outline scales and arpeggios. When a tune is played it harmonises itself in my head even if I’m not consciously thinking about it. With most tunes there are lots of different possibilities with potential counter melodies or chords. Whether or not those chords are actually played or not is neither here nor there - I love unbacked tunes also.

Take 2 simple reels as an example - say the Concertina Reel and the Primrose Lass. I love the Primrose Lass because it’s nice and simple, but its question-answer phrases are beautifully balanced, and that run at the end rounds off the whole tune and causes the ear to anticipate a return to the beginning, or the start of another tune, or the end of the set. Also, despite its simplicity, there are heaps of possibilities for chords, and you could even vary it drastically each time through with a few minor substitutions or whatever. The Concertina Reel, on the other hand, is also very simple, but I dislike it because the melody outlines chords very rigidly, giving a would-be backer less to go on, and making it difficult to find inspiration from the notes themselves. The B-part follows musical ideas that are too similar to the A-part both melodically and rhythmically, and that makes the tune as a whole sound to repetitive to my ear. Because both parts are a bit similar, the ear expects any accompaniment to be similar as well, so that the whole thing just drones on with D and A major chords. If you try and spice it up with some minor chords, it just ends up sounding as though you’re trying too hard to do something interesting when the bare basics just aren’t there.

Another thing that makes me dislike tunes is when the melody line isn’t “smoothed out”, and you get notes that clumsily repeat themselves, or accidentals that just don’t belong. This can often be solved quite easily though - you can change the notes so that your setting works for your own ear. The standard session setting of the Longford Collector was always one of those awkward-sounding tunes for me, but I worked out my own setting and “smoothed it out” so that it sounds nice to my ear. Now I enjoy playing it, but when other people play it in a session I find that I want to put some chords to it otherwise I find it a bit uninspiring.
