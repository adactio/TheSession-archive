Re: Does the Comhaltas preserve or fossilize?

The same concern can be voiced about most any organization that promotes or supports folk and traditional music or dance, e.g., the Country Dance and Song Society, English Folk Dance and Song Society, the Morris Ring et al.
Generally (emphasis on “generally”) speaking, their task is to present the music or dance form -- e.g. through concerts, festivals, other events -- in a way that adheres to certain standards and guidelines the organization in question believes to be the most authentic representation of the tradition around which it is based. Now, obviously, there are any number of arguments as to how “authentic” a particular representation might be, but the point is that the organization -- through whatever process -- operates with a consensus on that depiction. By doing so, it establishes a benchmark, or a point of reference, for everyone who indulges in the music or dance tradition.
Certainly, there can be, and are, differences of opinions within the organization as to how valid their representation is. And if these objections are strong enough, those who voice them may decide to start their own organization -- in doing so, they must necessarily establish their own set of standards, or benchmark.
If this happens often enough, well, you’ll have a whole spectrum of interpretations of the tradition to ponder. You might decide to throw your lot with one, and only one, organization; or you might feel that participating in, or using the resources of, more than one gives you a richer appreciation of the tradition.

As a morris dancer who has been on mainly mixed-gender teams, I don’t particularly hold truck with the morris dance organizations that only recognize single-gender teams (or perhaps don’t even think women should dance morris). But I do appreciate the tremendous research and scholarship some of them have undertaken, which has been invaluable for documenting and preserving the different styles of morris dance.
Similarly, there are several reasons why I really could not see myself as a member of Comhaltas. But I appreciate the work the organization does, in organizing numerous events (quite a few of which I’ve attended) that keep the music and dance in the public eye and offers a (key word “a”) definitive representation of the tradition. I also know many people affiliated with the CCE who are decidedly _not_ fossilized, who enjoy the innovations which have come along in recent decades; for them, CCE offers a clear, identifiable mode of experiencing the tradition -- but it’s by no means the _only_ mode.

Re: Does the Comhaltas preserve or fossilize?

I don’t know if it’s worth chiming in here - others have already made some good points about the bigger picture of Comhaltas over the years. As a Comhaltas staffer, though, I’m inclined to share a couple of thoughts.

First, there’s certainly no sinister censorship when it comes to instruments like the bouzouki or mandolin. I edit and post the daily video clips based on what I have tapes of, and the reality of it is that there are more people out there playing flutes and fiddles than there are mandolin solos. It would be unrealistic for me to only post clips when I had a completely “balanced” selection of instruments, and the reality is that I see mandolins and bouzoukis playing as part of larger groups more often than solo.

Might have to do with instrument volume (quiet vs. loud), the long-time perception of bouzoukis as “backing instruments” (though I’d dispute that claim), etc. As you’ve seen, I don’t have as many group or session clips up there, mostly because it’s much harder to control the ambient noise, and I really do want our video clips to sound crisp, clean and suitable for learning. Also, as far as audience demand, I’m constantly asked for more fiddle and box players, not so much on the bouzouki. (I’m studying bouzouki myself at the moment, actually, so it’s not like I’m opposed to the instrument!)

How’s this: If anyone has some clear bouzouki or mandolin videos that you want to send me, I’ll post ’em! No censorship, I promise. I mostly work from MiniDV tapes at full PAL resolution and SP speed, 48khz audio, which (for ComhaltasLive) are dubbed down from DigiBeta D-9. Email me at brendan@comhaltas.ie and we’ll work something out. My plan for the website from the beginning of the redesign was to encourage more submission from musicians.

I do try and tag the videos, so you could certainly look at http://comhaltas.ie/music/tag/Banjo (for any instrument name) if you’re looking for a particular instrument.

Second, as far as fossilization, I’ve done some interesting reading lately about the nature of revivalist movements in general that might shed some light on the conservative perception. But that’s pretty academic -- the reality is that every day, around the world, there are lots of people who enjoy studying and playing Irish traditional music, and Comhaltas’ primary job is to hook those people up with each other, not to make some dramatic decree about what constitutes “real” Irish music. Most of our students want to study the fiddle, flute or button accordion, though more and more teenagers are getting into the mandolin, mandola or bouzouki. Branches organise music classes and lessons based on demand, simple as that. Nothing sinister!

Third, the archive. Yeah, I know that it’s been a long-time project, but I encourage you to compare our collection to that of, say, the Irish Traditional Music Archive in Merrion Square. Despite the ITMA having 5 or 6 full-time archive staff and massive state support as far as physical plant and ongoing revenue funding, I think we stack up pretty well. (The ITMA folks, and the collection, are absolutely first-class - I’m not trying to imply any different!)

No, we don’t have our archive collection online (much as I’d love to do exactly that.) Neither does anyone else. Anyone who can work out the rights and permissions required to publish 14,000 tracks from thousands of musicians free on the Internet is welcome to have my job, because it’s something that I struggle with! And last time I was at the ITMA (a couple of months ago), they were at the same point we’re at: ready to publish the *index* online as soon as they’re done with the extremely painstaking data-checking involved with any collection that spans a number of years.

But if you come on down to Monkstown, you’re free to browse through our archive index, by performer, instrument, tune name/type or county, and we’ll willingly give you access to a CD with the tracks you’re looking for. Come on down - we always learn a lot from the students and researchers who stop by. And unlike the ITMA which closes at 5.00pm, at the Cultúrlann there’s a good chance of a seisiún downstairs in the bar later on.

That said, I am working on an internet project involving the Comhaltas archive that I think everyone here will find pretty cool. Soon as I get my dissertation finished, I promise. :-)

Fourth, the re-issues. That’s actually more work than you’d think … it’s just a question of staff resources. Re-issues in this kind of niche market aren’t likely to ever make money, but we’d love to publish ‘em anyway once we can. I can say that there are some recordings in the pipeline, actually. We actually have just a tiny handful of people to deal with a pretty big non-profit organisation, and our first priority is to support our members, branches, county and regional boards and provincial councils, administer the traditional music exams, run the teachers’ certification course, work on the festivals -- and all the rest of it.

And as always, it’s the volunteers on the ground who are really the heart and soul of the whole thing. Exactly as it should be. Nothing at Comhaltas would work without the incredible dedication of so many wonderful people.

Re: Does the Comhaltas preserve or fossilize?

I’d add my own thanks to kilfarboy for bringing up important issues -- I’m also glad to see that trad itself can provoke such high feelings and debate. To me, that proves that whatever our distractions, there’s a core of excitement here about what Irish culture has to offer. I’m glad he’s here lending his voice.

Getting back to the original question about “preservation” vs. “fossilisation”: speaking for myself, it seems a false dichotomy. On the original question, I’m quite certain that there’s no active bias against particular instruments, on the website or within the organisation. Comhaltas has changed over the years to include more categories of competition, for example. And anyone who’s seen the Grúpai Cheoil over the last few years certainly can’t think that there’s any “fossilisation” within the ranks of players and teachers. Fabulous stuff.

And if you go to any local session, including the ones associated with Comhaltas branches, you’ll see pretty much every instrument imaginable represented there. I’ve seen the mandolas, mandolins, 4- and 5-string banjos, bouzoukis (bouzoukii?), melodeons, guitars, piano boxes and even spoons along with the pipes, fiddles, flutes and button accordions. Don’t think I’ve seen any saxophones playing in a regular session, but that probably just means I don’t get out enough, and there’s plenty of historical justification for sax as a historical trad instrument. If there’s a bias, it must be a pretty subtle one, or a pretty ineffective one, because all of these instruments seem to be blossoming within the tradition.

But the question is really much larger than that. Yes, organisations and movements (and “institutions”, though I hate that word) are just groups of people, and people who devote a lot of energy to holding on to something precious usually do so because they perceive a threat to that precious thing from “the outside.” So off the bat, the “preservationists”, for the most noble of motives, have created a hard boundary between “us” and “not-us”, which can cause problems later on. To me the trick is to create a stable, peaceful and democratic organisation in which to mutually explore the delight of that precious thing, a movement that can respond to change. Even if that response will always, always feel too slow.

Change comes too slow in every other domain, so I’m not surprised that it takes a while among a body of people who see value in a past tradition, no matter how ancient or how recent. In the process, our sense of time can get a bit distorted -- the concept of the “session” isn’t really as old as we usually assume, for example -- but on the whole musicians are pretty good at regulating the pace of innovation. We know what feels ‘real’ when we hear it. And bear in mind that the fundamental energy and goal of Comhaltas has less to do with “defining” trad -- by definition a tradition can’t be “defined”, just analysed and explored! The job of Comhaltas is in the area of “transmission” - trying to make sure that bits aren’t lost between the generations. With the family and the village not passing on the music in quite the same way, in many places, there’s plenty of work to do in creating and expanding and sustaining an alternate model.

And the model seems to work, for a lot of people, a lot of the time. Things that Comhaltas has promoted - instruments like kilfarboy’s uilleann pipes for example, have enjoyed a resurgence in the last several decades. I’m not someone who thinks we can entirely “claim credit” for that particular revival (and it would be stupid to overlook groups like Na Píobairí Uilleann within that dynamic), but the fact is that there are so many teachers and pipe makers and students around now, and there didn’t used to be. And that’s a good thing. If only some of those pipers received some of their training or experienced some of their sublime listening moments at a Comhaltas class or event or festival, then we’re succeeding in that aspect of preservation.

Are there problems and stupidities in the system? Sure. If any of you work in the non-profit world, you probably have a sense of the types of pressures and politics that come with the territory. What I’ve seen at Comhaltas are the exact same types of issues that I’ve seen at other non-profits that I’ve been involved with. But a lot of really cool people are drawn into the system, as well. You have to ask yourself: If the negative politics are so terrible, why would there still be so many interesting and creative people involved? It’s pretty easy to find trad these days; with the Internet and some commitment and maybe a local session, you can get as good as you want to get, and get tons of joy from ITM completely away from the Comhaltas orbit. And we’ll be delighted for you - a self-sustaining culture of continued amateur examination of Irish folk culture is probably more than we would have dreamed of in 1951 when Comhaltas was founded. But interesting people attract other interesting people, and I see a lot of ’em around here, too.

Looking around the landscape of players, young and old, amateur, professional and in-between, I don’t see much danger of fossilisation. How could Comhaltas stem such a massive tide of energy and innovation, even if we wanted to?
