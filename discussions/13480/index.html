↳ Posted by Saint on April 23rd, 2007.

Re: Loud, louder, loudest.

Bodhran is a backing instrument. It works best to my ears in a session when it underlies the melody instruments.

There are exceptions to this: when playing for dancers in a setting where the melody instruments do not carry well enough, and a bodhran can give the dancers the beat.

In a session, I like it best when all melody instruments aim to blend, rather than any one overpowering the others. A rule of thumb I suggest to newbies to sessions is that if they can’t hear everyone else over themselves, then they’re playing way too loud. Banjos have to be careful becuase their sound projects out, well down and away from the player’s own ears. So it’s easy to inadvertently overpower the people across from you.

↳ Posted by oldstrings on April 24th, 2007.

Go low

I tend to feel that the focus of the percussionist is to provide the best time in a group not the most volume. However there is no reason why ITM cannot be dynamic music. As in many styles of music I like percussion to accent (ie increase volume) the cadences of a tune (usually on bars 4,8,12, 16 etc.) And fills to proceed these sections. Also, if a drum is very loud at the beginning of a tune there is nowhere to build a climax to. Which shoud ideally be the last reiteration of a tune. Also as a general rule the lower tones don’t usually project as well as the higher pitched ones. So a good drummer should try to emphasize the low tones, if for to other reason then the pitch areas will contrast and not cover up the melodic pitch range which tends to be in the alto-soprano octaves. Just some thoughts. Personally I like a well pounded oak table, many times, better than higher pitched percussion!
