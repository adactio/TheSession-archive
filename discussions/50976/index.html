Hi , I ve been playing tenor banjo 35 yrs now , and 50 yrs on strings in general.
My advice would be simple, like any percussion instrument , a serious player will spend a lot of time with a metronome. But focused on hitting the clicks precisely so they vanish . So a reel typicaly has 8x 1/8 notes .
So set the gnome to 8 clicks . At a nice easy pace and hit every click on the head aiming for precision .
The better the gnome, modern ones will allow you to set swing , but at a basic level a straight beat will do the job

. Other instruments have more leeway for inaccuracy , not so loud , not so focused . But look on the banjo as a drum . Take a basic drumming book and work through the exercises . Paradiddles etc . Just substitute up and down for left and right .

This kind of technical practice will pay dividends . Be patient . Boredom is s bad habit . Take control of your picking and your life by regular focused technical practice . Enjoy it . And enjoy the rewards .

Re: Trouble keeping time on reels

I would suggest that you spend a little time looking at your picking. As a long time tenor banjo player, I have found that playing with a fairly strict picking pattern really helps me, and when I first started doing that, it great improved my rhythm, steadiness, and ultimately my ability to play faster without losing any of it.

It’s easy for reels. The picking pattern can simply be down-up down-up (DUDU DUDU), and if you put your down strokes on the down beats (1 and 3), you’ll find that your hand “dances” to the music nicely. That also helps you keep track of where you are in the tune. The natural inclination is to pick the direction that your hand is moving when moving from one string to another. If you’re moving from the E string to the A string, it’s natural to want to do the picking motion with an up stroke, since you’re moving your hand upwards. But my suggestion is to always pick the down beats down, and the up beats up, no matter which direction your hand might be moving between strings.

So to keep this pattern steady, if you play a quarter note, your hand will still move in the opposite direction after the first strike, without hitting a string, so that you’re ready for the next motion in the right direction. And then triplets are also something to get used to. You will pretty much always start a triplet on a down beat, with a down stroke, and when you finish the triplet, your next stroke will be down again. For example: DUDU (dud)DU… DU(dud) DUDU…

I will also say that there are great players who use the “economy of motion” technique instead of a strict picking pattern, but the picking patterns certainly helped me play better, and ultimately, they become ingrained so that you never think of them again. (Jigs are another matter, and I pick my jigs DUD DUD to keep a strict picking pattern… but there are many schools of thought about that one…)

Re: Trouble keeping time on reels

Just to confirm that there are many schools of thought, I would strongly advise you to avoid pattern picking, but this is primarily an aesthetic issue, not a matter of ergonomics. When I’m playing a tune, no matter what instrument I’m playing it on, I’m interested in the phrasing and dynamics, and I find that picking patterns hamstring my playing in both of those regards, so I don’t do use them. If you’re interested in phrasing and dynamics, my main suggestion would be to think about how you can use your fretting hand to cut down the amount of picking you have to do.

Regardless of whether you want to play a more rhythmic or a more fluid style - and again, this is an aesthetic choice, not a moral one - probably the biggest enemy of good banjo playing is tension. You want to relax your picking hand as much as possible, and then about three steps past that. One way to explore this is to grip the pick as you would normally do for playing, and try to identify five different levels of “clutch”, from zero muscle exertion holding the pick in place all the way through to a death grip with thumb and forefinger. Try playing a tune at each of those “clutch levels” and see what’s easiest. You’ll probably find that the death grip is extremely difficult to work with, but it’s also quite informative, since everything that’s hard about playing with a tight grip is something that’s going to get easier when you relax. You might also find that as you relax your right hand, you want to think about your choice of pick. There are lots of strong opinions here, so I’ll just say that you should keep a range of picks of different thickness and stiffness on hand, and as your playing develops, you should occasionally review the different options and see if your preferences are changing.
It’s also worth paying attention to the ergonomics of your playing position. This will vary based on your particular body, so don’t let anyone tell you there’s a right way to hold the banjo, but there’s certainly going to be a right way for _you_ to hold the banjo, and once you find it, things will be a lot easier. The angle of incidence between your pick and the string makes a huge difference, so a lot of your posture is going to be about getting that to be right by default.

All of this holds, I think, regardless of where you end up on the picking pattern issue.

Longtime fiddle and mandolin player here. I also play some banjo. In a past life I played bass in jazz bands big and small.

My advice is this: pay close attention to the ability to sing tunes to yourself. Lilt, scat, sing, whistle, etc. if you can do that, then the issue is probably a problem with your right hand muscle memory.

If your time varies between the low and high strings, you need to practice scales (not tunes) with a metronome slowly until you get even notes and control everywhere.

You can play jigs because the lift is more forgiving and gives your right hand more flexibility and space for recovery. In order to solve this problem, you need to practice scales (not tunes) with a metronome slowly until you get even notes and consistency through time.

My good buddy asked me this exact question last weekend. I told him to practice 7/8 time because it forces us out of our comfort zones. In order to solve this problem, you need to practice single notes (not tunes) with a metronome.

You are likely not dealing with accents and, as others have said, ornamentation properly. Ornamentation comes out of expression and good time. For this, you need to practice tunes slowly from memory with a metronome, and listen back to your practice recordings. You will feel better in a matter of weeks.

Metronome is your buddy.

Re: Trouble keeping time on reels

A tip from my son, who is a professional musician (jazz double bass player, one of those who keep the time!) is to use a metronome set at the tempo at which you can accurately play the part of the tune that you have the most difficulty with - that is, slow it down so that you play all of the tune at the same speed as the most difficult part. Once you have it, wind it up a bit, and then a bit more.

The old adage holds true, that practice doesn’t make perfect, but it does make permanent. If you learn a tune without correct timing it can be difficult to fix it later - not impossible of course, but it’s something else you have to do! I have personally found that playing with a metronome lets me know pretty quickly which sections of a tune I know and don’t know, because if I hesitate over a note or a phrase, I don’t it know it well enough to play confidently in a session. And the metronome spots it immediately.

And as a banjo player I agree with both the Reverend and Jon K. Start learning with patterns, and once you’ve got the pattern and the tune you can then alter it for economy, efficiency and flow. But you need to have a starting point.

Re: Trouble keeping time on reels

I’ll share something that I do when I want to work on getting a steadier sense of beat:

First start by just playing with the metronome on a tune or tunes that are all well under your hands. Do it at a tempo where your economy-of-motion is rock solid and without tension. Now, take out every other beat; cut the bpm marking in half. But don’t cut your playing speed in half. If at first you were hearing every quarter note in a reel, 4 notes, you will now hear the half-note from the metronome.

There is now more time passing between each opportunity to confirm that your playing is in-sync with the metronome, and you spend more time relying on yourself for a steady beat. This will highlight those section of the tune that you like to rush through, rather than let the music breath. Or highlights sections of music where the fingers gets bogged down and the tune looses its drive.

Only if you’re ready, and can play through the tune without any noticeable deviations(and without tension forming at any part of the tune), cut the bpm in half again, so you only hear the first beat of each measure. Then again, and again, ad infinitum

This method can challenge every skill level and has given me plenty to chew on. Every musician benefits from the ability to play with a steady beat without a crutch, but not music does this make. Music has a push and pull to it that can be involuntary. But most tempo variation I hear is because the tune got to an easy part, that flows nice under the fingers. Some sections are easy to play, and without disciplined internal time, it becomes hard to make those phrases musical.

Re: Trouble keeping time on reels

I’ll throw in my 2c on how to develop this.

In very short, my advice is this:
Anytime you are listening to music, practice noticing every part of the body, relaxing it if it’s not relaxed, and then begin moving that part with the music. Start with the most obvious bits like tapping the fingers and then tapping the feet, and gradually incorporate more and more until you can tap both sets of fingers, both feet, and sway your hands and arms and hips and shoulders and everything else, in time with the music and with varied emphasis based on the contour of the music. Of course you can’t always use every part of the body -- don’t be tapping the right foot while driving, but use whatever bits are available.

This practice will get the rhythm in you.

Also, in order to have more time for this, Listen to the music as often and as long as you enjoy it. There are plenty of opportunities - in the car or train or plane, while doing any kind of physical work, even while doing some kinds of thinking work, while walking or running or however you get exercise, perhaps for some people even while falling asleep.

Now I’ll rant a while on how and why for the one or two who might be interested:

I also had a lot more trouble with reels than jigs at first. Reels are more complex than jigs because instead of one main beat per measure that’s always pretty much in the same place, there are two main beats and the emphasis sometimes switches between them.

I think of this as a sort of holistic approach to rhythm. It’s more complex than just being able to hit the notes on the primary beats. The music if played tastefully to my ear involves lots more layers in the rhythm than just the main beats. So ultimately what I want is for my body - every part of it - to be able to move precisely in time with the timing of the sounds I hear or imagine in my head. As a fiddle player I might have thought this only mattered for the fingers of my left hand and for my right hand and arm, but really the whole body is involved, and with what little we understand of how the mind works, reinforcing patterns in one part of the body can strengthen the same patterns in the rest of the body; thus I believe that the practice of tapping the right fingers and swaying the left shoulder and moving my head to the music is helpful even though I don’t use these to play the fiddle.

In practice, at first I couldn’t do all these things at once. I spent time in sessions paying attention to my feet and practicing tapping just the right foot on the 1 beat. Then on reels I began to tap on both main beats (the 1 and 3 I think they’re called). Then eventually I got to where I can tap them with varied emphasis on both beats to match the emphasis of those beats in the music. I actually usually don’t tap my feet so much as lift the heel and thus the knee. This doesn’t make a sound on the floor and so it’s not disruptive to others, but it is just as good for getting the rhythm into the body. It’s also more flexible since it can be done anywhere that the toe has a hold. It’s also a lot easier to do and doesn’t matter what kind of shoes I have. And I can do it faster, so I can actually lift the heel on every note in a reel (i.e. all of the 1,2,3,4) if it’s not played too fast - but I’m finding I can do it even on fast reels in sessions now.

So I would sit and focus on getting my right foot moving with the rhythm. When this got to be consistent, I focused on this with my left. Then I focused on my right, got it going properly, and tried to incorporate the left as well. Then I focused on getting the right to move on both the 1 and 3 and the left to move on just the 1. And so on and so on. I ultimately want everything to be able to move on any bit of the rhythm I want, and with whatever emphasis on a given beat or sub-beat that I want. This is the same thing my hands and arm do after all - they need to move with varied emphasis and varied direction on any of the nuances of the rhythm that I want to express.

The same way with the fingers, and the hands. I hold my fiddle upright on my left thigh and tap my fingers gently on the back of it so they have something definite to be making contact with, and then I can relax the hand and fingers so the tapping becomes lighter and lighter and more subtle but still precise - the same way I want my touching of the strings to be as subtle as possible while still being precise. One benefit of this practice is it gives lots of time to practice incorporating the little finger, and the ring finger, which I found hard to move independently of the others. I don’t want to spend 100 hours sitting with the fiddle doing pink exercises - but I can easily get hundreds of hours of vigorous tapping to the rhythm while doing other things or sitting in sessions.

And the same way with every other part of the body. It helps that I really love the music and have no shame about aspiring to fully embody it even if other people might see.

This practice becomes mostly subconscious of course, I’m not thinking to myself “now I need to tap my foot harder because this beat is more emphasised”

If every bit of my body could move with precision to the subtlest bits of the rhythm at speed, then it would be possible for me to be able to play the music in time at that speed. So it seems likely to me that this practice spread across the many hours of listening that I do when I can’t actually be playing -- and I listen at least 10 times as much as I play -- would lead to rapid improvement my ability to keep the rhythm compared to if I don’t do this.

Another thing to add in is singing/lilting the tune at the same time.

And I would emphasize again the value of intentionally relaxing each part of the body before and constantly during this practice. The same way I practice relaxing everything while I’m playing.

Re: Trouble keeping time on reels

Good discussion.
1- First learn every note you want to play. Always learn notes out of Time, taking your time to be accurate, with calm choreography. How can you play fast when you don’t know what you are going to play? Your body must be as relaxed as possible while learning. Don’t practice tension.

2- There are 2 main aspects to playing: mind and body. When your mind has learned enuf to direct your body to play the correct notes, you may be ready to play in Time.

3- Most importantly: The body feels the Time. The body has many automatic cycles, short and long, so give the job of Timekeeper to your body. The mind is fickle - you can count to 100 quickly. Therefore give your mind the job of keeping track of things, paying attention to what section you are currently playing, prepare for a tricky spot in the tune, direct picking events, etc. IOW use the mind for mental activity, not adding to or confusing it by also giving it the job of regulating pace: feeling the Time, which is the body’s job.

4- Your body has an internal ticker in the abdomen area that will maintain a steady pulse. As with everything, knowing it’s there is halfway to finding it. Always involve your whole body (except for head banging :-) beginning with bending/swinging the knees with each count. When you can feel your internal pulse while standing with feet flat on the floor and knees swinging, then you can graduate to walking with your pulse.

I asked a jogger how he increased speed: “I make my ticker go faster.”

5- Musicmaking is not flailing at the extremities; it is cooking at your center. So tapping a foot or head banging may or may not help. I stopped foot tapping while standing because it tightens the opposite side of my body, which puts my body offbalance, making playing difficult. I always have both feet flat on the floor, swinging the knees in Time (even sitting), creating balanced released flow and musical feel. This generates the energy to motivate dancers, and leaves a residual bounce in the dancers when the music stops.

6- A metronome [a little guy who rides the subway] may be useful, but is never a substitute for your internal ticker and full body Time cycles. Always know first what you will play before challenging yourself to play in Time. The metronome does not generate your Time; you first generate and own your body’s Time pulse, then if necessary, use the machine to perfectly regulate what you already are making well.

Practice is discovery. Happy journey.

“your body has an internal ticker”, no it does not, there is no built-in atomic clock, no built-in GPS receiver (well, yes, there is - your ear listening to a drummer). it is well known that human perception of time is highly variable, changes with mood, heart rate, time of day, etc. there is a reason why cars have speedometers, train drivers are issued pocket watches, bands hire drummers, and musicians buy metronomes.
