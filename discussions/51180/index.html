Michael Coleman recordings …without the backing

Some of the most important and influential recordings ever made of traditional Irish music must be the records of Micheal Coleman made in the early 20th century in New York.

Those records had a big influence on the style and repertoire of fiddlers back in Ireland. Some argue that the Colemanification may have swamped other regional and individual styles. But everyone is in agreement that Michael Coleman’s playing was second-to-none.

Everyone is also in agreement that the piano backing on those recordings is often terrible! A piano player with no knowledge of the music gets hired by the hour to play along to tunes they’re hearing for the first time.

I know that some scholars of Coleman’s recordings say that they can “tune out” the piano accompaniment but I’ve always found it very distracting.

That’s why I was very intrigued to see this new project from the Irish Traditional Music Archive by David Lennon (son of Ben, nephew of Charlie).

Using AI Technology on Michael Coleman’s Recordings:
https://www.itma.ie/blog/using-ai-technology-on-michael-colemans-recordings/

I’m not a fan of generative “AI”, especially when it comes to music, but this is an interesting use of machine learning. The article is light on technical details, which is fine because I’m more interested in what you think of the end results.

On that link you can listen to the original recordings with the piano backing alongside the altered versions with the piano removed.

How do you find it listening to the fiddle playing without the piano?

Does this make it easier for you to listen to the recordings or are you one of those people who’s always been able to tune out the accompaniment?

Let me know what you think!

Re: Michael Coleman recordings …without the backing

For me, it’s not so much tuning out the backer as it is lasering in on the fiddle. Much as in a session, I still hear the backer, but my focus is on the melody being played by the fiddler.

In a session, with 3, 4, or more instruments, being able to direct your aural attention like this without completely cancelling out everything else is a useful skill. You can follow the player who started the set while being mindful of how other players are shaping the pace, pulse, lift, and swing.

It’s not uncommon, for example, when a less experienced player starts a set, for another player to help steady the pulse. And then someone else will gradually, gently give it more swing. If the other people in the circle are listening only to the lead player, the set may never gel—you’ll have five people playing the same tunes but not the same music.

In the case of these Coleman recordings, the backing—despite having very little to do with what Coleman is playing—does serve somewhat as a steadying influence on the timing. Coleman has a steady beat, but in almost everything he recorded, his timing feels hurried, rushing from note to note as though he just wants to get through the tune. He rarely lingers on a note or lets it stretch. His quavers are mostly clipped and he tends to ornament every crotchet and dotted crotchet. The result is a very bouncy, harried style. At best, this creates a sense of drive, but all too often it just sounds rushed. Compare Coleman’s recording of O’Dowd’s with this version. Yes, Fergal, Ian, and Jeremie are playing at a tad slower pace, but it’s their lingering on strong beats that gives it a smoother, more lyrical, feel. (Even if you set the playback speed to 1.25, their timing retains a push and pull that Coleman’s lacks.)

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=311shtEmrNk&list=RD311shtEmrNk&start_radio=1

From a historical context, I understand the reasons for Coleman’s mighty influence on players of his generation (and several following generations). But there are many, many fiddlers I prefer to listen to, including James Morrison and Paddy Killoran, and of course many modern-day fiddlers.

I grew up in Irish trad as a live, aural tradition, playing with and learning directly from the *people* whose playing I admired and aspired to. That process was fraught with complexities, nuances, personalities (and some personality disorders), and the usual tangle of different playing styles, diverse and divergent settings, myriad names for the same tune, myriad tunes for the same name, and all the other lovely swirls in the currents of this music. So it was a rich, challenging, at times overwhelming, and utterly human experience. I wouldn’t trade a nanosecond of any of that for a techy assist or “shortcut,” whether AI or an app to generate tune sets. Progress as a musician doesn’t happen by allowing a machine to provide the skill that we need to learn.

Re: Michael Coleman recordings …without the backing

Just to clarify things a bit, there are various sorts of AI, and the LLMs you’ve engaged with are only one of them. “Generative AI” is the cover term for AI that creates new text or other artifacts, this includes LLMs (which generate text, including music notation, based sampling insanely large corpus of examples) and stable diffusion (used to generate images or sound files). What you’re hearing here is pretty clearly not generative AI, it’s pretty clear that it’s using pattern recognition to identify the piano and selectively remove it, leaving whatever it did not identify as “piano” (hence the lingering traces of backing that you’re hearing).

To generate a new fiddle track based on the old one using stable diffusion would not be impossible, in the same way you can ask an LLM to create an image based on some well-known existing image, and that might be an interesting experiment, but that doesn’t seem to be what’s happened here.

Just to get up on my soapbox for a moment, it’s very important to not use the term “AI” as though it refers to a technology. It does not, it refers to a goal, namely, the goal of reproducing human cognitive processes by a machine. AI is not a thing, it’s an ambition, and the technologies used to approach that ambition are many and varied, and they do not all exhibit the same behaviors or failure modes, and they can’t all be applied to the same tasks. Putting it simply, most AI is not LLMs and LLMs can’t do most of what can be done with AI, and what you know about AI from interacting with LLMs applies only to LLMs.

I’m not saying this as a proponent of AI, I have a lot of problems with where we’re going with this suite of technologies, but I think it’s important to understand what’s going on under the hood, at least in broad strokes, particularly if you’re not a fan.

@mandocello8

Red dwarf’s a good laugh. And the point is valid. Though you might be surprised what you can actually do with modern tech.

If a signal is not completely random, you can take advantage of what’s information is there in a noisy signal to reconstruct what’s been lost. E.g., correlations in a signal. If you hear a long note, and it gets corrupted in the middle, you can fill in the gaps. Sometimes noise is also in a way predictable - high-frequency hisses can be fixed with a low-pass filter.

Here’s one explainer for images:
https://www.e-consystems.com/blog/camera/technology/what-is-image-denoising-what-are-its-methods/

What you get after denoising is not guaranteed to be the original, but it can definitely get you closer than a noisy wax-cylinder recording.

Re: Michael Coleman recordings …without the backing . . . and a tribute to a sort of legend

RIP Rosie “Twinkle-Fingers” Dooley, piano (Port St Lucie, Florida)

Anyone who has ever heard Rosie Dooley’s memorable accompaniment to Irish traditional music will, in all honesty, probably not be too saddened by her decease (mercifully due to natural causes) on October 13th at the age of 87.

While her name may not be well known in this uncaring day and age, music history will surely record the fact that Rosie, otherwise a likeable lady, left a big black ugly greasy indelible mark on traditional music when she founded her “Rosie Dooley School of Piano Accompaniment” in New York in the early 1930’s. From this academy of insensitivity graduated some of the least qualified practitioners of the accompanist’s art ever to “thump them ivories” (to use one of Rosie’s daintier phrases).

One of Rosie’s better-known exercises for her students consisted of pounding away at the piano for an hour or so with boxing gloves on both the student’s hands, to develop “stamina” as she called it (although for reasons not clear, Rosie pronounced it “stam-EYE-na”). Unfortunately this exercise invariably took place at the end of the group lesson, by which time Rosie would have gone sneaking into her office for her bit of “refreshment”, a sojourn from which she rarely emerged coherent. This repeated failure of Rosie’s to instruct her students on the means of removing the boxing gloves appears to have left many of her impressionable young charges believing that the gloves were an integral part of traditional piano accompaniment. The results are, unfortunately, history.

(It may be of interest to note that the building that formerly housed Rosie’s school is now owned by a company that manufactures custom-designed anvils . . . perhaps one of History’s little ironies, or then again perhaps not.)

(By the way, the rumor that the cement piano placed lovingly atop Rosie’s grave had been smashed to bits by a lightning bolt on Michael Coleman’s birthday is apparently true.)
